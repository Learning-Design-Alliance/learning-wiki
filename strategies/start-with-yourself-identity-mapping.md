# Start with Yourself (Identity Mapping)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Start with Yourself (Identity Mapping) asks educators to explicitly map their own identity markers — race, gender, class, language, citizenship status, ability, religion — and examine how these positions shape what they notice, assume, and value in learners. It is typically carried out through structured protocols such as identity maps and constructivist listening dyads, where participants articulate their positions and hear others' without debate or correction. The strategy operationalizes the premise that empathy and culturally responsive teaching begin with the educator's self-knowledge rather than with techniques applied to students.

## Design Implications

Identity mapping converts tacit cultural assumptions into articulable knowledge, a form of [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) applied to the educator rather than the learner. Reflective examination of one's own position supports the metacognitive monitoring that underlies self-regulated professional growth [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Because implicit biases operate outside awareness, structured externalization (mapping, dyad sharing) is more likely to surface them than unstructured reflection alone [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A psychologically safe setting with agreed norms (confidentiality, no debate, right to pass), since identity disclosure carries social risk
- A structured protocol (identity map template, listening dyad format) rather than open-ended "reflect on your identity" prompts
- Time for individual reflection before group sharing, and a trained facilitator who has done the exercise themselves
- A follow-on mechanism that connects insights to practice — e.g., revisiting the map when analyzing a classroom interaction or family communication

#### Constraints
- One-off workshops produce awareness without behavior change; benefits depend on sustained cycles of reflection and application [~M]
- Participants who feel targeted or "called out" by identity categories may disengage or experience the exercise as reductive; voluntary, self-selected framing of markers mitigates but does not eliminate this [-M]
- In low-trust or hierarchically pressured settings (e.g., administrators present), disclosure is performative and the exercise yields little [-W]
- Increased self-awareness alone does not reliably reduce implicit bias in behavior; without structural follow-through, effects fade [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Individual journaling with an identity map template, for settings without facilitation capacity
- Pair dyads using constructivist listening (listener paraphrases, does not respond or advise)
- Whole-faculty protocol cycles (e.g., National School Reform Faculty–style protocols) revisited across a semester
- Integrated into teacher-education coursework or induction mentoring, where reflection recurs with coaching support

### Target Learners
- Pre-service and in-service educators preparing to work with student populations whose identities differ from their own
- Educators engaged in equity, culturally responsive teaching, or family-engagement initiatives
- Mentors and coaches, whose interpretations of teacher practice are themselves identity-laden

### Target Learning Goals
- Professional self-awareness: recognizing how positionality shapes perception and judgment
- Bias identification: surfacing assumptions that affect expectations and interactions
- Empathy and relationship-building: grounding [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) work in the educator's own experience of identity
- Equitable practice: converting awareness into concrete changes in interactions with students and families

### Instructions
1. Individually create an identity map: place yourself at the center and mark the identity categories most salient to you, noting which you chose and which were assigned.
2. In pairs, share maps using constructivist listening — the listener paraphrases and asks clarifying questions only, practicing [Active Listening](active_listening.md).
3. Reflect in writing on one marker: how might it shape what you notice, assume, or value about students and families who differ from you on that dimension?
4. Connect to practice: identify one recurring interaction pattern (e.g., with a particular student or family) and analyze it through the lens of your map, using [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) or peer discussion to test the interpretation.
5. Revisit and revise the map periodically; treat it as a living document tied to ongoing [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or professional learning community cycles.

## Examples
- **CityBridge Education (Breakthrough Design Fellowship)** — participants complete an identity mapping exercise modeled on the National School Reform Faculty protocol "The Paseo," mapping key identity markers and discussing how these shaped their experiences as educators ([CityBridge Education](https://citybridge.org)).
- **National School Reform Faculty** — publishes free protocols including identity-focused activities and constructivist listening dyads used in Critical Friends Groups ([NSRF](https://www.nsrfharmony.org)).
- **Teacher education coursework** — programs grounded in culturally responsive pedagogy (e.g., Hammond's framework) begin with teacher self-examination of culture and positionality before analyzing student learning behavior.
