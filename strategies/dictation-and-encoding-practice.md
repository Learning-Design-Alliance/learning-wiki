# Dictation And Encoding Practice

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Dictation asks learners to transcribe spoken words, phrases, or sentences into writing; encoding practice is the broader activity of producing spellings from sound rather than reading them from print. Because the learner must segment the speech stream into phonemes and retrieve or construct the letter sequence, dictation exercises the productive direction of the orthographic mapping process that reading alone does not.

## Design Implications

Dictation is effective because production demands retrieval: recognizing a word in print requires far less memory strength than spelling it from sound, and the act of generating the letter sequence strengthens the orthographic representation that supports later automatic reading [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Encoding practice works best when the dictated material is decodable given the phonics patterns already taught, so learners apply rather than guess [Systematic phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]. Dictation of longer units (phrases, sentences) also builds working-memory span for language by requiring learners to hold and reconstruct sequences [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Dictated material matched to taught sound–spelling correspondences (decodable text, not arbitrary word lists)
- Immediate feedback against the correct written form, so errors are corrected before they consolidate ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md))
- Repeated, distributed encounters with the same spellings across sessions ([Spaced Repetition](../elements/spaced-repetition.md))

#### Constraints
- Dictation of irregular or not-yet-taught words forces rote visual memorization and yields weaker generalization than pattern-based encoding [Systematic phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [-S]
- Pure test-format dictation (mark-and-return) without corrective re-practice produces little spelling gain; feedback plus revision is what drives improvement [-M]
- For learners already reading fluently, dictation of familiar words adds little and can consume time better spent on composition [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Word-level dictation for early phonics; sentence dictation to add syntax, punctuation, and memory load
- Partial dictation (fill in the target pattern within a printed frame) to reduce load for beginners
- Self-dictation: learner studies a word, covers it, writes it from memory, and checks — a retrieval-practice variant usable independently

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers and spellers who are still building phoneme–grapheme mappings [Systematic phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]
- Struggling spellers and learners with weak orthographic memory, for whom production practice is more diagnostic and more corrective than silent reading
- Second-language learners consolidating sound–spelling relationships in a new orthography [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Orthographic mapping: securing sound–letter correspondences into long-term memory
- Automatic word recognition and spelling fluency [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Sentence-level transcription skills: punctuation, capitalization, and holding language in memory while writing

### Instructions
1. Select dictated material containing only taught patterns plus a small number of review words; sequence from single words to phrases to sentences ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md))
2. Say the word or sentence clearly, once, and give learners time to segment and write it ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
3. Reveal the correct form and have learners compare letter by letter, marking and correcting their own errors ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md))
4. Re-dictate corrected items in a later session rather than the same one, distributing retrieval across days ([Spaced Repetition](../elements/spaced-repetition.md))
5. Fade support by moving from partial dictation with printed frames to full dictation from memory ([Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md))

## Examples
- **[Jolly Phonics](https://www.jollylearning.co.uk)** — structured synthetic phonics program in which children write (dictated) letters and words corresponding to each taught sound from the first lessons.
- **[Spelling Mastery](https://www.mheducation.com)** (Direct Instruction) — daily dictation of words and sentences built from taught morphographs, with immediate correction.
- **Dictogloss** — learners hear a short text, reconstruct it from notes, then compare against the original; a sentence-level dictation variant used in second-language writing instruction.
