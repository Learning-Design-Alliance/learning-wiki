# Engage Positive Role Models

## Description
Engaging positive role models brings credible people — family members, community professionals, older peers, alumni — into learning activities as speakers, mentors, coaches, or collaborators. The strategy leverages observational learning: learners who see people like themselves succeeding at a task come to believe they can succeed too, and gain concrete images of what that success looks like.

## Design Implications

Role models work through [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md): learners acquire behaviors, standards, and self-efficacy beliefs by observing others, especially models perceived as similar, competent, and warm [Bandura, 1977] [+S]. Perceived similarity matters most — a model who shares the learner's background raises self-efficacy more than a distant expert [~M], and self-efficacy in turn predicts persistence in academic settings [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. Role models also raise task value when they demonstrate how content connects to real lives and careers [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Models who are credible in the target domain and relatable to the learner population (shared background, identity, or trajectory)
- Structured interaction — Q&A, mentoring, joint work — rather than one-way presentation
- Preparation of both model and learners: clear roles, expectations, and framing questions
- Follow-up activities that let learners connect the model's story to their own goals ([Reflection](../elements/reflection.md), career mapping, goal setting)

#### Constraints
- Models who are highly accomplished but dissimilar to learners can depress rather than raise self-efficacy ("they could never be me") [~M]
- One-off guest appearances without follow-up produce novelty effects, not durable motivation [-W]
- Coordination burden falls on teachers; involvement skews toward families with schedule flexibility, risking inequitable access [-M]
- If the model's path implies talent rather than effort ("I was just gifted at math"), it can reinforce fixed beliefs [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **In-person vs. virtual**: video or videoconference visits lower coordination costs and widen the pool of models
- **Peer and near-peer models**: slightly older students are often more effective than adults because similarity is maximized [Schunk & Hanson, 1985] [+M]
- **Embedded vs. event-based**: models integrated into ongoing units (e.g., judging projects, co-running [Expert Groups](../elements/expert-groups.md)) outperform standalone assemblies
- **Family involvement programs**: sustained, structured partnership models (e.g., Epstein's six-type framework) show stronger achievement effects than diffuse "involvement" [+M]

### Target Learners
- Students from groups underrepresented in a field, who benefit most from seeing similar others succeed [~M]
- Early adolescents and teens forming identity and career aspirations
- Learners with low self-efficacy or weak connections between school and future goals [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement: building task value and expectancy for success
- Career and identity development: expanding learners' sense of possible futures
- Social-emotional learning: belonging, empathy, and community connection
- Domain knowledge: authentic perspectives that contextualize academic content

### Instructions
1. Identify the gap the model should address (aspiration, self-efficacy, content relevance) and recruit models with both credibility and similarity to your learners.
2. Brief the model: emphasize narrating their *process* — struggles, strategies, effort — not just outcomes, consistent with [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../elements/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) modeling.
3. Prepare learners with background on the model and generative questions; assign roles if models join group work ([Expert Groups](../elements/expert-groups.md), [Peer Teaching](../elements/peer-teaching.md)).
4. Structure interaction as dialogue — Q&A, interviews, or collaborative tasks — not lecture.
5. Follow up with reflection or goal-setting so learners connect the model's trajectory to their own; revisit the model's example in later lessons.

## Related Strategies
- [Ask Experts](../principles/ask-experts.md) — a complementary move where learners interrogate expertise directly rather than observing it
- [Community-Based Learning](../principles/community-based-learning.md) — extends role-model contact into sustained engagement with community settings
- [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) — contact with diverse models supports perspective-taking alongside aspiration

## Examples
- **[Cristo Rey Network](https://cristoreynetwork.org)** — corporate work-study program placing high school students in professional workplaces, pairing them with adult workplace mentors over four years.
- **[Big Brothers Big Sisters](https://www.bbbs.org)** — long-term one-on-one youth mentoring; meta-analytic evidence links mentoring programs to modest but reliable gains in academic and socioemotional outcomes.
- A middle-school math teacher invites alumni now working in trades and healthcare to co-run a measurement unit, having each narrate a real problem they solved and the effort it took — then students interview them in small groups and write a "path to here" timeline.
