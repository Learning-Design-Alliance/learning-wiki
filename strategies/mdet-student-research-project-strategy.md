# Structure undergraduate courses so students autonomously carry out smartphone-based experimental research projects on everyday phenomena, with an introductory lesson, feedback, and graded presentations.

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · no claims cited

## Description
The article describes a course implementation in which "the potential of mobile devices as experimental tools was presented" in an early lesson with technological background, motivating examples and resources; students then autonomously devised experimental projects, received feasibility feedback and hints, and finally presented and were graded on their projects. The authors argue this provides a "proof of concept" that such projects can mobilize higher order thinking skills: autonomy, curiosity, creativity, problem solving and critical thinking. Extensions of student work by instructors turned projects into starting points for further investigation.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- An early lesson presenting technological background, motivating examples, and resources (literature, sensor-specific apps), as the article describes for the course implementation.
- Feedback on feasibility and hints during the work, plus final presentation and grading of the projects.
#### Constraints
- Many MDET experiments have limited accuracy and precision compared to standard equipment, and many lab-setting measurements cannot be carried out with them.
- Students need the most mentoring in considering errors and uncertainties and critically assessing results.
- The article states an empirical study on motivational and learning outcomes was not in the scope of the present work.

### Target Learners
- undergraduate physics students

### Target Learning Goals
- experimental physics competences such as introducing variation, carrying out measurement series, and considering errors
- higher order thinking skills: autonomy, curiosity, creativity, problem solving, critical thinking
