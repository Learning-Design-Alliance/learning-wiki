# Games + Structured Activity

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students engage with educational games designed to introduce or reinforce specific concepts, then complete a structured activity that requires them to apply, explain, or extend what the game surfaced. The game provides engagement, immediate feedback, and low-stakes experimentation; the structured activity converts that experience into durable understanding through [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and [Application](../elements/application.md). The pairing matters because game play alone rarely produces transfer — the debrief and application work is where consolidation happens.

## Design Implications

Games can raise engagement and time-on-task, but meta-analytic evidence shows that learning benefits from serious games depend heavily on the instructional support wrapped around them — debriefing, collaboration, and structured reflection substantially increase effectiveness [~S]. Games deliver frequent, immediate feedback at the action level, which supports learning when it directs attention to the task and process rather than the score [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. The structured activity should require learners to articulate the concepts the game exercised, since self-explaining game strategies strengthens conceptual understanding beyond what play alone yields [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A game whose mechanics genuinely embody the target concept — not a quiz wrapper around unrelated game play
- A structured follow-on activity ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Application](../elements/application.md), or [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)) that names and applies the concepts exercised in play
- Debrief time in which learners connect game decisions to the underlying principles
- Clear alignment between game mechanics, learning objectives, and assessment criteria

#### Constraints
- Games with high extraneous mechanics (complex rules, reward systems unrelated to content) consume working memory without supporting learning [~M] — align with [Cognitive Load Management](../elements/cognitive-load-management.md) by keeping mechanics simple relative to the content
- Free-choice game play without debrief produces engagement but shallow encoding; learners often overestimate what they learned from playing alone [-M]
- Competition and leaderboards can undermine motivation for learners who value mastery over performance, shifting focus from learning to winning [-M]
- Poorly matched games — fun but conceptually thin — can actively mislead by rewarding strategies unrelated to the target content [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Game-first: play to build intuition, then formalize with instruction and structured practice
- Instruction-first: teach the concept, then use the game for low-stakes rehearsal and feedback
- Collaborative variant: teams play and debrief together, adding peer explanation to the structured activity ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md))
- Digital or analog: board games, simulations, and card games work on the same structure without technology

### Target Learners
- Learners with low prior knowledge, for whom the game provides safe, feedback-rich exploration before formal instruction [~M]
- Learners whose motivation is fragile — game framing can raise task value and persistence [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Less effective for advanced learners, for whom game scaffolds and mechanics may feel redundant or slow [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual intuition-building: experiencing a system's behavior before formalizing it
- Procedural fluency: repeated low-stakes practice with immediate feedback
- Strategy and decision-making: games make consequences of choices visible and rapid

### Instructions
1. Select or design a game whose core mechanics map onto the target concept; verify that winning requires the reasoning you want to teach.
2. Brief learners on the concept-level goal, not just the rules — tell them what to watch for during play.
3. Run the game session, ensuring feedback during play points to task and process, not just outcomes [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
4. Debrief: learners explain what strategies worked and why, connecting game decisions to the underlying principles ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) or [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
5. Follow with structured [Practice](../elements/practice.md) or [Application](../elements/application.md) that transfers the concept beyond the game context.

## Related Strategies
- [Simulation-Based Learning](../strategies/simulation-based-learning.md) — the same play-then-debrief structure applied to modeled systems rather than game mechanics
- [Gamification](../strategies/gamification.md) — applies game elements (points, levels, narrative) to non-game activities; here the game itself is the content vehicle
- [Case-Based Learning](../strategies/case-based-learning.md) — an alternative experiential anchor with more explicit structure and less intrinsic feedback

## Related Elements
- [Practice](../elements/practice.md) — the structured follow-on that consolidates what play introduced
- [Application](../elements/application.md) — transfer of game-learned concepts to authentic tasks
- [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) — the debrief mechanism that converts experience into understanding
- [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) — team play and joint debrief add peer explanation
- [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) — the game's core instructional mechanism during play

## Examples
- **DragonBox (https://dragonbox.com)** — algebra concepts introduced through game mechanics; effective when followed by worked problems that formalize the notation the game implicitly taught.
- **Minecraft Education Edition (https://education.minecraft.net)** — classroom lessons embed curriculum tasks in game play, with structured build challenges and reflection prompts.
- **Kahoot! (https://kahoot.com)** — quiz-game review followed by teacher-led discussion of missed items; the debrief, not the game, addresses the errors.
- **Model UN and market simulation games in economics courses** — negotiation or trading games followed by structured debrief linking decisions to supply/demand and incentive concepts.
