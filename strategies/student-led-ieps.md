# Student Led IEPs

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A student-led IEP shifts the annual special education planning meeting from an adult-run review into a meeting the student prepares for and directs. The student introduces themselves, explains their disability and how it affects learning, presents current goals and progress data, requests or negotiates accommodations, and helps set next-year goals. Preparation typically spans weeks and involves explicit instruction in self-determination skills, rehearsal, and structured materials such as scripts, slides, or portfolios.

## Design Implications

Student-led IEPs are a high-stakes application of self-advocacy instruction: the meeting itself becomes an authentic performance task with a real audience and real consequences [Authentic audiences improve student work.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M]. The approach is grounded in self-determination research showing that autonomy and competence experiences drive intrinsic motivation and post-school outcomes [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Students who understand and can articulate their own accommodations are better positioned to request them independently in college and work settings.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit, extended preparation — instruction on the student's disability, strengths, needs, and current IEP content; a single rehearsal is insufficient
- Age-appropriate materials: visual scripts, slide templates, progress-data summaries the student can read and explain
- Adult facilitation that transfers control gradually — teachers and families must genuinely hand over speaking time rather than tokenizing participation
- A meeting culture where IEP team members address the student, not the adults, and respond to student proposals seriously
- Ongoing [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) during the year so the student has current progress data to present

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops sharply when preparation is rushed or when the student's participation is ceremonial (reading a prepared statement without understanding it) [-M]
- Students with significant communication or cognitive support needs require augmented formats (AAC, choice-based participation); a full verbal presentation model will exclude them if applied rigidly [~M]
- Family and teacher resistance — adults accustomed to controlling meetings can undermine student leadership; without adult retraining the meeting reverts to form [-M]
- Poorly handled disclosure discussions (student explaining their disability to a room) can harm [belonging](../elements/belonging.md) if the framing is deficit-oriented [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full leadership**: student runs the entire meeting agenda (most common in secondary transition planning)
- **Partial participation**: student presents one section (e.g., strengths or progress) while adults lead the rest — appropriate for younger students or first attempts
- **Portfolio model**: student compiles a work portfolio or video presentation used in lieu of live presentation
- **Curriculum-embedded**: programs such as the *Self-Determined Learning Model of Instruction* (SDLMI) build the underlying goal-setting and self-monitoring skills across the year, with the meeting as capstone

### Target Learners
- Secondary students (middle and high school) preparing for transition to postsecondary education or employment [+M]
- Students with high-incidence disabilities (learning disabilities, ADHD, autism) in inclusive settings benefit most from the self-advocacy demands [~M]
- Students with extensive support needs can participate meaningfully through partial or augmented formats, with benefits for communication and choice-making skills [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-determination: goal setting, decision making, problem solving, self-monitoring
- Self-advocacy: disclosing and explaining one's disability and needs; requesting accommodations
- Academic self-knowledge: understanding one's own learning profile, strengths, and effective strategies
- Communication and presentation skills in an authentic, high-stakes context

### Instructions
1. **Build self-knowledge** — teach the student about their disability, evaluation data, and learning profile using accessible language and visuals ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) help structure this).
2. **Review the current IEP together** — walk through goals, services, and accommodations; have the student collect or interpret their own progress data ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) results become the student's evidence).
3. **Set goals collaboratively** — use a structured goal-setting routine such as the SDLMI so the student generates, monitors, and evaluates their own goals rather than receiving them.
4. **Prepare the presentation** — script, slides, or portfolio; rehearse multiple times with feedback, including handling questions and disagreement ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) with feedback, not memorization alone).
5. **Hold the meeting** — student leads the agenda; adults follow a "prompt only when needed" rule and address the student directly.
6. **Debrief and follow through** — after the meeting, review what the student proposed, what was decided, and the student's role in implementing and monitoring the plan during the year ([Check-ins](../elements/check-in.md)).

## Examples
- **[I'm Determined](https://www.imdetermined.org)** (Virginia Department of Education) — statewide initiative providing student-led IEP templates, "One-Pagers," and meeting scripts; students present their strengths, preferences, and needs at their own meetings.
- **[Zarrow Center for Learning Enrichment](https://www.ou.edu/zarrow)** (University of Oklahoma) — source of the *Self-Directed IEP* curriculum (part of the *Steps to Self-Determination* suite), which uses video modeling and lesson sequences to prepare students to lead their meetings.
- **SDLMI** (Wehmeyer, Palmer, Agran, Mithaug, & Martin) — a teacher-delivered instructional model in which students set and self-monitor learning goals; the IEP meeting serves as a natural capstone performance.
