# Timely Peer Review

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Timely peer review schedules structured peer feedback at several points across the life of an assignment: on drafts before submission, on the work the day it is due, and again after it has been graded. The repeated cycles signal that writing is an ongoing process and that feedback — peer or teacher — is a tool for continued improvement, not a terminal verdict.

## Design Implications

Peer review works because reviewers must evaluate work against criteria, which activates evaluative schema that transfer to their own writing [Peer assessment improves both reviewing and writing performance.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Timing is the critical variable: feedback received *before* a deadline can be acted on, while feedback received only after grading is typically discarded [Feedback is most effective when it targets the task and process rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Post-grading review rounds serve a different function — reframing graded work as revisable and sustaining a growth orientation rather than improving that specific piece.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rubric or review protocol that makes evaluation criteria explicit, so reviewers give task- and process-level comments rather than vague praise
- Protected class time or platform-supported windows for each review round
- A mechanism for acting on feedback — revision time, revision memos, or resubmission — so pre-deadline feedback is consequential
- A supportive climate where critique is framed as helping, not judging

#### Constraints
- Untrained reviewers default to surface-level comments ("looks good") that produce no revision [-M]; without rubric training or exemplar calibration, review quality is too low to help
- If no revision opportunity follows the pre-deadline review, the timing advantage disappears and the round becomes ceremonial [-M]
- Post-grading review of work students consider "finished" can feel punitive or pointless unless the instructor explicitly frames improvement of graded work as legitimate [-W]
- Scheduling three rounds consumes substantial class time; in compressed terms, the middle round is the first to be cut [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Reciprocal pairs, rotating groups of three or four, or anonymous digital review (e.g., [Calibrated Peer Review](https://calibratedpeerreview.org), Peergrade, Turnitin PeerMark)
- Same partners across rounds (builds trust and continuity) versus different partners each round (broadens perspectives)
- Reviewer comments only, or comments plus a rating against the rubric
- Instructor-mediated post-grading review, where the teacher's feedback becomes a shared object of discussion rather than private correspondence

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary writers, who benefit most when reviewers are trained and criteria are explicit [Peer assessment improves both reviewing and writing performance.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Students with low writing self-efficacy, for whom seeing peers' drafts and revisable graded work normalizes imperfection [Self-efficacy predicts persistence on academic tasks.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Weaker for novices with no evaluative schema yet — they need [worked examples](../elements/worked-examples.md) of strong and weak drafts before they can review meaningfully [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Revision skill: diagnosing and improving one's own drafts using external feedback
- Evaluative judgment: internalizing assignment criteria by applying them to others' work
- Feedback literacy: interpreting, filtering, and acting on critique
- Process orientation: treating writing as iterative rather than single-draft

### Instructions
1. **Train reviewers.** Model evaluation on an anonymous sample draft using the rubric, contrasting strong and weak reviews ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md), [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)).
2. **Pre-deadline round.** Students exchange drafts with sufficient time to revise; require a brief revision plan noting which comments they will act on and why ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)).
3. **Due-date round.** On submission day, pairs review the final draft against the rubric, surfacing discrepancies between intent and effect.
4. **Post-grading round.** After work is returned, students review the same piece (same or new partners) alongside instructor feedback, identifying one improvement worth making even though the grade is set — reinforcing that feedback drives growth, not just grades.
5. **Close the loop.** Have students log what changed across rounds; comparing drafts makes the value of each review visible ([Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md)).

## Examples
- **[SWoRD (Scaffolded Writing and Rewriting in the Discipline)](https://www.peerreview.io)** — Cho and Schunn's web-based system assigns each paper to multiple anonymous reviewers, aggregates calibrated ratings, and requires reviewers to evaluate the helpfulness of comments they receive, making review quality itself assessable.
- **[Calibrated Peer Review](https://calibratedpeerreview.org)** — Students first review calibration essays with known scores; only after matching expert judgments do they review peers, addressing the untrained-reviewer problem.
- A first-year composition instructor runs all three rounds on each major essay: workshop two weeks before the deadline, a rubric-based read on due date, and a post-grading session where students revise one paragraph for their portfolio — making revision of graded work a course norm.
