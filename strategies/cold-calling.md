# Cold Calling

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Cold calling is the practice of directing a question to a specific, named student rather than accepting only volunteer responses. It is carried out by posing a genuine, prepared question, naming the student, and treating the response as a contribution to be built upon rather than a test to be passed. It functions as a participation-distribution and accountability technique, most common in discussion-based and case-based classrooms.

## Design Implications

Cold calling increases the proportion of students actively processing a question, because every learner knows they may be called on and therefore rehearses an answer mentally [Active participation improves exam performance relative to passive listening.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. It also redistributes airtime away from a small set of volunteers, surfacing reasoning from students who would otherwise remain silent. Its effects depend heavily on classroom climate: cold calling perceived as punitive suppresses participation, while cold calling framed as respectful inquiry increases both voluntary and involuntary participation over time [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions that are genuinely answerable — tied to assigned reading, prior work, or recently taught content, not trivia or gotchas
- Adequate wait time after the question and after the student's response ([Wait Time](wait-time.md))
- A response climate where wrong or partial answers are treated as material for discussion, not failure
- Consistency: applying the technique predictably and equitably across the class, not targeting individuals

#### Constraints
- Raises anxiety for some students, particularly those with social anxiety or who are new to a discussion culture; unmitigated, this reduces rather than increases engagement [-M]
- Perceived unfairness (calling on unprepared students to embarrass them) damages trust and belonging [~M]
- In large anonymous lecture settings without prior relationship-building, it can feel coercive and produce compliance rather than thinking
- If questions are recall-only, cold calling enforces attention without producing deeper processing [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Warm calling**: warn students in advance (e.g., "I'll come to you in a moment") or share the question before naming the student
- **Pre-briefed cold calling**: distribute questions before class or assign roles in [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) so all students prepare an answer
- **Randomized selection** (name cards, apps): signals fairness and prevents perceived targeting
- **Group cold calling**: direct the question to a team that confers briefly before one member responds, lowering individual stakes

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and graduate students in discussion-heavy courses (business, law, medicine, seminar formats), where evidence is strongest [~M]
- Reluctant participants who have relevant preparation but do not volunteer; cold calling draws them in without requiring self-initiation [~M]
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for students experiencing acute anxiety or for classes early in the term before norms are established

### Target Learning Goals
- Verbal reasoning and articulation: students must formulate and defend answers aloud
- Preparation accountability: ensuring assigned reading or pre-work has been processed
- Class-wide engagement: keeping all students cognitively active during discussion, not only speakers [Active participation improves exam performance relative to passive listening.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Prepare questions in advance, sequenced from lower to higher demand, and tied to material students have had a fair chance to learn.
2. Pose the question to the whole class first; allow several seconds of [Wait Time](wait-time.md) so every student rehearses an answer.
3. Name the student (or use randomized selection), keeping tone invitational rather than evaluative.
4. After the response, use [Probing](../elements/probing.md) or follow-up questions to extend the answer; involve peers ("Who can build on that?") rather than correcting immediately.
5. Debrief periodically with students about how cold calling is used, and adjust — the technique's benefits depend on students perceiving it as fair and supportive.

## Examples
- **Harvard Business School case method** — cold calling is the default participation mechanism; students are assigned case questions in advance, so cold calling enforces preparation rather than surprise ([HBS Case Method](https://www.hbs.edu/mba/academic-experience/Pages/the-hbs-case-method.aspx)).
- **Dallimore, Hertenstein & Maruffi's classroom research** — studies in business courses found required (cold-called) participation correlated with higher perceived learning and, over a term, increased voluntary participation as norms settled.
- **Kagan Structures (K-12)** — randomized "numbered heads together" variants where teams confer and a randomly selected member reports, combining cold calling with [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md).
