# Observations and Conversations

## Description
Observations and conversations are formative assessment practices in which instructors monitor learning by watching students work and by engaging them in purposeful dialogue — asking probing questions, listening to peer discussion, and documenting what they hear and see through anecdotal notes. The goal is to surface student thinking as it happens, so instruction can be adjusted before misconceptions consolidate rather than after a summative test reveals them.

## Design Implications

Observation and conversation are core moves of formative assessment, and formative practices that feed forward into instruction show some of the largest documented effects on achievement [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Their value depends on the quality of the questions asked and the instructor's responsiveness: open prompts that elicit reasoning ("How did you decide that?") reveal far more than yes/no checks, and the resulting data must actually change instruction to count as formative [Formative assessment practices improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Skilled questioning: instructors must elicit reasoning, not just answers, and respond contingently to what they hear ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- A documentation system — anecdotal notes, checklists, or conferencing records — that makes observations usable for later instructional decisions
- Time built into the lesson for circulating, listening, and conferring; a classroom culture where students talk through their thinking publicly
- A plan for acting on the evidence: regrouping, reteaching, or adjusting task difficulty

#### Constraints
- Judgments from observation are subjective and vulnerable to instructor bias (halo effects, attention concentrated on vocal or struggling students) [-M]
- Sampling is incomplete: a few students' responses may not represent the class, and some outcomes (e.g., retention over time) are poorly captured [-M]
- Time-intensive; difficult to sustain in large classes without structured routines [-M]
- Conversations can become evaluative interrogation, suppressing the honest disclosure of confusion they are meant to elicit [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Circulating observation**: instructor moves among groups during collaborative work, listening for reasoning patterns
- **Conferencing**: brief one-on-one conversations (e.g., 5-minute writing conferences) focused on a specific piece of work
- **Structured check-ins**: whole-class routines such as exit questions or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) prompts that scale observation to larger groups
- **Peer observation**: students observe and question each other, extending coverage and building evaluative capability

### Target Learners
- All levels, K–12 through adult education; especially valuable for emergent bilinguals and young learners whose understanding outpaces their written output [~W]
- Students in hands-on, exploratory settings (STEM labs, projects, [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)) where the process of reasoning matters as much as the product
- Struggling learners benefit most when observation leads to contingent, in-the-moment support [Contingent scaffolding responsive to real-time evidence improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Assessing conceptual understanding and problem-solving processes, not just final answers
- Developing self-regulation: students who expect to explain their thinking monitor it more carefully
- Gathering qualitative evidence on collaboration, strategy use, and misconceptions that tests miss

### Instructions
1. Define what evidence of understanding would look like for the current task before the lesson begins.
2. Circulate during work time, listening to student dialogue and asking open probes ("Why does that work?" "What would happen if…?") ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
3. Record brief anecdotal notes tied to specific students and specific evidence, not general impressions.
4. Hold short conferences with individual students on their work in progress ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)).
5. Use the same day's evidence to adjust instruction — regroup, reteach, or raise challenge — and close the loop with [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md).

## Examples
- **Cognitively Guided Instruction (CGI)** — elementary teachers interview students about how they solved arithmetic word problems, using the conversations to select the next problems ([https://www.education.wisc.edu/ci/cognitively-guided-instruction/](https://www.education.wisc.edu/ci/cognitively-guided-instruction/)).
- **Reading and writing workshops** (e.g., Teachers College Reading & Writing Project) — teachers confer one-on-one with students during independent work, keeping dated anecdotal records that drive subsequent mini-lessons.
- **Inquiry-based science labs** — instructors circulate among lab groups, listening to hypothesis talk and probing with "What's your evidence?" before deciding whether to intervene.
