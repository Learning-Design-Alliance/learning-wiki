# Shared Reading

## Description
Shared reading is an interactive reading experience in which an adult reads aloud while learners follow along with the visible text, often with an enlarged or duplicated copy so every child can track print. The adult models fluent, expressive reading, makes thinking visible through prediction and questioning, and invites children to join in on refrains, rhymes, and eventually whole passages. Over repeated sessions, responsibility for reading shifts from adult to child, making shared reading a bridge between being read to and independent reading.

## Design Implications

Shared reading works because it combines expert modeling of fluent reading with active participation, letting children attend to meaning and print features without having to decode everything themselves [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]. Its distinctive value over plain read-alouds comes from interactivity: dialogic techniques — asking open-ended questions, prompting predictions, and requesting expansions of children's utterances — produce larger gains in language and emergent literacy than passive adult reading [Dialogic reading techniques add value over straightforward read-alouds.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.44.2.398) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Text visible to all learners (big books, projected pages, or individual copies) so print can be tracked while it is read
- An adult fluent enough to model prosody and expressive reading accurately
- Repeated readings of the same text across sessions, so children can join in progressively as the text becomes familiar
- Deliberate interactive moves: prediction prompts, picture walks, comprehension questions, and print-referencing (pointing to words, letters, and punctuation)

#### Constraints
- Adult-only reading without child participation reduces the approach to passive listening, which yields weaker language gains than dialogic interaction [-S]
- Ineffective if the text is far beyond learners' comprehension level — children cannot predict or discuss a story they do not understand [~M]
- Gains depend on adult skill in scaffolding talk; untrained readers tend to read straight through without print-referencing or questioning [-M]
- Does not by itself teach systematic decoding; it complements rather than replaces explicit phonics instruction [Phonics instruction outperforms whole-word approaches for generalization.](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Dialogic reading** (Whitehurst): child-centered prompting with PEER sequence (Prompt, Evaluate, Expand, Repeat) and CROWD prompts (Completion, Recall, Open-ended, Wh-, Distancing)
- **Enlarged-text shared reading** (Don Holdaway's original "shared book experience"): big book or projected text with the whole class tracking print
- **Partner/echo reading** for older readers: alternating turns reading aloud, shifting the balance toward the learner as fluency grows
- **Repeated reading of familiar favorites**: rereading builds automaticity and lets children join in on memorized text, connecting print to speech

### Target Learners
- Preschool through early elementary children building print awareness, concepts of print, and vocabulary
- Beginning readers who benefit from hearing fluent reading modeled before attempting it themselves [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Children with limited home literacy exposure, for whom school-based shared reading may be their primary source of book experience [+M]
- Older struggling readers, adapted as partner or echo reading with turn-taking

### Target Learning Goals
- Emergent literacy: concepts of print, directionality, letter–sound connections
- Oral language and vocabulary growth through discussion of rich text
- Reading fluency: prosody, phrasing, and expression modeled and then imitated
- Comprehension strategies: predicting, questioning, and making connections

### Instructions
1. Select a text slightly above independent reading level but within comprehension range, with engaging illustrations and predictable or rhythmic language.
2. Do a picture walk before reading, activating predictions and background knowledge ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
3. Read aloud with expressive fluency while tracking print, pointing to words so children connect spoken and written language ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
4. Pause for interactive prompts — predictions, open-ended questions, invitations to join refrains — and expand children's responses rather than simply evaluating them ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
5. Reread across sessions, inviting children to take over increasingly larger portions of the text as familiarity grows ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
6. Extend into writing or retelling — journaling, illustration, dictated sentences — to connect reading and writing ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).

## Related Strategies
- Dialogic reading — the best-researched variant, emphasizing child-led conversation around the book
- Echo and partner reading — the turn-taking adaptation for older or more advanced readers
- Repeated reading — rereading for fluency, naturally embedded in shared reading routines

## Examples
- **Dialogic reading interventions** (Whitehurst's original studies): parents of 2–3-year-olds trained in PEER/CROWD prompting showed significant gains in expressive language compared with controls ([https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.24.4.552](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.24.4.552)).
- **Big-book shared reading** in kindergarten classrooms (Holdaway's shared book experience): teacher reads an enlarged text while the class tracks print, then children reread it in small groups.
- **Reading Rockets** ([https://www.readingrockets.org](https://www.readingrockets.org)) — practitioner guidance and video models of interactive read-aloud techniques for teachers and parents.
- A teacher reads *The Very Hungry Caterpillar* with a big book, pausing at each page for children to predict ("What do you think he'll eat next?") and join the refrain, then children illustrate and dictate their own "hungry" story pages.
