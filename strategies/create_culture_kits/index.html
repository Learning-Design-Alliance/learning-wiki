Create Culture Kits¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

A culture kit is a curated collection of 3–5 personally meaningful objects, images, or artifacts that a student assembles to represent facets of their cultural background, family history, or identity. Students present their kits to peers, respond to questions, and often write reflectively about the objects' significance, converting private identity knowledge into shared classroom knowledge.

Design Implications¶

Culture kits work by activating students' funds of knowledge and positioning them — rather than the teacher or textbook — as experts on the content, which supports identity safety and engagement in culturally responsive classrooms [~W]. Because the objects are concrete and personally chosen, they anchor abstract discussion of culture in specific, memorable referents, and the sharing format builds peer relationships that sustain later collaborative work [~W]. The strategy functions as both an Activating Prior Knowledge activity and a community-building ritual; its learning payoff depends on structured discussion and reflection, not the assembly itself.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear framing that culture includes language, food, traditions, geography, hobbies, and family roles — not only ethnicity — so every student has entry points
  • A protocol for sharing and questioning (e.g., peers ask clarifying rather than judgmental questions) to keep disclosure safe
  • Teacher modeling: the instructor presents their own kit first, including honest uncertainty, to set norms
  • A reflection or writing component that consolidates what students learned about themselves and others

Constraints¶

  • Students from marginalized backgrounds may feel exposed or tokenized if sharing is compulsory or if their culture becomes a teaching spectacle [-W] — participation in disclosure should be gradable-free and offer alternatives (e.g., a kit about a hobby or future identity)
  • Superficial "foods and festivals" treatments can reinforce stereotypes rather than complicate them if the teacher does not push discussion toward within-culture variation and change over time [-W]
  • In low-trust classrooms, early identity disclosure can backfire; kits work better after community norms are established [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Physical kits of real objects sent in a bag or box; strongest for younger learners and sensory engagement
  • Digital kits (slides, Padlet boards, or video walkthroughs) for remote or older learners, lowering the risk of lost or expensive objects
  • Mystery kits assembled by the teacher from a culture being studied, used as an inquiry prompt before students build their own
  • Curriculum-linked kits where objects connect to an upcoming unit (e.g., immigration, geography), so the kits become primary sources for later analysis

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 students in diverse classrooms establishing community at the start of a year or course [~W]
  • Multilingual learners, whose home languages and artifacts become assets rather than deficits during sharing [~W]
  • Adult and pre-service teachers, for whom building and exchanging kits surfaces their own cultural assumptions before they teach diverse populations [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Intercultural understanding and perspective-taking
  • Identity development and self-knowledge
  • Oral communication and narrative skills through structured sharing
  • Classroom community formation that supports later Collaborative Learning

Instructions¶

  1. Model first. Present your own culture kit, narrating why each object matters and where your knowledge is incomplete.
  2. Frame culture broadly. Offer categories (traditions, places, language, values, hobbies) so no student is reduced to an ethnic label.
  3. Assemble. Students gather 3–5 objects or images over several days; provide a planning sheet with prompts.
  4. Share in structured rounds. Use small groups or a circle with a question protocol rather than whole-class presentation for every student; reserve Class Discussion for cross-cutting themes.
  5. Debrief toward generalization. Ask what surprised students, what cultures have in common, and how cultures change — moving from artifacts to concepts.
  6. Reflect and connect. Students write about one thing they learned about a peer and one thing the exercise revealed about their own identity; revisit kits when relevant content arises.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — culture kits are an identity-centered variant that makes students' prior knowledge a classroom resource
  • Check-ins — lower-stakes community rituals that can precede or follow kit sharing
  • Community-based Learning — extends the same asset-framing beyond the classroom to neighborhoods and families
  • Use structured e-mail exchanges with target-language peers to build cross-cultural interest and motivation
  • Panel protocol: open with community cultural resources, then moderated discussion connecting community assets to computing education

Examples¶

  • Rookie Teacher of the Year practice (Bank Street College teacher education) — pre-service teachers build culture kits as a first assignment to experience being "the other" and to practice asset-based framing before their own classrooms.
  • "Me Bags" in Responsive Classroom schools — a simplified K–2 variant where each child brings one meaningful object during the first weeks of school to establish sharing norms (Responsive Classroom).
  • Museum outreach kits — institutions such as the Smithsonian Center for Learning and Digital Access provide digital artifact collections teachers can use as pre-assembled culture kits for inquiry units.
