# Prioritize improving sleep quality for lower-performing students rather than increasing academic pressure

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 1 study (1 associational), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article recommends that for students with weaker academic performance, adequate sleep should be treated as a lever for improving outcomes rather than adding study time. It states, "Rather than increasing academic pressure on lower-performing students, priority should be given to improving their sleep quality," arguing this enhances learning efficiency and emotional well-being while reducing anxiety and depression symptoms. It also recommends managing electronic device use, especially before bedtime, and balancing homework demands with sufficient sleep opportunities.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Adequate sleep opportunity must be feasible within students' schedules, which the article links to homework load and device-use management.
#### Constraints
- The supporting evidence is observational, from Shanghai eighth-graders; the article cautions that associations may be confounded by unmeasured variables.

### Target Learners
- Lower-performing eighth-grade students in high-pressure middle school contexts

### Target Learning Goals
- Academic performance across subjects, particularly cognitively demanding ones such as mathematics and science
