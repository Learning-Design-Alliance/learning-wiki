# Self-Regulation - Mindfulness Techniques

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Incorporate mindfulness techniques — brief breathing exercises, body scans, and attention-anchoring practices — to help learners notice and manage their initial emotional impulses and reactivity. Self-regulation is the ability to alter and regulate emotional and behavioral responses; mindfulness trains the attentional and monitoring capacity that underlies it, giving learners a pause between stimulus and reaction.

## Design Implications

Mindfulness training reliably improves attention and reduces mind-wandering, which supports the attentional control needed for self-regulated learning [Mindfulness training improves attention and reduces mind-wandering.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612459659) [+M]. Effects on academic outcomes are smaller and more variable than effects on attention and well-being, so mindfulness should be positioned as a support for learning, not a substitute for instruction [School-based mindfulness programs show modest, variable effects on academic outcomes.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tate.2014.05.003) [~M]. Brief practices (2–5 minutes) integrated at natural transition points outperform long, decontextualized sessions for classroom use.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A facilitator who has personal practice experience; scripted delivery without familiarity reduces credibility and effectiveness
- A predictable, low-stimulation routine (consistent time, place, and duration)
- Voluntary participation norms — practices framed as invitations, not mandates
- A follow-on link to the learning task ("Now we'll begin the problem set with that focus")

#### Constraints
- Mandated or performative mindfulness can backfire; adolescents in particular respond poorly to practices framed as behavior control [-W]
- Learners with trauma histories may find body scans or eyes-closed practices distressing; offer eyes-open and movement-based alternatives [-W]
- Effects fade without regular practice; one-off sessions show little durable benefit [Neurocognitive changes from mindfulness depend on sustained practice.](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3916) [~M]
- Not a treatment for clinical anxiety or attention disorders; do not substitute for appropriate support

#### Implementation Variability
- Breathing anchor (2–3 min) at lesson start or after transitions
- Body scan or progressive relaxation before high-stakes assessments
- Movement-based versions (mindful walking, posture resets) for younger learners or eyes-open preferences
- Integration into advisory periods, SEL curricula, or [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) routines

### Target Learners
- Learners with high reactivity or test anxiety, who gain most from the pause-and-notice routine [+M]
- Adolescents and adults, who can engage with the metacognitive framing; younger children need shorter, concrete, movement-based forms
- Learners with strong existing regulation skills gain little; effects are largest where baseline attention control is weakest [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing and modulating emotional and behavioral responses
- Attention management: sustaining focus and reducing mind-wandering during [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and independent work
- Emotional awareness: building the vocabulary and noticing skills that support reflection

### Instructions
1. Establish a consistent trigger point (start of class, after recess, before assessments) so the practice becomes automatic rather than negotiated.
2. Lead a brief (2–5 minute) attention practice: breath anchor, body scan, or sound focus. Model it yourself rather than only directing it.
3. Debrief in one or two sentences — what did learners notice? Connect noticing to the upcoming task ([Attention](../elements/attention.md)).
4. Transition immediately into learning activity; the practice is a ramp, not a standalone lesson.
5. Fade facilitation over weeks: shift from teacher-led to brief student-led or silent self-initiated practice.

## Examples
- **MindUP** (Goldie Hoxha Hahn Foundation, https://mindup.org) — evidence-informed curriculum embedding brief "brain break" breathing practices across K–8 lessons.
- **Learning to BREATHE** (https://learning2breathe.org) — a published mindfulness curriculum designed for adolescents, typically delivered in 6–18 sessions.
- **Pre-assessment reset** — a 2-minute breathing anchor before an exam, framed as "settling attention," with an eyes-open option for students who prefer it.
