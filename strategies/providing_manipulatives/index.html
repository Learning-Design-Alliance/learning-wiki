Providing Manipulatives¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 causal), q2–q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Providing manipulatives means giving learners physical or virtual objects — base-ten blocks, fraction tiles, balance beams, simulations — that they can act on directly to model abstract concepts. The strategy rests on the idea that embodied, hands-on interaction grounds symbolic content in perceptual and motor experience, making invisible structures (place value, ratios, forces) observable and testable.

Design Implications¶

Manipulatives support learning when they function as representations of a concept rather than as objects of play, and when instruction explicitly connects the concrete object to the abstract symbol system it stands for Uttal and colleagues argue manipulatives fail when learners treat them as meaningful in themselves rather than as symbols. [~M]. A meta-analysis of 55 studies found small but reliable benefits overall, with effects strongest when learners are actively taught the mapping between object and concept and weakest when manipulatives replace rather than supplement symbolic instruction Carbonneau, Marley, and Selig's meta-analysis found overall small positive effects moderated by instructional support and perceptual richness. [+M]. Physical experience itself can enhance conceptual understanding in science by grounding ideas in sensorimotor activity Physical experience enhances science learning. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Explicit linkage between the manipulative, the concept, and the formal notation — the teacher must name the mapping ("this rod is one ten; the numeral 1 in this column means one ten")
  • A structured task with a goal, not free exploration alone (Practice with the representation)
  • A planned fade toward abstract representation (Fading from concrete → representational → symbolic, as in the Concrete-Representational-Abstract sequence)
  • For virtual manipulatives, interaction that is meaningful rather than decorative clicking

Constraints¶

  • Highly perceptually rich objects can distract from the target structure; "seductive details" in the object compete with the concept for attention Carbonneau et al. found plain, non-realistic manipulatives often outperform richly realistic ones. [~M]
  • Manipulatives alone, without explicit mapping to symbols, produce little transfer to symbolic tasks Uttal, Scudder, and DeLoache showed transfer fails when the object–symbol connection is left implicit. [-M]
  • Learners can develop dependence on the concrete support and fail to internalize the abstraction if the concrete phase is never faded
  • Handling objects adds motor and procedural load that can overwhelm novices if the task is complex; simplify the surrounding task (Cognitive Load Management)
  • Benefits shrink or reverse for learners who already grasp the abstraction Carbonneau et al. found effects diminish with learner age and prior knowledge. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Physical manipulatives: Cuisenaire rods, base-ten blocks, algebra tiles, fraction strips; best for tactile grounding and social negotiation
  • Virtual manipulatives: Brainingcamp, The Math Learning Center apps, PhET simulations; scalable, low-cost, and can constrain actions to relevant ones
  • Everyday objects vs. mathematical models: realistic contexts boost engagement but can blur the mathematical structure; simplified models make structure salient
  • Duration: brief concrete experiences to introduce a concept vs. extended Concrete-Representational-Abstract instructional sequences for struggling learners

Target Learners¶

  • Young children and novices who lack the prior knowledge to interpret abstract notation directly Physical experience enhances science learning. [+M]
  • Learners with learning disabilities in mathematics, for whom CRA sequences have consistent evidence Carbonneau et al. found stronger effects for learners with disabilities. [+M]
  • Less beneficial for advanced learners, for whom concrete supports can be redundant and slow Carbonneau et al. found effects diminish with age and expertise. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding of mathematical and scientific structures (place value, fractions, conservation, equilibrium)
  • Bridging informal intuition to formal symbolism
  • Spatial and proportional reasoning

Instructions¶

  1. Select a structurally transparent manipulative — choose the simplest object whose internal structure maps onto the target concept; avoid rich realism that invites distraction (Cognitive Load Management)
  2. Set a goal-directed task — pose a problem the manipulative helps solve, not free play (Practice)
  3. Make the mapping explicit — name the correspondence between object features and symbols while learners act; have learners articulate it themselves (Articulation)
  4. Move toward abstraction — transition from objects to drawings to symbols, fading the concrete support as competence grows (Fading)
  5. Check transfer to symbols — verify learners can solve bare symbolic problems without the object before retiring it

Related Strategies¶

  • Multiple Representations — manipulatives are one representation in a representational sequence
  • Worked Examples — a teacher-modeled manipulation demonstrates how the object encodes the concept before learners manipulate independently
  • Unplugged coding with printable motion and trigger blocks as a physical introduction to online coding

Examples¶

  • Cognitively Guided Instruction — teachers use counters and drawings to model children's solution strategies for word problems, connecting informal strategies to formal arithmetic
  • PhET Interactive Simulations — virtual manipulatives (e.g., Balancing Chemical Equations, Circuit Construction Kit) that let learners vary parameters and observe consequences
  • Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) intervention — a scripted sequence used widely in special education mathematics, moving from base-ten blocks to drawings to numerals
