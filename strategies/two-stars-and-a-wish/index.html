Two Stars And A Wish¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 against) · 6 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 qualitative), q2–q3 · 2 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Two Stars and a Wish is a low-stakes peer feedback protocol: each reviewer identifies two specific strengths of a peer's work and one actionable improvement, phrased as a wish ("I wish you would…") rather than a criticism. The fixed 2:1 ratio and simple sentence frames make feedback accessible to young or inexperienced reviewers while guaranteeing that every round of feedback contains both affirmation and a next step.

Design Implications¶

The protocol operationalizes Assessment for Learning: feedback that describes current performance against criteria and points to the next step improves achievement, especially when learners act on it Formative feedback practices improve achievement. [+S]. The "stars" component is not mere cushioning — naming what works requires evaluative judgment against criteria, which develops the reviewer's own understanding of quality. The single "wish" keeps improvement focused; one prioritized suggestion is more likely to be acted on than a list of deficits.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • The work under review must be visible to peers (drafts, performances, products)
  • Shared criteria or a rubric so "stars" and "wishes" reference quality, not taste
  • Sentence frames or modeling of specific, kind, useful comments before the first round
  • Time for the author to read feedback and revise — feedback without a revision opportunity rarely changes work
  • A shared rubric or success criteria so "stars" and "wishes" reference the task, not taste
  • Training in specific, descriptive language ("Your opening states a claim" vs. "It's good")
  • Time for learners to act on the wish in a revision cycle, or the feedback is wasted
  • A check-in or norm-setting step so critique feels safe

Constraints¶

  • Generic praise ("nice job") satisfies the format without informing learning; teachers must require evidence-linked comments tied to criteria
  • The 2:1 ratio can train learners to expect praise and soften or bury the improvement; over time the "wish" should carry more weight
  • Novice reviewers often lack the expertise to diagnose why something is weak, producing wishes that are vague or wrong [~M] — pair with exemplars or a checklist to scaffold judgment
  • Online implementations with checkbox-style or rubric-only evaluation lose the diagnostic specificity that makes the protocol work Checklist-based evaluation is less effective for online feedback. [-M]
  • The 2:1 ratio can train learners to expect praise and discount improvement suggestions; over time the wish should carry equal weight
  • If no revision time follows, the protocol becomes a ritual with no learning payoff

Implementation Variability¶

  • Oral gallery walk: work posted around the room; peers leave sticky notes with two stars and a wish
  • Written slips or digital comments: Google Docs comments, Padlet, or Seesaw annotations
  • Two Stars and a Step: replaces "wish" with a concrete next step, tightening actionability for older learners
  • Author-selected wish: reviewer offers two stars and two candidate wishes; the author chooses which to act on, building evaluative autonomy
  • Reverse protocol: authors pre-identify their own two stars and one wish before peer review, priming self-assessment
  • Two stars and a step: replace "wish" with a concrete next action to increase actionability
  • Silent gallery walk: written sticky-note feedback on displayed work, reducing social pressure
  • Teacher-modeled rounds: instructor demonstrates the format on anonymous work before peers try it
  • Escalating ratio: shift from 2:1 toward 1:1 or 1:2 as learners develop evaluative skill

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary and lower-secondary students, for whom the sentence frames make peer feedback tractable [+W]
  • Novice writers and creators at any age who need both encouragement and a single clear next step
  • Less suitable as-is for advanced learners, who benefit from more extended critique and multiple improvement points [~W]
  • Elementary and secondary learners new to giving and receiving critique — the structure scaffolds the social skill
  • Less suitable for advanced learners who can handle open critique formats without a fixed ratio

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Improving product quality through revision (writing, design, problem-solving)
  • Developing evaluative judgment — recognizing quality criteria by applying them to others' work
  • Building a supportive feedback culture and peer-learning norms
  • Evaluative judgment: recognizing quality features in work against criteria
  • Feedback literacy: giving and receiving specific, non-evaluative critique
  • Revision behavior: translating feedback into concrete improvements

Instructions¶

  1. Share the task criteria or rubric and model the protocol on a sample work, thinking aloud about what makes a comment specific (Think-Aloud modeling of the feedback itself).
  2. Pair or group learners and distribute work for review.
  3. Each reviewer writes two stars — strengths tied to criteria — and one wish phrased as an actionable suggestion.
  4. Authors read feedback and identify one change to make; build in time for revision (Practice with feedback as the scaffold).
  5. Debrief: collect example stars and wishes that were most useful, refining the class's feedback quality over successive rounds.

Related Strategies¶

  • Gallery Walk — a common delivery format for the protocol
  • Peer Review — the broader family; Two Stars and a Wish is its most scaffolded entry point
  • Critique Protocol — a more demanding variant for older learners
  • Gallery critique — a whole-class variant using the same star/wish structure on displayed work
  • Author's chair — oral sharing format where the protocol structures audience response
  • Rubric co-construction — building the criteria that make stars and wishes specific

Examples¶

  • Primary writing workshops (e.g., Units of Study classrooms): students exchange drafts and complete star/wish slips before revising.
  • Seesaw: classmates record audio two-stars-and-a-wish comments on posted work in elementary portfolios.
  • Design critique in maker education: project teams present prototypes; audience members give two stars and a wish on sticky notes before the next iteration.
  • Writing workshops (elementary): after drafting personal narratives, students trade papers and complete a Two Stars and a Wish slip tied to the class's narrative checklist before revising.
  • Design critiques (secondary STEM): teams present prototypes; audience members post sticky-note stars and wishes on a feedback board, which the team prioritizes for the next iteration.
  • Peergrade / Peerstudio-style platforms: structured peer-review prompts can be configured with the two-strengths-one-suggestion template, with rubric criteria attached to each field.
