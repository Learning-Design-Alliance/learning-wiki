# Skimming

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Skimming is a pre-reading strategy in which learners rapidly survey a text — headings, subheadings, captions, figures, bolded terms, and summaries — to build a preliminary map of its structure and key points before reading it thoroughly. For younger students this often takes the form of a "picture walk" through illustrated pages. The goal is not comprehension of detail but activation of relevant knowledge and formation of expectations that subsequent reading can confirm or revise.

## Design Implications

Skimming functions as a form of advance organizer: a brief exposure to the structure of upcoming material that provides anchoring subsumers for the detailed content that follows [Ausubel's advance organizers improve retention of unfamiliar material.](../claims/prior-knowledge-not-related-to-performance.md) [+M]. By surfacing headings and emphasis cues, skimming directs attention to high-value information and reduces the working-memory burden of the later thorough read [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the skim being structured and purposeful — an unfocused flip through pages produces little benefit.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Reading materials with navigable structure (headings, captions, figures, or highlighted terms)
- A focused skim task — specific questions or features to locate — rather than unstructured page-turning
- Sufficient background knowledge to make sense of what the skim surfaces; skimming activates prior knowledge but cannot substitute for it
- A follow-up thorough reading in which the preview is used ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md))

#### Constraints
- Unfocused skimming with no guiding purpose yields minimal comprehension benefit [-W]
- Learners lacking relevant background knowledge cannot connect skimmed features to a coherent schema, so the preview does not aid later comprehension [~M]
- Skimming can create an illusion of familiarity; students who skim may believe they have understood the text and skip the thorough read [-W]
- For dense, unfamiliar, or highly technical texts, surface features may be too opaque to generate useful expectations [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Picture walk** (early grades): learners narrate what they see in illustrations and predict content before reading
- **Structured text survey** (secondary/postsecondary): a checklist — title, headings, first and last paragraphs, bold terms, figures — completed in 3–5 minutes
- **Teacher-guided skim**: the teacher selects which headings and captions matter most for the lesson, narrowing attention to lesson-relevant features
- **Question-driven skim**: learners skim to answer pre-questions, which primes relevant schema during the subsequent read

### Target Learners
- All grade levels; particularly students who struggle with reading comprehension, for whom a structural preview compensates for weaker self-generated organization [+W]
- Learners encountering unfamiliar topic domains, where an organizer provides missing anchoring structure [+M]
- Less beneficial for expert readers of a domain, who already generate their own text structure and may find a mandated skim redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: improving understanding of the full text after a structured preview
- Text-structure awareness: learning how informational texts signal importance through headings, captions, and emphasis
- Activation of prior knowledge: connecting new material to what learners already know before detailed reading

### Instructions
1. Select the reading and identify the headings, captions, figures, and highlighted terms most relevant to the lesson ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)).
2. Pose 2–3 guiding questions or a short survey checklist so the skim has a purpose ([Activation](activation.md)).
3. Have learners skim for 3–5 minutes, locating the targeted features and noting predictions or questions ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
4. Briefly discuss predictions to surface prior knowledge and misconceptions ([Activation](activation.md)).
5. Assign the thorough reading, asking learners to confirm or revise their predictions ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).

## Examples
- **Elementary picture walk**: before reading a trade book, kindergarteners walk through the illustrations, predicting the story; the teacher records predictions to revisit after reading.
- **Textbook survey routine**: a biology instructor has students spend five minutes surveying a chapter — headings, figure captions, end-of-chapter summary — then write one question the chapter should answer before reading it fully.
- **Reciprocal Teaching** ([https://www.readingrockets.org/strategies/reciprocal_teaching](https://www.readingrockets.org/strategies/reciprocal_teaching)) — includes "predicting" from text features as its opening move, a guided skim embedded in a comprehension routine.
