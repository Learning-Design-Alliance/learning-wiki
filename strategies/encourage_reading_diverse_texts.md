# Encourage Reading Diverse Texts

## Description
Learners read a wide range of text types — narrative, informational, argumentative, visual/multimodal — across genres and disciplines, then think and talk about what they read. The strategy treats breadth of reading experience as a driver of academic language acquisition: each genre exposes learners to distinct vocabulary, syntax, and discourse structures that single-genre reading cannot supply.

## Design Implications

Exposure to varied text structures builds the genre knowledge and vocabulary that underpin academic comprehension, and discussing texts converts passive exposure into productive language use [~M]. The sequence matters: reading followed by structured thinking and talking about different genres is a robust routine for acquiring academic language, because talk forces learners to rehearse discipline-specific registers rather than merely recognize them. Text diversity also supports transfer, since learners must repeatedly map ideas across different structures and perspectives [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of underlying structure.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to a genuinely varied text collection — different genres, disciplines, difficulty levels, and cultural perspectives
- A purpose for reading each text (question, task, or discussion prompt), not just exposure
- Structured opportunities to think and talk: [Discussion Sections](../elements/discussion-sections.md), written response, or [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) tasks
- Some instructional framing of genre features, so learners notice *how* texts are built rather than only what they say

#### Constraints
- Unstructured independent reading of diverse texts produces weak vocabulary gains without discussion or accountability; talk is the active ingredient, not volume alone [-M]
- Learners may choose texts well below or above their instructional level without guidance; free choice improves motivation but can reduce comprehension growth when text difficulty is unmanaged [~M]
- Text difficulty that exceeds learners' decoding or background knowledge consumes working memory needed for comprehension [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [-M] — scaffolding or accessible alternatives (e.g., [Audiobooks](../principles/audiobooks.md)) are needed
- Diverse texts without discussion of differing perspectives can leave stereotypes unexamined rather than challenged [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Text sets**: clusters of texts on one topic in multiple genres and perspectives, read comparatively
- **Genre cycles**: the class rotates through genres (e.g., memoir → report → argument), with genre-feature mini-lessons between
- **Choice boards**: learners select from a curated diverse menu, balancing autonomy with level-appropriateness
- **Multimodal diversity**: podcasts, infographics, and video count as "texts" and broaden register exposure

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults developing academic language, who need exposure to the dense nominal syntax and technical vocabulary of informational and disciplinary texts [~M]
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from encountering academic registers across contexts and from discussion that rehearses new vocabulary [~M]
- Struggling readers, when texts are paired with audio or scaffolded discussion rather than assigned cold
- Less effective as-is for early readers still building automatic word recognition; decoding support must come first

### Target Learning Goals
- Academic vocabulary acquisition and morphological awareness
- Genre knowledge and disciplinary literacy — understanding how historians, scientists, and mathematicians read and write differently
- Comprehension of complex text structures (cause/effect, claim/evidence, narrative arc)
- Perspective-taking and critical evaluation of sources

### Instructions
1. Curate a text set spanning genres, disciplines, and perspectives on a shared topic or theme.
2. Activate relevant knowledge before reading ([Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md)) and set a purpose for each text.
3. Assign texts with a framing task — [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md) paired with annotation prompts that direct attention to genre features [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention during reading.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M].
4. Have learners think and talk: structured [Discussion Sections](../elements/discussion-sections.md) or written [Self-Explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) prompts comparing how the texts treat the topic [+S].
5. Close with a synthesis task that requires using the vocabulary and structures encountered (short written argument, summary across texts).
6. Assess through discussion participation, cross-text summaries, and learners' application of new vocabulary in their own writing.

## Examples
- **Text sets on a science topic**: students read a news report, a primary research abstract, and a narrative essay about the same phenomenon, then discuss how each genre frames evidence — a core disciplinary literacy routine.
- **CommonLit thematic units** pair a short story with an informational article on the same theme, with discussion questions bridging the two genres.
- **Shanahan & Shanahan's disciplinary literacy work** in Chicago public schools had chemistry and history teachers teach the distinctive reading moves of their disciplines using authentic texts from each field.
