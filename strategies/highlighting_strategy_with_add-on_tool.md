# Highlighting Strategy with Add-on Tool

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students use a Google Doc add-on tool to create individualized highlighters in different colors over a shared text. For example, all text supporting "Argument #1" might be highlighted red, evidence for "Argument #2" green, and so on. Highlights can be exported by color into a table that gathers all ideas in each category; students then add a column in which they write summaries of everything in that highlighted category, converting an attention-directing act into a generative one.

## Design Implications

Highlighting is best understood as an *attention* strategy, not a learning strategy in itself: emphasis directs attention to relevant information [Emphasis and relevance cues direct attention during reading.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M], but learning gains come from what students do next. Unstructured highlighting is one of the least effective study techniques [Student-generated underlining is often no better than experimenter-provided underlining and can produce illusions of competence.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~M]; the add-on workflow improves on plain highlighting by (a) imposing a category scheme before reading, (b) making the highlighting visible and exportable, and (c) requiring a written summary per category, which adds the generative processing that highlighting alone lacks [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding beyond re-reading.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to Google Docs and a highlighting add-on (e.g., [Highlight Tool](https://workspace.google.com/marketplace/app/highlight_tool/526435045620)), plus time for reading, highlighting, and summarizing
- A teacher- or student-defined category scheme (color legend) established *before* reading — categories should map to the analytical goal (claims, evidence, counterarguments, rhetorical moves)
- A text complex enough to warrant categorization but within learners' reading range
- A summarization step; export tables without the added summary column leave students with sorted text but little learning

#### Constraints
- Highlighting alone is a low-utility technique: students who only highlight tend to treat selection as learning and show poor inference-making [Student-generated underlining is often no better than experimenter-provided underlining and can produce illusions of competence.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [-M] — the summary column is not optional
- Over-highlighting (marking more than roughly 20–30% of the text) eliminates the attention-directing benefit [Emphasis and relevance cues direct attention during reading.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [-M]
- Novices often highlight what *looks* important (topic sentences, bold terms) rather than what serves the analytical goal; without a category scheme and modeling, colors become decorative
- Requires device access and add-on installation permissions, which some school IT environments restrict

#### Implementation Variability
- **Teacher-defined vs. student-defined categories:** begin with a given legend; as expertise grows, have students devise their own categories — a shift from [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) toward independence
- **Collaborative highlighting:** groups highlight a shared document, then compare and defend divergent selections in discussion
- **Cross-text synthesis:** students highlight multiple sources with the same color scheme, then use the exported tables to compare evidence across texts
- **Analog tools:** the same color-code-then-summarize sequence works with physical highlighters and a three-column chart when devices are unavailable

### Target Learners
- Middle school, high school, and adult learners building reading comprehension and analysis skills
- Students who highlight indiscriminately and need an external structure to force selective, goal-directed marking [Emphasis and relevance cues direct attention during reading.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]
- Multilingual learners, when paired with [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) in text selection — the color legend doubles as a language scaffold
- Less beneficial for advanced readers who already categorize fluently; for them the table export may feel like busywork [Student-generated underlining is often no better than experimenter-provided underlining and can produce illusions of competence.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: identifying and organizing key information in expository or argumentative text
- Analysis: distinguishing claim types, evidence, and rhetorical functions within a text
- Summarization and synthesis: producing category-level summaries from exported highlights [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding beyond re-reading.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Metacognition: making students' selection decisions visible and discussable

### Instructions
1. **Set the category scheme.** Define 3–5 colors mapped to analytical categories (e.g., red = claim, green = supporting evidence, blue = counterargument). Fewer categories keep working memory load manageable [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
2. **Model the strategy.** Demonstrate highlighting one paragraph aloud, explaining why a sentence earns a color and why a tempting one does not ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
3. **Read and highlight.** Students apply the legend to the shared Google Doc using the add-on's individualized highlighters
4. **Export by color.** Use the add-on to generate a table of all highlights grouped by category
5. **Summarize each category.** Students add a column and write a summary of the ideas in each color — this is where learning happens [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding beyond re-reading.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
6. **Discuss and compare.** Students compare highlight patterns with peers; divergent selections become the basis for [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)
7. **Provide feedback.** Review highlights and summaries for accuracy and completeness ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))

## Examples
- **Argument analysis in ELA:** students highlight a persuasive essay (claims / evidence / counterarguments), export by color, and write a per-category summary that becomes a draft of an analytical paragraph
- **Primary-source work in history:** a shared document of excerpts from multiple sources, color-coded by perspective; exported tables let students compare how each source frames the same event
