# Feed Up, Feed Back, Feed Forward Model

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The Feed Up, Feed Back, Feed Forward Model structures feedback around three questions: **Feed Up** — Where am I going? (learning intentions and success criteria); **Feed Back** — How am I going? (evidence of current performance relative to those criteria); **Feed Forward** — Where to next? (specific actions and activities to close the gap). The model, articulated most influentially by Hattie and Timperley (2007), treats feedback as an information system rather than a judgment, and insists that all three components be present for feedback to improve learning rather than merely report on it.

## Design Implications

Feedback is among the most powerful influences on learning, but its effects are highly variable — poorly designed feedback (personal praise, grades without information) can depress achievement [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [~S]. The three-question structure disciplines feedback design: without a clear Feed Up, learners cannot interpret Feed Back; without Feed Forward, feedback terminates in evaluation rather than action.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clear learning intentions and success criteria shared with learners before work begins ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
- Evidence of current performance gathered through [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) or [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)
- Time and routines for discussion, reflection, and revision — feedback that arrives after the opportunity to act is inert
- [Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md) framed at task and process levels, not the person

#### Constraints
- Feedback directed at the self ("you're so smart") undermines performance and can reduce persistence [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-S]
- Grades or marks accompanying comments can cause learners to ignore the comments entirely [~S]
- Depends on learners' capacity to interpret and act on feedback; novices need Feed Forward made concrete rather than implied
- Time-intensive; whole-class feedback routines are needed to make it sustainable

#### Implementation Variability
- Teacher-to-student, peer feedback (structured protocols), or self-assessment against success criteria
- Whole-class "feedback lessons" addressing common gaps instead of individual written comments
- Digital tools that deliver criterion-referenced comments and next-step tasks

### Target Learners
- All learners, but especially those developing self-regulation, since the three questions mirror self-regulatory monitoring [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Struggling learners, who benefit most from explicit Feed Forward specifying next actions rather than gap statements alone
- High-achieving learners may need less Feed Back and more Feed Forward at the self-regulation level [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Progress monitoring toward explicit learning intentions
- Metacognition and self-reflection: internalizing the three questions as a self-regulatory habit
- Closing performance gaps through deliberate next-step practice

### Instructions
1. **Feed Up:** Establish and communicate learning intentions and success criteria before the task ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)); co-construct criteria where possible.
2. **Feed Back:** Gather evidence of performance ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)) and give criterion-referenced feedback at the task and process levels ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)).
3. **Feed Forward:** Specify concrete next steps — a revision task, a targeted practice activity, a re-teaching opportunity — and create time to act on them.
4. **Reflect:** Have learners self-assess against the criteria and articulate their own "where to next" ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)), building toward self-regulated feedback loops.

## Examples
- **Writing conferences:** A teacher uses the model in a one-on-one conference — the student restates the learning intention (Feed Up), examines their draft against the success criteria (Feed Back), and selects a revision focus for the next draft (Feed Forward).
- **Whole-class feedback:** After marking a set of essays, a teacher delivers a feedback lesson addressing three common gaps, then gives revision time — Feed Forward built into class time rather than appended comments.
- **[Hattie's Visible Learning](https://visible-learning.org) work** popularized the three questions as a diagnostic for auditing whether classroom feedback actually functions as information.
