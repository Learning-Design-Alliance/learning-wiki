# Letter Strategy

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The letter strategy is a mnemonic technique that uses acronyms and acrostics to aid memory. Acronyms are words whose individual letters represent elements in a list (e.g., HOMES for the Great Lakes); acrostics are sentences whose first letters cue the to-be-remembered items (e.g., "My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Nine Pizzas" for the planets). Both work by imposing a familiar, meaningful structure on arbitrary material, reducing the burden of rote rehearsal [Mnemonic devices reduce working memory load by chunking to-be-remembered items into a single retrievable unit.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

## Design Implications

Letter strategies convert unstructured lists into a single retrieval cue, which is especially valuable when the order or completeness of recall matters and the content itself has no inherent logic [Mnemonic strategies reliably improve recall of list-based material relative to free study.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Their benefit is strongest for retention of factual content; they do little for conceptual understanding, so they should be paired with meaning-oriented activities [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding beyond memorization.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Content that is genuinely list-like or sequential — acronyms applied to non-list material distort it
- A memorable, pronounceable cue word or sentence; learners should be taught how the letters map to items ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of the mapping process)
- Periodic retrieval practice to maintain the association [Spaced repetition improves retention of mnemonic-cued material.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Poorly constructed acronyms (unpronounceable, or letters that don't map cleanly) can interfere with recall rather than support it [~M]
- Supports recall of items, not understanding of relationships; learners may recite an acronym without knowing what the items mean [-M]
- Effectiveness declines as material grows in volume or complexity — a 20-letter acronym is itself a memory load [~M]
- Learners with strong existing knowledge of the content often find mnemonics redundant and slower than direct recall [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Teacher-provided vs. learner-generated mnemonics; learner-generated cues are more personal and often more durable, but require instruction in the generation process (e.g., the IT FITS routine)
- Acronyms (single words) for short lists; acrostics (sentences) for longer or ordered lists
- Combined with keyword imagery for vocabulary: the letter cue points to a keyword, which triggers a visual image

### Target Learners
- Novices facing arbitrary lists, sequences, or taxonomies with no inherent structure [Mnemonic devices reduce working memory load by chunking to-be-remembered items into a single retrievable unit.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Students with learning disabilities or memory challenges, for whom mnemonic instruction has unusually strong, well-replicated effects
- Less valuable for advanced learners who already organize content through schemas [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Factual recall: lists, sequences, categories, steps of a procedure
- Vocabulary retention when paired with keyword imagery
- Not appropriate as the sole goal for conceptual understanding or transfer [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding beyond memorization.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Identify the list or sequence to be memorized and verify it is stable (not likely to change).
2. [Demonstrate](../elements/demonstration.md) the mapping: show an existing acronym (e.g., HOMES), making explicit how each letter cues each item.
3. Have learners construct their own acronym or acrostic, or refine a provided one; encourage pronounceable, vivid cues.
4. Pair the letter cue with meaning — learners should be able to state what each letter stands for and explain the item ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) the list helps).
5. Schedule retrieval practice with the cue removed, spaced over time [Spaced repetition improves retention of mnemonic-cued material.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S].
6. Fade the mnemonic once recall is automatic, shifting learners to direct retrieval.

## Examples
- **HOMES** (Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, Superior) — classic acronym for the Great Lakes.
- **IT FITS** (Identify the term, Tell the definition, Find a keyword, Think about the definition, Study) — a strategy-instruction routine teaching students to generate their own mnemonics for vocabulary.
- **LINCS** (List the parts, Imagine a picture, Note a reminding word, Construct a LINCing story, Self-test) — the University of Kansas Strategic Instruction Model routine for content-area vocabulary ([SIM](https://sim.kucrl.org)).
- **ROY G BIV** for the colors of the visible spectrum in physics and art instruction.
