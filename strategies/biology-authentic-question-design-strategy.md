# Design interdisciplinary exam and homework questions that ask students to use physics and mathematics for biological ends, such as explaining organismal structure, function, and diversity

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (5 for) · 2 studies (2 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

## Description
This strategy operationalizes disciplinary authenticity in course materials: rather than adding biology-flavored contexts to physics-style calculation problems, questions should require students to use mathematical and physical tools for uniquely biological purposes. The article's exemplars ask students to relate equations to biological systems — for example, to "explain how the circulatory system of mammals is able to generate a higher flow rate than the circulatory systems of trees" using the Hagen-Poiseuille equation, or to describe the constraints diffusion places on worm size and shape. Such questions convey that physics and mathematics offer insight into organismal diversity.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions must demand use of the tools for biological ends, not merely situate a standard calculation in a biological context
#### Constraints
- The article notes a biology-related context alone is insufficient; the trout-density calculus problem had a biological context but its activities served only obtaining a mathematical expression

### Target Learners
- life-science undergraduates in introductory physics courses
- introductory biology students

### Target Learning Goals
- using mathematics and physics to understand organismal structure, function, and evolution
