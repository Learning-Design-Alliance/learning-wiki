Mastery Based Retesting¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q2–q4 · 2 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

Mastery based retesting structures assessment so that a test is not a terminal verdict but a checkpoint: learners who fall short of a defined standard receive targeted reteaching, then retest on the unmastered material. The recorded score reflects eventual mastery rather than the first attempt, and retests are typically capped, staggered, and paired with a required remediation activity so that retaking is earned through further learning, not repetition alone.

Design Implications¶

The strategy operationalizes Bloom's "learning for mastery" model: with adequate time and instruction, most learners can reach criteria that traditional grading reserves for the few, and holding the standard constant while varying time produces large achievement gains Kulik et al.'s meta-analysis of mastery learning programs. [+S]. Retesting converts assessment into feedback — it only works when the gap between attempts involves genuine instructional change, not merely another look at the same test Formative assessment improves achievement when feedback drives instructional adjustment. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An explicit, criterion-referenced standard for each unit — what "mastery" means, stated before instruction (Assessment aligned to objectives)
  • Diagnostic feedback that identifies which objectives were missed, not just a total score
  • A required remediation step between attempts (targeted practice, reteaching, Coaching) so retests measure new learning
  • Parallel test forms or item reordering to prevent memorization of answers
  • A gradebook policy that replaces, rather than averages, earlier scores — averaging punishes the learning curve and undermines the mastery message

Constraints¶

  • Retesting without substantive remediation produces score inflation, not learning — learners retest on short-term memory of corrected answers Formative assessment improves achievement when feedback drives instructional adjustment. [-M]
  • Time costs are real: mastery pacing slows the whole class when implemented uniformly, and the benefit shrinks in lockstep curricula where later units depend on everyone moving together Mastery learning effects diminish when instructional time is held fixed for all learners. [~M]
  • Unlimited low-stakes retests can erode the desirable difficulty of first attempts; learners may under-prepare knowing a retry is available Distributed practice improves retention. [~W]
  • Poorly specified standards make "mastery" arbitrary, inviting grade negotiation rather than learning

Implementation Variability¶

  • Unit-level mastery: whole-unit retest after reteaching (classic Bloom model)
  • Objective-level retests: learners retest only missed objectives, often via item banks (e.g., Adaptive Mastery Learning platforms)
  • Hybrid models: first-attempt scores count partially, final mastery score caps or replaces them
  • Group-based mastery: class moves on when a large majority (e.g., 80–90%) demonstrates mastery, with Adaptive Difficulty handling the remainder

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling learners and slower-paced students, who gain most when time varies and the standard does not Kulik et al.'s meta-analysis of mastery learning programs. [+S]
  • Learners with low academic self-efficacy, for whom replaceable scores reduce the stakes of early failure
  • Less beneficial for advanced learners already meeting standards on first attempts, who need enrichment rather than retesting Mastery learning effects diminish when instructional time is held fixed for all learners. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Foundational prerequisite skills where later content depends on solid early mastery (e.g., algebra procedures, phonics)
  • Criterion-referenced competencies with defensible standards (Competency-Based Learning)
  • Less suited to goals where first-attempt performance under realistic conditions is the outcome (timed performance, fluency under pressure)

Instructions¶

  1. Define mastery criteria per objective before instruction begins; publish them with the unit (Assessment).
  2. Teach the unit, then administer the first attempt as a diagnostic, not a verdict.
  3. Return objective-level feedback identifying what was missed and why.
  4. Require remediation tied to missed objectives — targeted Practice, reteaching, or peer explanation — before a retest is unlocked.
  5. Administer a parallel-form retest on missed objectives only; replace the earlier score.
  6. Cap retests (typically one or two per unit) and set a window, so preparation effort stays distributed across the term Distributed practice improves retention. [+S].

Related Strategies¶

  • Standards-Based Grading — the reporting system that makes replacement scores coherent
  • Formative Assessment Loops — the shorter-cycle version of the same feedback logic
  • Negotiate learning contracts at the start of term and allow test retakes until mastery

Examples¶

  • Bloom's "Learning for Mastery" (University of Chicago, 1968) — the original model: unit tests, corrective instruction, parallel retests, with ~80% of mastery-group students reaching levels only ~20% of control students attained.
  • Khan Academy — mastery system where skill levels (attempted → proficient → mastered) can drop if spaced follow-up items are missed, requiring re-demonstration rather than one-time completion.
  • Modern Classroom Project — blended self-paced units in which mastery checks gate lesson progression; students revise and retake checks until passing.
  • Medical licensing (USMLE Step examinations) — high-stakes criterion-referenced retesting with a defined standard and remediation requirement between attempts.
