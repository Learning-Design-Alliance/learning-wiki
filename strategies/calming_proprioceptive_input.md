# Calming Proprioceptive Input

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Using proprioceptive input, also known as "heavy work," involves engaging muscles and joints to provide a calming and organizing sensory experience. Activities include squishing play dough or a stress ball, pulling against resistance bands, pushing or moving chairs or desks, climbing, holding a heavy door open, carrying books, and chewing against resistance. These activities stimulate the proprioceptive system, which many practitioners report has a regulatory, calming effect — particularly for learners who are sensory-seeking or easily overwhelmed.

## Design Implications

Proprioceptive input is best understood as a regulatory support that prepares learners for instruction, not as an instructional technique itself. The strongest rationale comes from occupational therapy practice rather than controlled learning research: systematic reviews find the evidence base for sensory-based interventions to be limited and inconsistent [The evidence for sensory integration therapy as a learning intervention is weak and inconsistent.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [~W]. Effects, where observed, are typically short-lived and individualized, so input should be scheduled around instructional demands rather than delivered indiscriminately.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Low-cost materials: play dough, stress balls, resistance bands, weighted items, chewable tools, or naturally occurring heavy work (carrying books, moving chairs, opening heavy doors)
- A brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routine to establish the learner's baseline arousal state before and after input
- Clear expectations that heavy work is a tool for readiness, embedded in routines rather than used as a reward or escape

#### Constraints
- Evidence for sensory-based interventions as academic or behavioral treatments is weak; gains are often not distinguishable from attention or novelty effects [The evidence for sensory integration therapy as a learning intervention is weak and inconsistent.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [-W]
- Effects are transient — a few minutes of calming does not substitute for instruction in self-regulation skills [The evidence for sensory integration therapy as a learning intervention is weak and inconsistent.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [-W]
- Whole-class delivery can reduce instructional time and may distract learners who do not need it; benefits appear specific to learners with identified sensory regulation needs
- Activities must be safe, age-appropriate, and supervised; chewing items require hygiene protocols

#### Implementation Variability
- Embedded classroom jobs that involve heavy work (carrying a stack of books, wiping boards, moving chairs) normalize the input without singling learners out
- Portable options (stress ball, resistance band on chair legs) allow discreet self-administration during instruction
- Scheduled "movement breaks" before high-demand tasks (testing, sustained writing) versus on-demand access when a learner shows escalation

### Target Learners
- Children with sensory processing difficulties or autism spectrum conditions, for whom practitioners most often recommend heavy work [The evidence for sensory integration therapy as a learning intervention is weak and inconsistent.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [~W]
- Learners who show escalation, fidgeting, or withdrawal before demanding tasks — input may serve as a transition ritual
- Not indicated as a general intervention for typically regulating learners; unnecessary input consumes time without benefit

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation and emotional readiness for learning (a prerequisite condition, not a curricular goal)
- Behavioral participation: increasing time-on-task for learners whose dysregulation otherwise blocks access to instruction
- Independence: with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), learners can learn to recognize their own arousal states and select heavy-work activities proactively

### Instructions
1. Establish baseline with a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) — identify signs of dysregulation or over-arousal for this learner.
2. Select an individualized heavy-work activity matched to the learner's preference and the setting (pushing, pulling, carrying, chewing).
3. Deliver the input for a short, defined period (3–10 minutes) before or during a demanding task.
4. Re-check arousal and transition promptly into instruction; treat the input as preparation, not the lesson.
5. Fade adult prompting over time, using [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) so the learner initiates regulation independently; document as part of [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) where formalized in a plan.

## Examples
- **Classroom jobs as heavy work** — a student who carries the lunch bin or moves chairs for the class receives proprioceptive input as a natural, non-stigmatizing part of the routine.
- **Sensory paths and movement breaks** — schools such as those using published "sensory hallway" programs install wall push-up and climbing stations learners pass between activities.
- **Individual toolkits** — a resistance band tied to a chair's legs for leg-pressing during seatwork, paired with a chewable pencil topper for oral proprioceptive input.
