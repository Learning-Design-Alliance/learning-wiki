Authors' Chair¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 5 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 5 report an effect size

Description¶

The Authors' Chair is a writing-workshop routine in which a student sits in a designated chair and reads a piece of their own writing aloud to the class, then fields questions, comments, and feedback from peers. Developed by Donald Graves and Jane Hansen within the process-writing movement, it positions students as real authors writing for an authentic audience rather than producing work solely for the teacher.

Design Implications¶

The Authors' Chair builds an authentic audience into the writing cycle, which raises the stakes of drafting and revision and gives writers immediate evidence of how readers respond to their text Authentic audiences and purposes increase engagement and writing quality. [+W]. Peer questions and comments function as audience-based feedback, which is most useful when it addresses the writer's purpose and craft rather than surface errors Feedback is most effective at the task and process levels. [+S]. The routine also develops oral language, listening, and a classroom community of writers who see a range of approaches to the same task.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A predictable, low-stakes routine (regular time slot, designated chair, established norms for responding)
  • Explicit teaching of response norms: questions and specific, kind, helpful comments before criticism
  • A writing workshop structure in which pieces move through drafting and revision before sharing
  • Teacher modeling of what productive audience feedback sounds like

Constraints¶

  • Public sharing can threaten anxious writers, English learners, or students with weak oral fluency; forced participation reduces rather than builds writing identity [-W]
  • Unstructured peer response often defaults to "I liked it," which carries little informational value for revision [-M]
  • Dominant voices can monopolize response time unless turn structures (e.g., sentence stems, timed rounds) are imposed
  • Feedback on first drafts, before the writer has revised, tends to focus on mechanics rather than meaning [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Voluntary sign-up versus rotating schedule — voluntary builds ownership but lets reluctant writers defer indefinitely
  • Whole-class, small-group, or partner formats — small groups increase airtime per student and lower the affective filter
  • Digital variants — recorded read-alouds or shared documents with comment threads replicate the audience function in online or hybrid settings
  • Pre-conference — a brief teacher–writer conference before sharing focuses the audience's attention on one craft question

Target Learners¶

  • K–12 writers, especially in elementary writing workshops; the routine originated in primary classrooms and scales to secondary with more craft-focused response protocols
  • Reluctant writers who benefit from seeing peers' varied abilities and approaches [+W]
  • Students developing oral presentation skills alongside writing; requires adaptation (rehearsal time, sentence stems) for English learners and students with communication-related disabilities

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Writing as communication: understanding audience, purpose, and reader response
  • Revision: hearing where readers are confused or engaged identifies revision targets
  • Oral language and presentation fluency
  • Community and identity: developing a sense of self as an author

Instructions¶

  1. Establish response norms and model them, using Think-Aloud style modeling of a thoughtful audience comment.
  2. During writing workshop, students draft and revise; the teacher confers briefly with the sharing author to select a focus for audience response (Coaching).
  3. The author reads from the chair; peers ask clarifying questions first, then offer specific comments tied to craft or meaning (Class Discussion).
  4. The author notes feedback and returns to revision, closing the loop between audience response and Revision of the text.
  5. Rotate authorship so all students eventually share; keep sessions short (2–3 authors per sitting) to protect engagement.

Related Strategies¶

  • 5-minute writing conferences — the one-to-one counterpart; conferences prepare writers to make use of Authors' Chair feedback
  • Active listening — the audience skill the routine explicitly teaches and depends on
  • Acting-role-play — a related public-performance routine that builds oral confidence

Examples¶

  • Graves and Hansen's original routine in process-writing classrooms (Atwell, In the Middle; Calkins, The Art of Teaching Writing): daily sharing slots at the end of writing workshop, with peers responding using "I noticed… I wondered…" stems.
  • Two Writing Teachers blog community (https://twowritingteachers.org) documents digital adaptations, including virtual Authors' Chair via recorded read-alouds and comment threads in remote workshops.
