Adopt a hybrid human-AI tutoring model rather than replacing expert instructors with AI¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 8 claims (6 for, 2 mixed) · 6 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 causal), q2–q4 · 2 of 6 report an effect size · 7 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Based on the pooled findings — comparable ICEMS performance, a small low-certainty OSATS advantage, and higher extraneous cognitive load with AI — the authors recommend against replacing human instructors with AI tutoring systems. They state the evidence "supports a hybrid model", in which AI feedback is integrated into a training pathway as a parallel tutor or as a quantitative substrate informing expert instruction, while cautioning that this hybrid model itself "requires rigorous empirical validation".

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • rigorous empirical validation of the hybrid model before implementation

Constraints¶

  • the small OSATS advantage is of uncertain clinical significance and rests on low-certainty evidence driven by a single high-risk-of-bias study

Target Learners¶

  • medical trainees and surgeons in simulation-based surgical training

Target Learning Goals¶

  • simulated surgical skill acquisition
  • standardized performance feedback

Claims¶

  • AI tutoring imposes significantly higher extraneous cognitive load than expert instruction [+W]
  • No significant difference in ICEMS performance scores between AI tutoring and expert instruction [~W]
  • AI tutoring yields a small, statistically significant improvement in expert-rated OSATS scores versus expert instruction, with low certainty [~W]
  • Pooled AI tutoring produces statistically equivalent GRE learning gains to expert human tutoring [+M]
  • Ma et al. (2014) meta-analysis: step-based ITSs outperformed no tutoring and large-group teacher-led instruction, but showed no differences against small-group or one-to-one human tutoring [+M]
  • ITS substitution: meta-analyses find no significant cognitive learning differences between ITS and human tutoring or small-group instruction [+M]
  • VanLehn (2011) found step-based intelligent tutoring systems nearly as effective as average human tutors, and both more effective than no tutoring but less effective than expert human tutors [+M]
  • Intelligent tutoring systems improve achievement over non-individualised instruction [+M]
