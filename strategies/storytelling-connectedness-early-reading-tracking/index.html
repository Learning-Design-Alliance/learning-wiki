Use storytelling-based connectedness assessment as a low-cost, inclusive early tracking tool for reading acquisition¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 1 study (1 associational), q1 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

Because the storytelling task "does not require the child to be able to read," the authors recommend it as "a feasible, low-cost, and inclusive assessment" for tracking cognitive development related to reading acquisition at scale. Sessions take about 15 minutes per child, collecting three short narratives from positive affective images plus reading tasks. For countries like Brazil, the authors argue "scalable solutions could be crucial to planning public educational policies based on data." The strategy operationalizes the predictive finding that earlier connectedness forecasts later reading performance.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Trained interviewers to administer the storytelling task and general prompts when needed; automated or manual transcription; graph analysis pipeline

Constraints¶

  • Validated so far in a high-socioeconomic-status sample from three private schools; the authors call for replication in more diverse, less privileged samples

Target Learners¶

  • first- and second-grade children, including those not yet able to read

Target Learning Goals¶

  • early identification of future reading difficulties
  • tracking literacy-related cognitive development

Affordances¶

  • Word Recurrence Graph Connectedness

Claims¶

  • Oral narrative connectedness predicts reading performance 3–4 months in advance for phonological awareness, reading accuracy, and text comprehension, but not reading fluency. [+W]
  • Oral narrative connectedness in June positively correlates with phonological awareness, reading comprehension, and word accuracy measured in October. [+W]
  • Single-point connectedness measures from three stories are stable across the study period. [+W]
  • Girls showed higher oral narrative connectedness than boys, with a different pattern of association with reading performance. [~W]
