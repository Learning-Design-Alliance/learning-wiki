Graphic Organizers for Retrieval Practice¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 14 studies (7 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 5 of 14 report an effect size

Description¶

Students first recall as much as they can about a topic from memory — a "brain dump" — and then sort, cluster, and structure that retrieved information into a graphic organizer such as a timeline, concept map, or comparison matrix. Afterwards, they check against source texts to confirm, correct, or add information, which consolidates the retrieval benefit while making knowledge gaps visible.

Design Implications¶

The strategy combines two complementary mechanisms: retrieval strengthens memory more than restudying Retrieval practice improves long-term retention relative to restudying. [+S], and spatially organizing information into a coherent structure supports relational processing and reduces working-memory demands during integration Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. The sorting step matters: concept-map-style organization produces better relational learning than verbatim note-taking when learners actively select and connect ideas Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]. The verification step converts the organizer into a feedback event, which is most effective when it targets the task and process level rather than just marking items right or wrong Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A retrieval prompt (topic, question, or text just studied) and time for unaided recall
  • Graphic organizer templates matched to the knowledge structure (timeline for sequences, matrix for comparisons, concept map for hierarchies)
  • Access to source texts or notes for the verification phase
  • Learners must attempt recall before seeing the organizer; providing the organizer first turns the activity into copying rather than retrieval [-S]
  • A topic with enough conceptual structure (hierarchies, processes, cause–effect relations) to be worth diagramming
  • A retrieval phase conducted strictly from memory — no notes, texts, or peers during the dump
  • An organizer format appropriate to the content (concept map for taxonomies, flowchart for processes, matrix for comparisons)
  • Source material available for the verification phase, and time allocated to actually use it

Constraints¶

  • Time-consuming relative to a plain brain dump; the organizing and verification phases can double the activity's duration
  • If the organizer type is imposed and mismatched to the content structure, learners spend effort fitting information to the template rather than thinking about relationships [~M]
  • Learners with strong prior knowledge may gain little from the retrieval phase and find the organizer redundant — an expertise-reversal pattern [~M]
  • Copying from a text into an organizer without a genuine recall attempt first produces weak retention benefits [-S]
  • If the organizer is provided pre-filled or pre-structured, the retrieval benefit largely disappears — copying into a given framework is not retrieval [-S]
  • Organizing before retrieving (e.g., rereading notes then mapping) converts the activity into restudying, which is markedly less effective for retention [~S]
  • Poorly structured topics (isolated facts, lists) gain little from the organizer step; the mapping adds load without relational payoff [-W]
  • Learners unfamiliar with a diagram format may spend working memory on the format itself rather than the content; brief modeling of the organizer type is needed first [~M]
  • Verification must be genuine — students who skip checking against sources consolidate their errors, and high-confidence errors are especially persistent [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Individual vs. collaborative: pairs can negotiate where each item belongs, adding elaborative discussion
  • Progressive organizers: a partially completed organizer (some nodes or links given) scaffolds novices; a blank one suits more advanced learners
  • Repeated cycles: repeating the dump-and-organize sequence across spaced sessions compounds retention benefits [+S]
  • Digital tools (e.g., concept-mapping software) allow easy revision during the verification phase
  • Individual → paired: students map alone, then merge maps with a partner and negotiate differences; the discussion surfaces gaps neither noticed alone [+W]
  • Scaffolded → open: provide category labels or a partial skeleton on first use, then fade to blank organizers as learners gain facility
  • Repeated cycles: repeat the dump–map–verify sequence across days; each retrieval attempt strengthens memory and the organizer shows growing coherence
  • Digital tools: concept-mapping software (e.g., CmapTools) supports revision, though paper is equally effective and faster for quick cycles

Target Learners¶

  • Novices and intermediate learners who need both retrieval strengthening and help seeing relational structure [+M]
  • Students who overestimate their understanding from rereading; the dump makes gaps concrete [-S for rereading, +S for the strategy]
  • Less beneficial for experts, for whom retrieval of well-organized schemas adds little and the organizer may be redundant [~M]
  • Students in upper elementary through higher education; retrieval practice benefits are consistent across age groups, though younger learners need more organizer scaffolding [+S]
  • Students with some prior knowledge of the topic — enough to retrieve something, but with gaps and misconceptions the map will expose
  • Less suitable as a first exposure: learners with no prior knowledge have little to retrieve and should encounter the material first [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Factual and conceptual recall of recently studied material
  • Relational knowledge: hierarchies, sequences, cause–effect and comparative structures
  • Metacognition: identifying what is not yet known before a test or further study
  • Long-term retention of declarative and conceptual knowledge
  • Relational understanding: seeing how ideas connect, not just recalling them in isolation
  • Metacognitive calibration: the gap between what was retrieved and what the source contains makes knowledge gaps visible

Instructions¶

  1. Retrieve: Pose the topic and have students write or sketch everything they remember, unaided, for 3–5 minutes (Practice).
  2. Organize: Students sort their dumped items into a graphic organizer — timeline, matrix, concept map, or hierarchy — selecting the structure that fits the content (Advance Organizers).
  3. Verify: Students check the organizer against source texts, adding missing items and correcting misplaced ones (Feedback).
  4. Reflect: Briefly prompt students to note what they could not recall and what they misorganized, so gaps guide the next study session.
  5. Space: Repeat the cycle in a later session on the same topic to gain the spacing benefit [+S].

Examples¶

  • After a Civil War chapter, students brain-dump, then sort their recalled items onto a timeline and a causes/effects organizer, then verify against the text (see Cult of Pedagogy's retrieval practice resources).
  • Retrieval Practice (Agarwal & Bain) — publishes classroom-tested "brain dump" and "two things" routines, including dump-then-map variants.
  • A biology class brain-dumps the circulatory system, then arranges items into a flow diagram of blood path; verification reveals students omitted the pulmonary loop, which becomes the focus of the next lesson.
  • Science class (middle school): after a unit on photosynthesis, students brain-dump everything they recall, arrange it into a flowchart linking sunlight, water, CO₂, glucose, and oxygen, then verify against their textbook and add missed inputs/outputs.
  • Agarwal & Bain's "Two Things" routine (retrievalpractice.org): brief brain-dump cycles followed by organization and feedback, designed for low-stakes classroom use across subjects.
  • CmapTools (IHMC) — free concept-mapping software usable for the organizer phase, allowing students to revise maps after verification.
