# Make-Believe: Problem-Based Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Make-believe problem-based learning places learners inside a simulated real-world situation — a role, a scenario, a stake — and asks them to solve a complex, open-ended problem collaboratively, as practitioners in that world would. The "make-believe" frame (a kidnapped teacher, a restaurant the class must launch, a climate summit) gives authentic problems a narrative container that makes them emotionally engaging and manageable within a classroom. Learners work alongside expert guidance, identify what they need to know, and develop solutions that are evaluated against real-world criteria rather than a single correct answer.

## Design Implications

Problem-based scenarios leverage [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md): learners constructing solutions to authentic problems outperform passive instruction on application and skill measures, though effects on basic knowledge acquisition are more mixed [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Scenario-based and case formats improve exam performance when problems are well-structured around target concepts [Case-based learning improves exam performance.](../claims/case-based-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. The make-believe frame raises task value — learners persist more when problems connect to real-world stakes they care about [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M] — and successful navigation of a challenging scenario builds self-efficacy that supports persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rich, plausible scenario with authentic stakes and a role for each learner ([Problem Presentation](../elements/problem-presentation.md) or [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
- Access to resources learners can consult as they identify knowledge gaps ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md), expert interviews, datasets)
- Ongoing facilitation: tutors question, probe, and model expert reasoning rather than lecture ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../elements/cognitive-apprenticeship.md))
- Collaborative structures with defined roles so group work distributes expertise ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md))
- A culminating product or performance evaluated with authentic criteria ([Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md), [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))

#### Constraints
- Minimally guided versions overload novices: without prior knowledge or strong scaffolding, learners flounder in unstructured search and learn less than with guided instruction [~S] — the expertise-reversal pattern means the approach that helps advanced learners can hurt beginners
- The narrative frame can crowd out the content: learners remember the story of the kidnapped teacher but not the forensic science, unless debriefs explicitly extract the target concepts
- Resource- and time-intensive; poorly designed scenarios collapse into unfocused play
- Group free-riding is common without individual accountability structures

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured PBL**: tutor-posed problems with scaffolded knowledge-gathering cycles (medical school model) — appropriate for novices
- **Open make-believe projects**: learner-driven scenarios with light facilitation — appropriate for learners with prior knowledge
- **Hybrid**: direct instruction on core concepts first, then a make-believe problem for application and transfer
- Scale varies from a single lesson (a one-hour mystery) to a semester-long simulation (running a mock company)

### Target Learners
- Learners with sufficient prior knowledge to navigate open-ended problems productively; novices need more scaffolding or prior direct instruction [~S]
- Middle school through higher education; the narrative frame is especially motivating for adolescents
- Learners building professional identities (pre-service teachers, medical, business students) who benefit from practicing roles before enacting them for real

### Target Learning Goals
- Problem-solving and diagnostic reasoning in ill-structured domains
- Collaboration and communication within authentic role structures
- Motivation and engagement through task value and authentic stakes [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Transfer: applying concepts to novel, messy situations rather than recalling them

### Instructions
1. **Design the scenario**: identify the big ideas of the unit, then find a real-world situation where those ideas matter; wrap them in a narrative with roles and stakes.
2. **Launch with a [Problem Presentation](../elements/problem-presentation.md)**: present the scenario dramatically and concretely — artifacts, news, a visitor — so learners perceive a genuine problem worth solving.
3. **Support knowledge identification**: have learners list what they know, need to know, and how they will find out; provide [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md) and expert access rather than answers.
4. **Facilitate collaborative solution development**: use [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and [Socratic Questioning](../elements/socratic-questioning.md) to probe reasoning; keep [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) structured with roles and individual accountability.
5. **Require [Application of Knowledge](../elements/application-of-knowledge.md)**: solutions must be artifacts or performances judged by real-world criteria (budget must balance, menu must be feasible).
6. **Debrief**: explicitly extract the target concepts from the story so learning outlives the make-believe frame; connect solutions back to the big ideas.

## Related Strategies
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — the same authentic-problem core without the narrative role-play frame; cases are typically past-tense, make-believe PBL is present-tense
- [Simulation](../elements/simulation.md) — a more systematized version where the make-believe world has explicit rules and feedback loops
- [Project-Based Learning](project-based-learning.md) — shares authentic products and sustained inquiry, but centers on creating an artifact rather than solving a posed problem
- [Role-Play](role-play.md) — the enactment technique that supplies the make-believe frame

## Examples
- **Problem-based medical education (McMaster, Maastricht)** — the canonical model: small groups diagnose simulated patients, identify learning needs, and research them between tutorials ([Hmelo-Silver, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:EDPR.0000034022.16470.f3)).
- **A high school class designs a restaurant** — budget, menu, and brand developed collaboratively, with math, economics, and writing embedded in the make-believe venture.
- **Forensics mystery unit** — learners arrive to find the teacher "kidnapped" and must collect fingerprints and analyze clues, embedding scientific method in a narrative.
- **Model UN / climate summit simulations** — learners take national roles and negotiate solutions to global warming, practicing argumentation under authentic constraints.
