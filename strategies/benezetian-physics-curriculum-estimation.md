# Sketch a Benezetian K–12 physics curriculum built on everyday quantities, scaling, and estimation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 1 study (1 theoretical), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Description
The authors' proposed science counterpart of the Benezet experiment (Table 2): build on Benezet's mathematics program, adding physical quantities 'related to everyday exper ience' such as density of rocks and power in climbing stairs, proportional reasoning and scaling from grade 4, dimensional analysis and hand simulation of planetary motion in grades 9–11, and exact calculations only in grade 12. Qualitative assessment would lean on estimation problems and flaw-finding.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Physical quantities must connect to everyday experience, and qualitative assessment uses estimation problems; high-school students could try to 'ﬁnd ﬂaws in perpetual-mot ion machines' (article, §6).
#### Constraints
- The article notes that, as far as its authors know, few research-based diagnostic tests exist for measuring science understanding in grades K–10 (§6).

### Target Learners
- K–12 students

### Target Learning Goals
- estimation and order-of-magnitude reasoning
- proportional reasoning and scaling
- conceptual understanding of physical quantities
