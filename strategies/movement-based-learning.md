# Movement Based Learning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Movement based learning embeds physical activity into instruction so that learners encode, rehearse, or demonstrate content through bodily action — acting out concepts, gesturing explanations, or performing brief exercise bouts around instruction. It ranges from gesture-enriched explanation to full embodied activities such as [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) and [Acting-Role-Play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md).

## Design Implications

Movement supports learning through two complementary routes: embodied encoding, where gesture and action create additional motoric memory traces that enrich retrieval [~M], and transient physiological arousal, where acute exercise before or shortly after encoding improves subsequent memory [Acute exercise timed near encoding improves memory performance.](../claims/acute-exercise-timing-memory.md) [+M]. Classroom physical activity also improves on-task attention, which indirectly supports learning [Classroom physical activity improves attention.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]. The movement must be meaningfully connected to the content; arbitrary movement that competes for attention can impair encoding [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A mapping between the movement and the target concept (e.g., arm angles representing fraction size), not movement for its own sake
- Brief, structured activity that fits within instructional time without extended transition costs
- Debriefing that connects the physical experience back to the abstract representation ([Application](../elements/application.md))

#### Constraints
- Movement that is semantically unrelated to content adds extraneous load and can reduce learning [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Exercise bouts that are too long or too intense immediately before fine-motor or heavy working-memory tasks can temporarily degrade performance [~M]
- Whole-class movement activities can escalate off-task behavior without clear behavioral routines [-W]
- Benefits of acute exercise on memory attenuate with long delays between exercise and encoding [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Gestural enrichment**: instructor or learner gestures during explanation of abstract concepts (math, science)
- **Enacted simulation**: learners physically model systems (orbiting planets, blood flow, sentence structure) via [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)
- **Activity breaks**: 3–10 minute moderate exercise bouts between instructional segments to restore attention
- **Movement-based response**: learners answer by moving (walking to corners, holding up body positions), combining formative assessment with action

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle school learners, who show the largest attention gains from classroom activity breaks [Classroom physical activity improves attention.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]
- Learners who struggle with purely verbal or symbolic representation, including many English learners
- Less necessary for learners who already encode the content efficiently through abstract symbols [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of spatial, dynamic, or relational content (geometry, physics, grammar)
- Procedural fluency through physically rehearsed sequences
- Attention regulation and readiness to learn, as a supporting condition for other goals

### Instructions
1. Identify the concept's inherent spatial or dynamic structure and design a movement that mirrors it.
2. Model the movement clearly, connecting each action to its meaning ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
3. Have learners perform the movement while verbalizing the concept, pairing action with language ([Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md)).
4. Fade the movement and check that understanding transfers to abstract representation ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).
5. Use short activity breaks between segments when attention wanes, keeping transitions under one minute.

## Examples
- **Kinesthetic math routines** (e.g., Math & Movement, https://mathandmovement.com) — learners hop on numbered floor mats to rehearse arithmetic facts, pairing motor action with number recognition.
- **Enacted sentence diagramming** — students physically form sentences, with each student representing a part of speech; stepping out of line makes syntactic errors visible.
- **GoNoodle (https://www.gonoodle.com)** — short structured movement breaks used in classrooms to restore attention between instructional segments.
