# Impressions First

## Description
Before diving into specifics, students record and share their initial impressions of a mentor text — what struck them, confused them, pleased them, or rang false. This activates prior knowledge and personal experience, connects the text to the reader's own interpretive frame, and gives the class a record of first readings to compare against later, more analytical ones. Because reading to learn is ultimately a solitary act, the strategy positions students' own responses — not the teacher's — as the starting point of interpretation.

## Design Implications

Impressions First is a form of [Activation](../principles/activation.md): eliciting existing knowledge and affective responses before instruction improves comprehension and engagement with new material [Activating prior knowledge improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. It also draws on reader-response theory, which treats meaning as constructed in the transaction between reader and text rather than extracted from it — making students' first reactions legitimate interpretive data rather than noise to be corrected. The strategy works best when impressions are *recorded* (so they can be revisited) and *shared* (so students encounter divergent readings), not merely felt.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Appropriate reading material that invites response (poetry, short fiction, narrative nonfiction, or an excerpt with interpretive tension)
- Time for individual reflection before group discussion, so impressions are formed independently rather than anchored by peers
- A classroom climate where tentative or unconventional responses are treated as acceptable ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) norms help establish this)
- A mechanism for capturing impressions in writing so they can be revisited after deeper study

#### Constraints
- Students with limited fluency or confidence may struggle to articulate impressions; sentence stems ("This reminds me of…", "I noticed…") reduce this barrier [~M]
- Unstructured sharing privileges confident voices; without facilitation structures (turns, pairs before whole class), the same students dominate [-M]
- Time-consuming if used with every text; best reserved for texts that anchor a unit or demonstrate a craft move
- If the teacher evaluates impressions for "correctness," students learn to suppress genuine responses, defeating the purpose [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Quick-write then pair-share**: 3 minutes of writing, 3 minutes of partner talk, then whole-class harvest
- **Favorite/least favorite sentence**: students select and defend a line, grounding impressions in textual evidence from the start
- **Impression revisits**: students reread their first impressions after analysis and write about what changed — making interpretive growth visible
- **Silent discussion or digital board** (e.g., Padlet, Perusall) for classes where verbal sharing is a barrier

### Target Learners
- All grade levels; especially valuable for adolescents and adults developing independent interpretive stances
- Reluctant readers, for whom personal connection is often the entry point to sustained engagement with text [~M]
- Advanced students benefit from encountering divergent peer readings, which destabilizes the assumption that there is one correct interpretation [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Personal and aesthetic response to text (reader-response goals)
- Interpretive reasoning: learning to ground impressions in textual evidence
- Metacognitive awareness of how reading changes with rereading and with new knowledge
- Preparation for analysis: first impressions supply the raw material that later craft analysis explains

### Instructions
1. Select a mentor text and read it aloud or have students read it cold, without pre-teaching vocabulary or context.
2. Ask students to record first impressions individually ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)) — quick-write, annotation, or a selected line with a note on why.
3. Have students share impressions in pairs or small groups before whole class ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)), harvesting responses without adjudicating them.
4. Name and cluster recurring impressions on the board; note genuine disagreements as questions worth investigating.
5. Proceed into analysis or craft study, returning to the recorded impressions as evidence of how understanding evolved.

## Examples
- A middle school class reads Naomi Shihab Nye's "Famous" cold; students quick-write one line that struck them and why, then pair-share before the teacher introduces the craft study of personification.
- A high school literature class records first impressions of the opening chapter of *The Great Gatsby* in a shared document, then revisits them after completing the novel, writing a short reflection on how and why their reading changed.
- In a writing workshop using mentor texts, students share their favorite and least favorite sentences from a published personal essay, generating the criteria the class later uses to revise their own drafts.
