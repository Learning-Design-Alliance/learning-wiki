# Encouraging Help-Seeking Behavior

## Description
Encouraging help-seeking behavior means deliberately building the norms, routines, and tools that make asking for assistance a normal, low-cost part of learning rather than a face-threatening act. Adaptive help-seeking — recognizing a difficulty, formulating a specific question, and directing it to an appropriate source (teacher, peer, or resource) — is itself a learnable self-regulated learning skill, not merely a personality trait. The strategy operates on two levels: shaping classroom culture so help-seeking is not stigmatized, and teaching the *how* of effective help-seeking (asking specific, instrumental questions rather than "just give me the answer").

## Design Implications

Help-seeking is a core component of [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md): students must monitor their own comprehension, recognize when they are stuck, and act on that recognition. Many students avoid seeking help because they interpret it as evidence of low ability, especially in classrooms where ability is perceived as fixed and publicly compared [~M]. Interventions that reframe help-seeking as a strategy of successful learners, and that provide private or anonymous channels for questions, measurably increase help-seeking and reduce avoidance. Effective help must also be *instrumental* (hints, explanations) rather than *executive* (answers), or it short-circuits learning [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit norms and teacher modeling that frame asking for help as competent behavior, not weakness
- Accessible channels: office hours, question queues, anonymous question tools, peer tutoring structures
- Instruction in *how* to ask: formulating specific questions, describing what was already tried
- Help resources that respond with hints and scaffolds rather than final answers

#### Constraints
- In competitive or ability-focused classroom climates, public help-seeking carries social cost and students avoid it [~M] — changing individual behavior without changing the climate fails
- Overly available, answer-revealing help (e.g., click-through hints in tutoring software) leads to "help abuse": students game the system and learn less [-S]
- Students with low prior knowledge often lack the vocabulary to formulate a specific question, so they ask vague questions or none at all [-M]
- Cultural factors: in some classroom cultures, asking an adult for help is perceived as disrespectful or as public failure; anonymous or peer channels may be required

#### Implementation Variability
- Anonymous question submission (question boxes, digital backchannels) for students unwilling to ask publicly
- Structured peer help: reciprocal tutoring roles, "ask three before me" routines
- Embedded adaptive help in software: hints on demand, sequenced from generic to specific
- Teacher-led normalization: sharing one's own struggles, celebrating good questions

### Target Learners
- Students transitioning to more demanding environments (middle school, first-year college) where help-seeking norms are unclear [~M]
- Low-achieving students, who avoid help most often precisely when they need it most [-M]
- Students in classrooms emphasizing mastery and improvement rather than relative performance [~M]
- Belonging-focused framing benefits students vulnerable to stereotype threat or marginalization [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for students at risk of feeling they do not belong.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulated learning: monitoring comprehension and acting on confusion
- Metacognitive skill: knowing *when* one needs help and *what* to ask
- Content mastery: removing obstacles before they compound
- Classroom community: normalizing interdependence and peer support

### Instructions
1. **Set norms explicitly.** Open the term by naming help-seeking as a strategy of strong learners; model it by thinking aloud through your own confusion ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines can surface confusion early).
2. **Teach the question format.** Require students to state what they tried and where they got stuck before receiving help — this produces instrumental rather than executive questions.
3. **Provide graduated help channels.** Offer peer discussion first, then teacher support, then embedded hints; align hint design with [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md) so help fades as competence grows [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]
4. **Protect face.** Use anonymous or private channels for initial questions; move public discussion of good questions to whole-class settings once normalized.
5. **Reward the behavior.** Acknowledge specific, well-formed questions publicly; never answer a question with visible irritation.
6. **Monitor and follow up.** Track who is *not* asking; proactively invite quiet or struggling students via [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) conversations.

## Related Strategies
- Formative feedback routines — feedback is a form of delivered help; its tone determines whether students seek more
- Peer tutoring structures — institutionalize help-seeking between students
- Mastery-oriented grading — removes the performance cost of admitting confusion

## Examples
- **Carnegie Learning's MATHia (Cognitive Tutor)** — implements an on-demand hint system with sequenced hints and a Help-Seeking tutor that coaches students on *when and how* to request help, based on Aleven and Koedinger's research (https://www.carnegielearning.com).
- **Anonymous question routines** — physical question boxes or digital backchannels (e.g., a course forum with anonymous posting) let students surface confusion without public exposure.
- **"Ask three before me"** — a classroom routine requiring students to consult peers and notes before the teacher, building peer help-seeking networks.
