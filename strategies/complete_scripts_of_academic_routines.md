# Complete Scripts of Academic Routines

## Description
Complete scripts of academic routines provide learners with full, usable language models for recurring academic discourse moves — presenting, disagreeing politely, summarizing a source, asking for clarification. Instead of leaving learners to infer the conventions of academic talk and writing, the instructor supplies sentence frames and complete exemplar passages ("The topic of my presentation is ______. In the first part, I give a few basic definitions. In the next section, I will explain ______."). Learners rehearse with the script, then progressively adapt or replace it with their own language.

## Design Implications

Academic discourse is a hidden curriculum: its routines are second nature to instructors but invisible to novices, especially multilingual learners and first-generation students. Explicit scripts convert tacit disciplinary conventions into learnable structures, reducing the working-memory burden of composing language and content simultaneously [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Scripts work best as temporary scaffolds that are faded as learners internalize the routine [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Identification of the target routine (e.g., oral presentation, seminar contribution, lab report discussion section) and its constituent moves
- Complete, authentic-sounding scripts or sentence frames — not just vocabulary lists
- Low-stakes rehearsal opportunities before performance ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- A plan for fading: from full script → partial frame → independent production

#### Constraints
- Over-reliance on scripts can produce formulaic, depersonalized language and block development of flexible voice [-M] — scripts must be faded, not permanent
- Generic scripts ignore disciplinary variation; the language of a science argument differs from a humanities one, so scripts must be discipline-specific [~M]
- If scripts are presented without modeling or rehearsal, learners may not recognize when or how to deploy them [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full scripts**: complete model passages learners study and adapt (strongest support)
- **Sentence frames**: partially completed structures with slots for learner content
- **Word banks + frames**: vocabulary options paired with structural frames
- **Contrastive pairs**: effective vs. ineffective versions of the same move, sharpening discrimination
- Scripts can be delivered orally ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling), on anchor charts, or as writing templates

### Target Learners
- Multilingual learners and English learners acquiring academic language alongside content [+M]
- First-generation and novice students unfamiliar with the norms of academic discussion and presentation [~M]
- Younger learners still developing formal register
- Less necessary for learners already fluent in academic register, for whom scripts may feel constraining [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Disciplinary literacy: mastering the discourse conventions of a field
- Communication skill: structuring presentations, arguments, and discussions
- Confidence and participation: lowering the barrier to speaking in academic settings
- Metadiscourse: learning to signpost, hedge, cite, and transition

### Instructions
1. Identify the routine and break it into its constituent discourse moves (e.g., presentation = orient → preview → elaborate → signal transition → conclude).
2. Write or select a complete script for each move, using authentic disciplinary language.
3. Model the script in use, narrating why each move occurs ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
4. Have learners rehearse with the full script in low-stakes settings ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
5. Fade support: replace the full script with sentence frames, then with a word bank, then remove support entirely [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].
6. Provide feedback targeted at the discourse moves, not just content [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

## Related Strategies
- Sentence frames and sentence starters — the partial-script variant of the same function
- Structured academic discussion — routines that scripts slot into
- Modeling expert performance — the oral demonstration of a script in use

## Examples
- **Zwiers' academic language scripts (USA)** — Jeff Zwiers' work on academic conversation prompts ("I agree with ___ because…", "What's your evidence?") supplies complete scripts for classroom dialogue across content areas.
- **SIOP Model** — sheltered instruction lesson designs routinely include posted sentence frames and word banks for content-area discussion and writing.
- **Presentation courses** — providing a full spoken template ("The topic of my presentation is… In the first part, I give a few basic definitions…") before a first oral presentation, then requiring students to present without it on the second attempt.
