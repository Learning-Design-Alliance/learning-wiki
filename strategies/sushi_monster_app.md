# Sushi Monster App

## Description
Sushi Monster is a free mathematics app (Scholastic) in which learners build addition and multiplication combinations to feed a hungry sushi monster. Plates of sushi bearing numbers are placed on a counter; the player selects the correct combination of plates to reach a target number, and correct combinations are "gobbled up" by the monster. Each round contains 14 target numbers, and the app offers 12 levels — 7 for addition and 5 for multiplication — providing structured [practice](../elements/practice.md) with immediate feedback.

## Design Implications

Sushi Monster is an example of game-based fluency practice: it embeds repeated arithmetic retrieval in a playful, goal-directed context with immediate outcome feedback. Digital game-based learning shows small-to-moderate positive effects on achievement relative to conventional instruction when the game content is tightly aligned to learning objectives [Clark, Tanner-Smith, & Killingsworth meta-analysis of digital game learning.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. The target-number structure requires learners to decompose numbers and consider multiple combinations, which supports number sense rather than rote recall alone [~W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to the Sushi Monster app on a compatible device (iOS)
- Sufficient fluency with basic number combinations to attempt target sums/products without frustration
- Teacher or parent framing that connects play to the underlying arithmetic ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) works best when paired with debriefing)

#### Constraints
- Limited to addition and multiplication — no subtraction, division, or conceptual/word-problem content
- Fluency practice does not by itself build conceptual understanding of operations; it should supplement, not replace, instruction on the meaning of addition and multiplication [-W]
- Timed, performance-framed play can induce anxiety or speed pressure for low-fluency learners, shifting attention from reasoning to speed [~M]
- No detailed progress reporting for teachers, limiting use as an assessment data source

#### Implementation Variability
- Use as a station in math rotations (10–15 min sessions) rather than extended play blocks
- Ask learners to verbalize or record the combinations they found, converting play into [self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) opportunities [+M]
- Pair with concrete manipulatives or number talks so the app's symbolic practice is grounded in representations

### Target Learners
- Elementary students (roughly ages 7–11) consolidating addition and multiplication facts
- Learners who benefit from low-stakes, game-framed repetition and immediate feedback
- Less suitable for students who have not yet been introduced to the operations conceptually, or older students needing more advanced content

### Target Learning Goals
- Arithmetic fluency: automatic retrieval of addition and multiplication combinations [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S] — automaticity frees working memory for higher-level problem solving
- Number decomposition: recognizing multiple ways to compose a target number
- Strategic thinking: planning plate selections under time and resource constraints

### Instructions
1. Introduce the game mechanics and the target-number goal before independent play ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
2. Have students play a level, selecting plate combinations to reach each target number ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
3. Use the app's immediate feedback (monster eats correct combinations) to surface errors in the moment ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md))
4. Debrief: ask students to share the combinations they used and compare strategies ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
5. Monitor level progression across the 12 levels to gauge fluency growth ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
