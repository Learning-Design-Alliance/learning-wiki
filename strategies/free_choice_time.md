# Free Choice Time

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Free choice time allocates a structured portion of learning in which learners select their own activity, topic, material, or working mode from a defined set of options. It operationalizes autonomy support: rather than prescribing every task, the instructor curates the choice space and provides guidance within it. The strategy is most effective when choices are meaningful (all options lead to legitimate learning), bounded (the option set is curated, not unlimited), and followed by reflection on what was chosen and why.

## Design Implications

Choice reliably increases intrinsic motivation and engagement, consistent with self-determination theory's prediction that autonomy is a basic psychological need [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Meta-analytic evidence finds that providing choice modestly boosts intrinsic motivation, perceived competence, and effort, with weaker and more variable effects on objective learning outcomes [~M] — choice is primarily a motivational lever, not a direct instructional one. Choice works best when options are task-relevant and when learners have the background knowledge to choose wisely; undifferentiated or purely cosmetic choice ("pick the color of your worksheet") shows little benefit [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated set of 2–5 genuinely viable options, each aligned to the same learning goal ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) are a common structure)
- Clear success criteria so that any chosen path can be evaluated fairly ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) rubrics that are option-agnostic)
- Enough instructor availability to confer with learners pursuing different activities ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routines)
- Time for learners to plan and reflect on their choices ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md))

#### Constraints
- Choice can impose decision-making load on novices who lack the knowledge to evaluate options; too many options or too much ambiguity reduces its motivational benefit and can degrade performance [~M]
- Learners unfamiliar with autonomy may default to the easiest option or flounder; choice works better after [Scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md) has built self-regulatory routines
- Choice effects shrink or reverse when options differ in difficulty and learners systematically avoid challenge [~M] — instructors must ensure all paths are comparably demanding
- In high-stakes or tightly sequenced domains (e.g., early literacy skills), free choice time supplements but cannot replace [Explicit Instruction](../principles/explicit-instruction.md)

#### Implementation Variability
- **Topic choice** — learners pick the subject of inquiry (research topic, case, reading)
- **Process choice** — learners pick how to work (alone, in pairs, with manipulatives, digitally)
- **Product choice** — learners pick the demonstration format (essay, podcast, model, presentation)
- **Choice boards / menus** — a grid of activities from which learners select a required number, common in differentiated elementary classrooms
- **Genius hour / 20% time** — a recurring block for fully self-directed projects on personally chosen questions

### Target Learners
- Learners with sufficient prior knowledge and self-regulation to evaluate options; for complete novices, choice should be narrowed or scaffolded [~M]
- Disengaged or low-motivation learners, for whom autonomy can restore a sense of ownership [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Younger learners benefit from smaller, concrete option sets; older learners and adults can handle broader choice including goal-setting

### Target Learning Goals
- Intrinsic motivation, engagement, and ownership of the learning process
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own choices
- Retention and depth on self-selected content, where personal interest sustains effort [~M]
- Not well suited to: tightly specified procedural skills or content every learner must cover identically

### Instructions
1. Define the learning goal and success criteria that all options share ([State Objectives](../elements/state-objectives.md))
2. Curate 2–5 options of comparable rigor and value; present them in a [Choice Board](../elements/choice-boards.md) or menu
3. Brief learners on what each option involves and, where needed, model how to choose well ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) the decision)
4. Confer briefly with learners about their choices and redirect unproductive ones ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
5. Have learners pursue their chosen activity with [Practice](../elements/practice.md) embedded in each option
6. Close with [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md) on what was chosen, why, and what it produced

## Examples
- **Responsive Classroom's Academic Choice** ([https://www.responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)) — elementary teachers offer a teacher-designed choice of tasks; students plan, work, and reflect, building autonomy within a predictable structure.
- **Montessori classrooms** ([https://amshq.org](https://amshq.org)) — extended work periods in which children select from prepared materials; the "prepared environment" is the curated choice space.
- **Genius Hour / 20% Time** — used in many K–12 and corporate settings (popularized by Google's 20% policy) to devote recurring time to self-chosen projects with a public product.
- A business ethics professor lets students select which published case to analyze ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)), then runs a shared debrief across cases so all learners still reach the common analytical goals.
