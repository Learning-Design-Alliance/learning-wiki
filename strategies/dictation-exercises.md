# Dictation Exercises

## Description
Dictation exercises ask learners to listen to spoken language and transcribe it accurately in writing. The task forces learners to segment the speech stream into words and phonemes, map sounds to spellings, and hold the utterance in working memory long enough to write it. Variants include partial dictation (filling gaps in a transcript), running dictation (memory-and-movement versions where text is memorized in one location and written in another), and phrase-by-phrase dictogloss, where learners reconstruct a gist-level text from notes.

## Design Implications

Dictation is a form of retrieval practice applied to the sound–spelling system: each word must be reconstructed from a degraded acoustic trace rather than copied [Spaced retrieval practice produces durable retention.](../claims/spaced-retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. Because transcription requires simultaneous listening, segmenting, and writing, the task is working-memory intensive; texts must be short and delivered in manageable chunks [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. For beginning readers, accurate transcription practice strengthens automatic word recognition, which in turn frees cognitive resources for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Audio delivered at a controlled pace, with repetition permitted (teacher-read, recorded, or text-to-speech)
- Texts matched to learners' decoding level — mostly known grapheme–phoneme correspondences, with a small number of new items as the learning target
- Immediate feedback against the source text, so errors are corrected while the acoustic trace is fresh ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md))
- Short utterances (a phrase to a sentence per repetition) to avoid overload [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Dictation of texts beyond learners' decoding level degrades into letter-by-letter copying with little phonological benefit [-M]
- Whole-sentence dictation without chunking overwhelms beginning writers; errors then reflect memory failure, not spelling knowledge [~S]
- As a high-stakes graded spelling test, dictation adds anxiety without adding practice value; errors should be treated as diagnostic information [-M]
- For advanced L2 learners, verbatim dictation has diminishing returns — meaning-focused listening tasks outperform transcription once decoding is automatic [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Partial dictation / gap-fill**: learners complete a transcript with target words missing; lowers memory load and focuses attention on the target feature
- **Dictogloss**: learners take notes on a text read at normal speed, then reconstruct it in small groups; shifts the goal from verbatim accuracy to grammatical reasoning and negotiation of meaning
- **Running dictation**: text posted on walls; learners memorize a chunk, run to a scribe, and dictate it; adds movement and memory demands
- **Self-dictation**: learners record themselves or use apps (e.g., [ReadTheory](https://www.readtheory.org)-style tools, or spaced-repetition apps like [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) with audio cloze cards) for independent practice

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers and emergent writers building phoneme–grapheme mappings [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Second-language learners developing listening decoding and orthographic knowledge
- Learners with dyslexia or processing-speed challenges benefit from partial dictation and extended time ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)); full-speed verbatim dictation is counterproductive for them [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Phonological awareness: segmenting the speech stream into words and sounds
- Orthographic knowledge: mapping sounds to conventional spellings
- Listening decoding accuracy in an L2
- Automaticity of high-frequency word spellings ([Automaticity](../elements/automaticity.md))

### Instructions
1. Select or write a short text (1–3 sentences for beginners) containing mostly known patterns plus the target feature.
2. Read or play the text once at natural speed for gist, then in [chunked](../principles/chunking.md) phrases with pauses for writing.
3. Have learners transcribe, using partial transcripts for lower-memory-load variants.
4. Reveal the source text and have learners self-correct immediately, marking the specific sound–spelling mismatch rather than just the wrong word ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md)).
5. Re-test misspelled items in a later session, spaced across days ([Spaced retrieval practice produces durable retention.](../claims/spaced-retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]).

## Examples
- **[Sounds-Write](https://www.sounds-write.co.uk)** — a UK phonics programme that uses dictation (word and sentence level) as a core activity in every unit, moving from sound-by-sound to whole-sentence transcription.
- **Dictogloss in ESL classrooms** — a standard technique (originated by Wajnryb, 1990) in which learners reconstruct a short text from notes, widely used for grammar-focused listening.
- **[Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net)** audio-cloze cards — learners hear a sentence with the target word deleted and type it, combining dictation with spaced retrieval scheduling.
