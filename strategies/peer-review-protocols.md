# Peer Review Protocols

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Peer review protocols are structured procedures through which learners evaluate one another's work against explicit criteria, exchange written or oral feedback, and often revise in response. The protocol — rubrics, sentence stems, calibrated exemplars, staged deadlines — is what distinguishes peer review from informal commenting: it specifies what reviewers look for, how judgments are justified, and what happens to the feedback.

## Design Implications

Peer review works through two channels: learners receive feedback from multiple sources, and reviewers deepen their own understanding by evaluating work against criteria [Peer assessment interventions improve learning outcomes on average, with larger effects when reviewers also receive training.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]. The reviewing act is often as valuable as receiving feedback — analyzing others' work builds evaluative judgment that transfers to self-assessment. Protocol quality is the main lever: unstructured "comment on your partner's draft" produces vague praise, while criterion-anchored review produces actionable feedback.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit evaluation criteria, ideally a rubric learners have practiced applying ([Rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md))
- Calibrated exemplars of varying quality so reviewers align their judgments before reviewing live work ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md)) [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Reviewer training or a low-stakes practice round before graded review
- A mechanism for learners to act on feedback — revision, response memo, or follow-up task

#### Constraints
- Checklist-style evaluation without justification produces shallow, criterion-ticking reviews with little learning benefit [Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective in online settings.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Reviewer accuracy is moderate at best; untrained peer grades deviate substantially from instructor grades, especially for high-quality work [Peer and teacher ratings correlate only moderately, with systematic bias by ability level.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~S] — do not use raw peer scores for high-stakes grading without moderation
- Anonymity is a double-edged tool: it increases candor but removes the dialogue that helps reviewers elaborate feedback [~W]
- Novice reviewers may not detect conceptual errors, only surface features; peer review of early drafts works better than review of polished work [~M]
- Social dynamics (friendship bias, fear of offending) degrade feedback quality when reviewers are identifiable and untrained [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Calibrated Peer Review**: reviewers first rate exemplars and receive feedback on their ratings before reviewing peers (used in the [Calibrated Peer Review](https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) system)
- **Staged review**: outline → draft → final, with different criteria at each stage
- **Two-stage exams / group review**: peers discuss and re-answer individually-missed items
- **Asynchronous online review**: platforms like [Peergrade](https://www.peergrade.io) or [Peerceptiv](https://peerceptiv.com) distribute submissions, enforce rubric-based review, and assess review quality
- **Reciprocal review with author response**: reviewers must justify ratings; authors must respond to each point before revising

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary students in writing-intensive or project-based courses [Peer assessment interventions improve achievement across education levels.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- Developing reviewers benefit most when they have enough domain knowledge to recognize quality but still find criteria effortful [~W]
- Less effective for complete novices who cannot distinguish good from flawed work without exemplar calibration [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluative judgment: internalizing quality criteria for a domain
- Revision and drafting processes in writing and design tasks [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Argumentation: critiquing claims, evidence, and reasoning in peers' work [Argumentation improves reasoning.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Self-regulation: using external feedback to plan revisions

### Instructions
1. Define 3–5 review criteria aligned to the assignment's learning goals; share them with authors *before* drafting ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
2. Show 2–3 anonymized exemplars at different quality levels; have learners rate and discuss them until judgments converge ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md))
3. Run a low-stakes practice review; give reviewers feedback on their feedback
4. Distribute work for review — 2–4 reviewers per submission, with a rubric and justification prompts ("cite the specific passage; explain what would make it stronger")
5. Require authors to triage feedback: accept, reject with reason, or ask a clarifying question ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
6. Build in revision time and, optionally, a second review round on the revision
7. Assess review quality, not just the reviewed product — rewarding useful feedback sustains reviewer effort

## Examples
- **Calibrated Peer Review (UCLA)** — discipline-agnostic web system where students calibrate on exemplars before reviewing peers; widely used in chemistry and writing courses.
- **Peerceptiv** — used in large university courses; algorithmically assigns reviews and grades both the work and the review quality.
- **Writing-across-the-curriculum peer editing** — typical protocol: writers post drafts, reviewers respond to a 4-question prompt (strongest point, weakest point, one specific suggestion, one question), authors revise.
