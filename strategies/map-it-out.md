# Map It Out!

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Before a family trip, children map the route, calculate mileage, estimate travel time, and track progress along the way. The strategy embeds measurement, estimation, and proportional reasoning inside an authentic activity with real stakes, so the mathematics serves a purpose the child cares about rather than existing as an exercise in itself.

## Design Implications

Authentic, purposeful tasks improve engagement and help learners see the value of the mathematics they are applying [Task-value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. Because the trip is a concrete, upcoming event, the activity functions as [Anchored Instruction](../patterns/anchored-instruction.md): the journey provides a meaningful context that anchors distance-rate-time reasoning, which is otherwise one of the most abstract topics in elementary mathematics. Tracking progress en route turns a one-shot calculation into repeated prediction-and-check cycles, which strengthens estimation and self-monitoring [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A map (paper or digital) covering the route, plus a ruler or scale bar for measurement
- Reliable information about distances, speed limits, or typical travel times
- A calculator or support with arithmetic for younger learners; a way to record predictions and compare them with actual outcomes

#### Constraints
- Requires access to a map and accurate route information; inaccurate sources undermine the prediction-check cycle [-W]
- Younger learners may be overwhelmed by multi-step calculations (distance × rate ÷ speed, unit conversion); unstructured, the task can exceed working memory capacity [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M] — break the trip into segments rather than computing the whole route at once
- If the adult completes the calculations, the child becomes a spectator and the mathematical benefit disappears [-M]
- Digital navigation apps that display answers directly can short-circuit estimation; use them only for the post-trip comparison, not during prediction

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-trip planning:** full version — route selection, mileage, time estimates, rest-stop scheduling
- **En route tracking:** simplified version — child checks odometer or road signs against predictions at checkpoints
- **Post-trip reflection:** compare estimates with actuals and discuss sources of error (traffic, detours, speed)
- **Scaffolded version:** provide pre-calculated distances so the learner focuses only on time estimation, or vice versa

### Target Learners
- Elementary students (roughly ages 7–12), with complexity scaled to grade level
- Learners who question the relevance of school mathematics benefit especially from seeing measurement and arithmetic produce a real, usable answer [Task-value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Learners with strong arithmetic fluency can be extended to scale, proportions, and fuel-cost calculations

### Target Learning Goals
- Map reading: interpreting scale, symbols, and direction
- Measurement and estimation: converting map distance to real distance, judging reasonableness of answers
- Distance-rate-time reasoning: connecting speed, duration, and distance in a meaningful context
- Self-regulated checking: comparing predictions with outcomes and revising strategies [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Show the child the starting point and destination on the map and let them trace candidate routes ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
2. Have the child measure route segments with a ruler and use the map scale to convert to real miles or kilometers; segment the trip to manage calculation load ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md)).
3. Ask the child to estimate travel time from average speed ("If we go about 60 miles each hour, how long for 180 miles?") and record the prediction before checking any source.
4. En route, have the child track progress at checkpoints — road signs, mile markers, or odometer readings — and update the estimate.
5. After the trip, compare predictions with actual outcomes and discuss why estimates were off ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)); feedback should target the estimation strategy, not just the answer [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

## Examples
- A family driving 300 miles to visit grandparents: the child measures the highway route on a road atlas, converts via the scale bar, estimates 5 hours at 60 mph, then tracks actual time and discusses the 45-minute discrepancy caused by a lunch stop.
- Classroom adaptation: students plan a fictional class trip using state highway maps, comparing two routes for distance and estimated time, then defend their recommendation — a structured version of the [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) approach applied to route choice.
- The [Jasper Woodbury Problem Solving series](https://peabody.vanderbilt.edu/research/iris/) (Cognition and Technology Group at Vanderbilt) embeds the same distance-rate-time reasoning in video-based trip-planning adventures, demonstrating the strategy's scalability to anchored instruction.
