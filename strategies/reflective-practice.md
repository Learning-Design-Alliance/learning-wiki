# Reflective Practice

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Reflective practice is the deliberate, structured examination of one's own experience — what happened, why it happened, what one thought and felt, and what to do differently — carried out through writing, dialogue, or guided questioning. It converts raw experience into transferable insight by forcing articulation of tacit reasoning rather than letting experience pass unexamined.

## Design Implications

Reflection works when it is structured and prompted, not when it is left to chance; unguided "think about your learning" prompts produce little change [Reflective practice improves professional capability only when structured with prompts or frameworks.](../claims/reflective-practice-improves-outcomes-when-structured.md) [+M]. Effective designs give learners a framework (e.g., Gibbs' cycle, "What? So what? Now what?"), time to write or discuss, and a follow-through step that connects insight to future action. Reflection is most powerful when it occurs close to the experience and when the learner articulates reasoning aloud or in writing ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).

### Context
#### Requirements
- A concrete, recent experience to reflect on (a task, case, performance, or [Practice](../elements/practice.md) episode)
- A guiding framework or prompt sequence, not an open invitation to "reflect"
- A mechanism for capturing reflection — journal, discussion, portfolio entry ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) or structured writing)
- A loop back into action: revised goals, plans, or next attempts

#### Constraints
- Unstructured reflection can devolve into venting or vague narration with no learning gain [-M]
- Reflection without feedback or external perspective risks entrenching misconceptions — learners may confidently draw wrong lessons from experience [Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, where to next?" improves learning.](../claims/feedback-improves-learning-when-it-addresses-task-goals.md) [~M]
- Overly long or frequent reflective writing tasks produce compliance writing and fatigue [-W]
- Novices may lack the knowledge to diagnose what went wrong; reflection on unfamiliar domains can mislead [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written journals or learning logs** — individual, asynchronous, deep but low-feedback
- **Structured dialogue** — [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) rounds, [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), or peer debriefs that add external perspective
- **Post-performance debriefs** — short, immediate, focused on specific decisions (standard in simulation and case-based teaching)
- **Portfolio reflection** — cumulative, connecting multiple experiences over time

### Target Learners
- Learners with enough experience to have something substantive to analyze — intermediate performers benefit most [~M]
- Professionals and pre-service practitioners in clinical, teaching, and other practice-based fields [Reflective practice is widely used and valued in professional education, though evidence for isolated reflection activities is mixed.](../claims/reflective-practice-evidence-mixed-in-professional-education.md) [~W]
- Novices need more scaffolding: prompts, exemplars of good reflection, and instructor feedback on the reflection itself

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition: recognizing one's own reasoning patterns, assumptions, and errors
- Transfer: extracting generalizable principles from specific experiences
- Self-regulation: planning, monitoring, and adjusting one's own learning [Self-regulated learning strategies improve academic performance.](../claims/self-regulated-learning-strategies-improve-performance.md) [+S]
- Professional identity and judgment formation

### Instructions
1. Anchor the reflection in a specific recent experience — a completed task, [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md) session, or performance.
2. Provide a framework with sequenced prompts: describe what happened → analyze why → plan what to change (Gibbs' cycle or "What? So what? Now what?").
3. Have learners articulate their reasoning in writing or speech ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)), not just rate their feelings.
4. Add an external perspective — peer dialogue, instructor response, or comparison against expert reasoning — so reflection is checked, not just emitted.
5. Close the loop: require one concrete commitment for the next attempt, then revisit it at the next [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md).

## Examples
- **Simulation debriefing in health education** — after a mannequin scenario, facilitators run a structured debrief (advocacy-inquiry style) in which learners analyze their own decisions; debriefing quality is a stronger predictor of learning than scenario fidelity.
- **Gibbs' Reflective Cycle in teacher education** — student teachers write cycle-structured analyses of lessons they taught, then revise their lesson plans accordingly.
- **Design studio crits** — architecture and design programs institutionalize reflection through public critique of work in progress, combining self-explanation with expert and peer feedback.
