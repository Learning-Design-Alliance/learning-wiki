# Drawing for Memory and Comprehension

## Description
Drawing involves visually, kinesthetically, and semantically processing information, which forces learners to grapple with what they’re learning and reconstruct it in a way that makes sense to them. It is an active learning strategy, not passive. Drawing encodes memory by layering together the visual memory of the image, the kinesthetic memory of drawing, and the semantic memory invoked during meaning-making. It increases the likelihood that the concept being drawn will later be recalled.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Requires drawing materials such as paper, crayons, pencils, or digital drawing tools. Requires time for students to create drawings and reflect on their meaning.
#### Constraints
- May not be suitable for all learning styles or preferences. Some learners may struggle with drawing or feel self-conscious about their artistic abilities. Effectiveness depends on learners’ engagement with the process and willingness to grapple with the material.

### Affordances
- Boosts memory by challenging students to explore an idea in different ways. Enriches learning by creating student-generated learning aids. Fosters creativity and expression through interactive notebooks. Aids data visualization by helping students collect, analyze, and present data in visual form.

### Personalization
- Students can choose the specific content they want to represent through drawing. Flexible formats allow for a range of drawing styles and techniques. Learners can create drawings, diagrams, charts, or comic books.

## Assessment Evidence
- Measure improvements in recall and comprehension through pre- and post-drawing assessments. Evaluate the depth and complexity of students’ drawings and explanations. Observe students’ engagement and enthusiasm during drawing activities.

## Impact
- Research shows that drawing information can boost memory by nearly double compared to other activities such as reading or writing. Drawing requires elaboration on the meaning of the term and translating the definition to a new form (a picture).

## Examples
- Teachers can have students create their own learning aids such as maps, anchor charts, or diagrams. Interactive notebooks can be used for written notes on one side and drawings, diagrams, and charts on the other. Students can collect, analyze, and present data in visual form to deepen their understanding of a topic. Students can also create comic books to tell stories or describe events.
