# Sentence Stems for Reflection

## Description
Sentence stems are partially completed first-person sentences ("I selected this piece because…", "What really surprised me was…") that learners complete to structure a reflective response. They function as a scaffold for [metacognition](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md): the stem names the reflective move (evaluating, comparing, goal-setting) while the learner supplies the content. Stems can be used in teacher–student conferences, written reflection journals, portfolio defenses, or exit tickets.

## Design Implications

Sentence stems reduce the blank-page problem by externalizing the structure of a reflective response, lowering the working-memory and language demands of self-assessment [chunking reduces working memory load](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. They are most effective when stems target specific reflective operations — evaluating quality, comparing across work, identifying a goal — rather than generic prompts, and when they are faded as learners internalize the reflective routine [contingent scaffolding improves learning](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Stems matched to the specific task and the reflective operation desired (evaluation, comparison, goal-setting)
- A low-stakes channel for the response: conference, journal, portfolio statement, or exit ticket
- Occasional modeling of a completed reflection by the teacher, so learners hear what a strong response sounds like ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
- A plan for fading: shifting from provided stems to learner-generated questions

#### Constraints
- Overused or overly narrow stems can produce formulaic, compliance-oriented reflections rather than genuine self-assessment [-M] — learners fill in the blank rather than think
- Stems impose a reflective frame; learners with strong reflective habits may find them constraining [~W]
- If stems are never faded, learners may remain dependent on external structure and fail to self-initiate reflection [-W]
- Written stems disadvantage learners with weak writing fluency unless paired with oral alternatives [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Choice boards**: offer 5–8 stems and let learners pick two or three, preserving autonomy [autonomy supports intrinsic motivation](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- **Task-specific stems**: "One strategy I tried that didn't work was…" for problem solving vs. "My claim is strongest because…" for argumentation
- **Progressive stems**: early stems describe ("What I did was…"), later stems evaluate and plan ("Next time I will…")
- **Oral stems**: teacher uses the same stems in conferences, modeling the reflection before requiring written versions

### Target Learners
- Emerging writers and English language learners, for whom the stem supplies syntax and reduces language load [+M]
- Novices who do not yet know what "good reflection" looks like; stems make the reflective moves visible
- Less necessary for learners with well-developed self-regulatory routines, who may find stems redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-assessment: evaluating one's own work against criteria [assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Metacognitive monitoring and goal-setting within [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- Portfolio curation and justification of learning artifacts

### Instructions
1. Identify the reflective operation you want (evaluate, compare, set a goal) and write 3–6 stems that name it explicitly.
2. Model completing one stem aloud with your own work, making the reasoning visible ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
3. Have learners complete stems individually ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md)) or in a conference ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
4. Have learners share or submit reflections and respond with feedback tied to their stated goals ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
5. Fade: replace provided stems with learner-generated reflective questions over successive cycles.

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) — the stem holds the sentence frame so working memory is spent on the reflective content, not on composing structure
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — stems operationalize self-assessment by giving learners concrete language for judging their work against criteria
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) — stems cue the forethought and self-evaluation phases (planning, monitoring, revising) that learners otherwise skip

### Personalization
- Teachers can write stems specific to the task or discipline; offering a menu of stems lets learners choose the frames they find most useful, and learners can eventually author their own.

## Related Strategies
- [Exit Tickets](exit-tickets.md) — a common delivery vehicle for stem-based reflection at the end of a lesson
- [Portfolio Assessment](portfolio-assessment.md) — stems structure the written justification that accompanies selected work
- [Writing Conferences](writing-conferences.md) — stems give the conference a reflective starting point

## Examples
- A writing teacher posts stems ("What makes this piece strong is my use of…", "What I want to work on for a reader is…") on a choice board; students select two to accompany a portfolio submission, then defend their choices in a conference.
- A mathematics teacher ends a problem-solving lesson with "One strategy I tried that didn't work was… because…" on an exit ticket, then opens the next class by discussing two anonymized responses.
- [EL Education](https://eleducation.org) portfolio protocols use structured reflection prompts and stems when students present their work during student-led conferences and passage presentations.
