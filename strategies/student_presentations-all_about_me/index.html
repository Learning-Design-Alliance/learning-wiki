Student Presentations All About Me¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Students create and deliver a short presentation about themselves — interests, background, strengths, goals — typically at the start of a course or school year. The task doubles as low-stakes oral communication practice and as a community-building ritual: peers learn who is in the room while the presenter practices organizing and delivering content to an authentic audience.

Design Implications¶

The strategy works because the audience and purpose are genuinely authentic — peers who will collaborate with the presenter all term — and authenticity raises the quality of student work Authentic audiences improve student work. [+M]. Personal-content topics eliminate the knowledge barrier of subject-matter presentations, letting learners focus entirely on structure, delivery, and audience awareness. The activity also functions as a belonging intervention: being seen and known by peers and instructor supports persistence, particularly for students at risk of feeling marginal Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A clear, simple structure for the presentation (e.g., three sections: background, interests, goals) so novices are not designing open-ended talks
  • Modeling: the instructor delivers their own "All About Me" first, making structure and delivery visible
  • A listener role for the audience — structured question time or a follow-up connection task — so peers engage actively rather than passively
  • Low-stakes framing and generous time limits (2–4 minutes) to manage presentation anxiety

Constraints¶

  • Public speaking is a genuine fear for a substantial minority of students; forcing immediate full-class delivery without rehearsal or choice of format can produce avoidance rather than learning [-M]
  • Can devolve into a sequence of passive listening if the audience has no task; attention and retention drop without an active listener role [~M]
  • Students with limited majority-culture background knowledge or who are new to the setting may struggle to select "relatable" content; overly personal prompts can feel exposing rather than connecting [-W]
  • Little subject-matter learning occurs — the strategy targets communication and community goals, not content objectives [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Format choice: live talk, recorded video, slide deck with voiceover, or poster walk — choice of modality supports autonomy and reduces threat Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S]
  • Small-group delivery (triads or class discussion circles) instead of whole-class, lowering stakes while preserving audience
  • Iteration: a second, revised presentation later in the term makes growth visible and turns the activity into practice rather than a one-off event
  • Subject-matter hybrid: "All About Me as a Learner/Scientist/Writer" connects the personal frame to disciplinary identity

Target Learners¶

  • New cohorts, advisee groups, or online classes where members are strangers
  • Adolescents and adults; younger learners benefit from highly structured templates and show-and-tell variants
  • Anxious presenters benefit from rehearsal, small audiences, and format choice; whole-class cold delivery is the least supportive entry point [-M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Oral communication: organizing ideas, pacing, eye contact, audience awareness
  • Community and belonging: peer-to-peer knowledge and interpersonal connection Belonging interventions improve outcomes. [+M]
  • Self-knowledge and reflection: articulating strengths, interests, and goals
  • Metacognition: planning and self-evaluating a performance task

Instructions¶

  1. Model the task: instructor delivers a 2–3 minute "All About Me," naming the structure aloud so the format is visible.
  2. Provide a template and rubric with 3–4 criteria (structure, delivery, connection to audience) — keep it short and readable.
  3. Give structured prep time with a rehearsal step; pair students for a low-stakes practice run before delivery.
  4. Offer format choice (live, recorded, slides) to support autonomy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+S].
  5. During presentations, give listeners a task: each audience member writes one question or one connection to share afterward, keeping the room active Active learning improves exam performance. [+S].
  6. Close with brief peer feedback against the rubric and an instructor check-in with anxious presenters.

Related Strategies¶

  • Icebreaker and community-building openers — the "All About Me" is a substantive, content-producing alternative to game-style icebreakers
  • Peer feedback protocols — structures the audience response that makes presentations formative rather than performative

Examples¶

  • First-week seminar practice: a college first-year seminar opens with instructor-modeled "All About Me" talks, then student talks in groups of four, with each listener submitting one connection card used later to form project teams.
  • Flipgrid (now Flip) — students record short video introductions asynchronously; recording removes the live-audience threat while preserving the authentic peer audience.
  • EL Education crew routines — advisory "crew" meetings open the year with personal presentations that establish norms of being known (https://eleducation.org).
