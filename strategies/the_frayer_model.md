# The Frayer Model

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The Frayer Model is a four-quadrant graphic organizer used to teach a concept rather than an isolated fact. Learners complete quadrants for **Definition** (a precise statement of the concept's category and boundaries), **Essential Characteristics** (attributes every instance must have), **Examples**, and **Non-Examples** (near-misses that share surface features but fail the criteria). Developed by Frayer, Frederick, and Klausmeier (1969) at the Wisconsin Research and Development Center, it operationalizes a "concept of definition" procedure in which word knowledge means knowing boundaries, not just meanings.

## Design Implications

The model forces learners to engage in the two operations that concept-learning research identifies as central: identifying the defining attributes that separate instances from non-instances, and comparing contrasting cases to abstract the underlying rule [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying principle.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]. The non-examples quadrant is the load-bearing wall: without near-miss non-examples, learners form overgeneralized concepts that include irrelevant surface features. Because the organizer externalizes the concept's structure, it reduces the working-memory burden of holding attributes, instances, and boundaries in mind simultaneously [Chunking reduces working-memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness increases when learners generate their own entries rather than copying teacher-provided ones [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A concept with genuine definitional structure — a category with criteria, not a proper noun or an arbitrary label
- A teacher who has clarified the concept's boundaries first; the model exposes fuzzy teacher understanding immediately
- At least two examples and two *near-miss* non-examples that differ on a defining attribute, not on surface features
- Time for learners to generate and defend entries, ideally through [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) of disputed cases

#### Constraints
- Ineffective for concepts without clear defining attributes (e.g., "democracy," "energy" in early grades) — forcing a definition produces pseudo-precision [-W]
- Non-examples chosen for surface similarity are essential; using obviously different non-examples makes the task trivial and produces no discrimination learning [~M]
- Copying teacher-completed models yields shallow encoding; the generative effort is where learning occurs [-M]
- One model per concept is slow; it does not scale to high-volume vocabulary demands, where it should be reserved for high-utility tier-two words or core disciplinary concepts [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Blank vs. partially completed**: give novices the definition and one example, letting them derive the rest; fade support as expertise grows
- **Student-generated non-examples**: asking learners to invent near-misses is a harder, more generative variant
- **Disciplinary adaptations**: in mathematics, quadrants become "what it is / what it is not / examples / counterexamples"; in science, characteristics split into necessary vs. sufficient
- **Digital versions**: shared slides or whiteboard tools allow collaborative completion and comparison across groups

### Target Learners
- Novices encountering a new disciplinary concept for the first time, who otherwise anchor on surface features [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of the underlying principle.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
- English language learners, when paired with [accessible vocabulary and syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) and visual examples — the structure makes implicit expectations explicit
- Less beneficial for advanced learners who already hold well-differentiated concepts; the organizer becomes redundant busywork [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Concept formation: learning what a term includes, excludes, and why
- Discrimination learning: distinguishing superficially similar concepts (e.g., *mass* vs. *weight*, *prime* vs. *odd*)
- Vocabulary depth: moving from definitional knowledge to flexible, criterion-based word use

### Instructions
1. Select a concept-critical term and verify its defining attributes yourself before teaching.
2. Present the term and activate related knowledge ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)); consider an [analogy](../elements/analogies.md) to a familiar concept.
3. Co-construct the definition, naming the superordinate category and the concept's boundaries.
4. Have learners list essential characteristics — attributes every instance must have.
5. Provide or elicit examples, then deliberately introduce a near-miss non-example to trigger [cognitive conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) and force criterion-based reasoning.
6. Have learners generate their own examples and non-examples and justify them against the essential characteristics [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].
7. Revisit and revise the completed model after subsequent instruction; treat it as a living artifact, not a one-time worksheet.

## Examples
- **Mathematics**: a Frayer Model for *prime number* — definition ("a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two factors"), characteristics (exactly two factors, greater than 1), examples (2, 7, 13), non-examples (1, which fails the "greater than 1" criterion; 9, which has three factors). The non-example *1* is the critical case that corrects the most common misconception.
- **Science**: *mammal* with the non-example *dolphin-as-fish* reasoning surfaced, forcing learners to use reproduction and warm-bloodedness rather than habitat as criteria.
- **Literacy/vocabulary programs**: widely embedded in tiered vocabulary routines such as those in Marzano's six-step academic vocabulary process (Marzano & Pickering, 2005), where the Frayer Model serves as the "represent and deepen" step.
