Graphic Organizers for Note-Taking¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 unmarked) · 12 studies (5 review, 4 causal, 3 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 2 of 12 report an effect size

Description¶

Learners use graphic organizers — matrices, concept maps, Venn diagrams, flowcharts, timelines — to structure notes taken from readings, research, films, or podcasts. The instructor may pre-select an organizer matched to the content structure (e.g., a comparison matrix for contrasting theories) to promote learning efficiency, or learners may construct their own.

Design Implications¶

Graphic organizers externalize relationships among ideas, reducing the working-memory burden of holding unstructured notes while processing new input Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Combining verbal notes with spatial-visual structure recruits dual channels, improving recall over text-only notes Dual coding improves recall. [+M]. Meta-analytic evidence shows graphic organizers used as note-taking or study aids improve learning outcomes, with the largest gains when organizers make explicit the relations the learner must infer Graphic organizers improve learning across content areas. [+M]. Critically, the generative act of selecting, relating, and arranging information — not the visual format itself — drives the benefit; simply handing learners a completed organizer is far less effective than having them complete one Learner-generated organizers outperform provided ones. [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • An organizer whose structure matches the content's logical structure (comparison → matrix; sequence → flowchart; hierarchy → tree)
  • Brief training in how to use the organizer type; learners unfamiliar with a format spend effort decoding it rather than processing content
  • Source material rich enough to populate the organizer meaningfully
  • Instructor modeling of how to complete the organizer (Think-Aloud or a partially completed example)

Constraints¶

  • A poorly matched organizer forces content into an alien structure and degrades learning [-M] — e.g., a linear timeline for a multi-causal argument
  • Pre-completed organizers encourage passive receipt rather than generative processing [-S]; learners who receive finished organizers often study them shallowly
  • Organizers can over-simplify complex, contested, or highly interconnected material, implying false clarity or a single "correct" structure [-M]
  • Without training, the format itself becomes a cognitive load rather than a scaffold [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Instructor-provided blank organizers — most efficient for novices; reduces format-decision load
  • Learner-constructed organizers — more generative; better for learners with sufficient prior knowledge to judge what matters [~M]
  • Partially completed ("skeleton") organizers — a Fading middle step; provide some cells, learners complete the rest
  • Collaborative completion — pairs negotiate structure, adding Collaborative Learning benefits
  • Post-hoc re-organization — learners take linear notes first, then restructure into an organizer as a second-pass synthesis activity

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who lack the prior knowledge to impose structure on incoming information on their own Graphic organizers improve learning across content areas. [+M]
  • Learners with weak self-regulated note-taking skills who default to verbatim transcription
  • Less beneficial for experts, who already possess schemas and may find imposed structures redundant or constraining [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension and retention of structured expository content Dual coding improves recall. [+M]
  • Identifying relationships: comparisons, hierarchies, cause–effect chains, sequences
  • Selective attention — deciding what is note-worthy rather than transcribing everything
  • Synthesis across sources (research notes, multi-source projects)

Instructions¶

  1. Activate prior knowledge about the topic and preview the organizer's structure so learners know what they are listening/reading for.
  2. Select or co-design an organizer matched to the content's structure; model completing one section with a Think-Aloud.
  3. Have learners complete the organizer during or immediately after engaging with the material, using Annotating conventions (abbreviations, symbols) to keep pace.
  4. Debrief: learners compare organizers with a partner, resolving discrepancies — this comparison is where much of the learning occurs.
  5. Use the completed organizer as a retrieval-practice or writing scaffold, not as an end product.

Related Strategies¶

  • Advance Organizers — organizers presented before instruction to prime structure; note-taking organizers operate during and after
  • Annotating — the text-marking counterpart for working directly with source documents
  • Chunking — organizers group information into processable units
  • Elaborative Interrogation — asking "why" and "how" questions while filling organizer cells deepens processing
  • Retrieval Practice With Graphic Organizers

Examples¶

  • Students complete a comparison matrix on two historical figures while reading paired biographies, then use it to draft a compare–contrast essay (see Cult of Pedagogy's graphic organizer collection).
  • Newsela and ReadWorks pair leveled articles with downloadable organizers (cause–effect charts, main-idea webs) aligned to each text.
  • Khan Academy unit notes and many AP course frameworks provide concept-map templates students populate while watching videos.
  • In a research-methods course, students use a source-evaluation grid (claim / evidence / method / limitation) to take notes across multiple papers, then synthesize across rows.
