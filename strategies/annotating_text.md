# Annotating Text

## Description
Annotating text is a reading strategy in which learners actively mark up a document while reading — highlighting key ideas, writing margin notes, coding passages with symbols, posing questions, and recording connections. It converts passive reading into a generative activity: the reader must select what matters, summarize it in their own words, and respond to it. Annotation can be done on paper, in shared documents, or with digital tools such as [Perusall](https://www.perusall.com) and [Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is).

## Design Implications

Annotation works because it forces generative processing — selecting, organizing, and integrating information — rather than passive re-reading [Generative activities that require learners to construct meaning improve retention and comprehension.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. However, the *quality* of annotation matters more than the act itself: unselective highlighting is one of the least effective study techniques learners commonly use [Highlighting and rereading produce weak learning gains compared with generative strategies.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]. Effective designs teach learners *what* to annotate and require elaborative notes (questions, summaries, connections), not just marking.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text worth engaging with closely (dense, concept-laden, or argumentative)
- Explicit instruction in annotation moves: what to mark, how to code, what to write in margins
- A purpose or task that the annotation serves (discussion prep, writing, problem-solving)
- For social annotation: a platform and norms for responding to peers' notes

#### Constraints
- Highlighting alone, without elaborative notes, adds little beyond re-reading and can create fluency illusions [Highlighting and rereading produce weak learning gains compared with generative strategies.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [-S]
- Over-marking eliminates the selection benefit — if everything is highlighted, nothing is prioritized; visual salience effects are unreliable guides to importance [Marking text based on salience does not reliably improve learning of the marked content.](../theories/von-restorff-effect-text-marking.md) [~W]
- Novices often highlight details rather than main ideas, because they cannot yet distinguish them
- Heavy annotation demands can overload working memory for difficult texts or struggling readers [Cognitive overload from excessive concurrent demands degrades comprehension.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured codes**: symbols for claim/evidence/question/confusion (e.g., "Say Something" or SOAPStone protocols)
- **Question-annotating**: learners write questions in margins rather than statements, priming discussion
- **Social annotation**: shared markup where learners see and reply to peers' annotations (Perusall, Hypothesis)
- **Teacher-modeled annotation**: instructor annotates a short passage live, thinking aloud, before learners annotate independently
- **Annotation as assessment**: graded annotation quality replaces reading quizzes

### Target Learners
- Adolescent and adult readers with decoding fluency, who can devote working memory to comprehension strategies [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension strategies.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Students preparing for discussion, writing, or exams who need a durable record of engagement
- Less suitable as an untaught expectation: learners must be trained in annotation moves before independent use

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension and close reading of complex texts
- Self-regulated learning: monitoring understanding and flagging confusion
- Preparation for discussion, argumentation, and evidence-based writing

### Instructions
1. Model the strategy: annotate a short passage on a shared screen, thinking aloud about *why* each mark is made (see [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) if available; otherwise narrate decisions explicitly).
2. Provide a small set of annotation codes (e.g., ✓ main idea, ? question, ! surprise, → connection) and keep them under five to limit load [Chunking procedures into a small set of categories reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].
3. Have learners annotate a shared text independently, then compare annotations in pairs — contrasting different markings sharpens discrimination of main ideas [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M].
4. Make annotation consequential: use it as the entry point for discussion, writing, or problem-solving, so learners read with a purpose.
5. Fade structure over time, shifting from prescribed codes to learner-chosen annotation goals as strategies become habitual.

## Related Strategies
- **Close Reading protocols** — annotation is the core move in most close-reading routines
- **Reciprocal Teaching** — shares the predict/question/clarify/summarize moves that annotation externalizes
- **Marginalia discussion** — social annotation as the discussion medium itself

## Examples
- **[Perusall](https://www.perusall.com)** — social annotation platform where students annotate course readings collaboratively and the system scores annotation quality; widely used in higher-ed STEM courses.
- **[Hypothesis](https://web.hypothes.is)** — open web annotation used in LMS-integrated reading assignments.
- **AP/Dual-enrollment English classrooms** — structured annotation of rhetoric (SOAPStone: Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone) as preparation for argument analysis essays.
