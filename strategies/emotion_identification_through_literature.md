# Emotion Identification through Literature

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy uses emotion posters — visual displays of emotion categories with facial expressions, labels, and example language — as a reference tool during read-alouds. The teacher pauses at emotionally charged moments and asks students to infer the character's feelings using both textual evidence (word choice, dialogue, actions) and visual cues (illustrations), then name the emotion using the poster's vocabulary.

## Design Implications

Naming emotions builds the vocabulary needed for later emotional regulation and empathy; emotion knowledge in early childhood predicts later social competence [~M]. Literature is an effective vehicle because characters' internal states must be inferred from partial cues — the same inferential work required for real-world social understanding — and reading narrative fiction is associated with improved theory-of-mind performance [~M]. The strategy works best when the teacher makes the inference process explicit rather than simply labeling the emotion for students [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text with strong, inferable emotional content and supporting illustrations
- Emotion posters displaying a manageable set of emotion categories with faces and labels
- Teacher facilitation that requires students to cite textual or visual evidence for their inference ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [Conceptual Questioning](../elements/conceptual-questioning.md))
- Repeated exposure across multiple read-alouds so vocabulary is revisited and consolidated

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops when the teacher labels emotions for students instead of requiring evidence-based inference — passive labeling produces recognition without transfer [-M]
- Students with limited emotion vocabulary or alexithymia-like difficulties may guess from facial expression alone and ignore textual context; the poster can anchor them to a single cue [-W]
- Poorly chosen texts (weak emotional content, ambiguous illustrations) leave students with insufficient evidence, producing frustration rather than inference practice [-W]
- Effects on general empathy are small and depend on sustained, repeated use rather than single sessions [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Whole-class read-aloud with poster reference, small-group guided reading, or independent reading with a personal emotion bookmark
- Posters can range from six basic emotions (early elementary) to nuanced gradations (frustrated vs. angry vs. disappointed) for older students
- Extend into role-play: students act out the character's emotion ([Act It Out](act_it_out.md)) or play a feelings check-in game (['What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In](whats_my_emotion_game_check-in.md))

### Target Learners
- Early elementary students (K–3) still building basic emotion vocabulary and facial-expression recognition
- English language learners, who benefit from the paired visual-verbal labels on posters
- Students with weak inferential comprehension — emotion inference is a low-stakes entry point to inferential reading [~M]
- Less beneficial for students who already name and discuss emotions fluently; for them the poster routine becomes redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotion recognition and labeling (social-emotional learning competency: self-awareness)
- Inferential comprehension: using text and image evidence to construct a character model
- Empathy development: adopting a character's perspective ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Vocabulary growth for abstract emotion words

### Instructions
1. Select a read-aloud text with clear emotional arcs and supportive illustrations ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)).
2. Preview the emotion posters with students, connecting each face and label to a personal experience ([Activating Prior Knowledge](activating_prior_knowledge.md)).
3. During the read-aloud, pause at emotionally significant moments and ask: "How does the character feel? What in the words or pictures tells you that?" ([Conceptual Questioning](../elements/conceptual-questioning.md)).
4. Have students point to or name the emotion on the poster, then justify the choice with specific evidence ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
5. Model the reasoning aloud when students struggle, narrating how word choice or an illustration signals a feeling ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
6. Contrast similar emotions on the poster (e.g., sad vs. disappointed) using multiple story moments as contrasting cases [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].
7. Close by asking students to connect the character's emotion to their own experience or predict what the character will do next.

## Examples
- **Second Step (Committee for Children)** — SEL curriculum that pairs emotion-face cards with story-based discussion, structurally similar to the poster-plus-read-aloud routine. https://www.secondstep.org
- **RULER Approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence)** — the "Mood Meter" functions as a scaffolded emotion-labeling tool integrated into classroom texts. https://ei.yale.edu/ruler
- **Kindergarten read-aloud of *Where the Wild Things Are* (Sendak)** — teacher pauses when Max is "sent to bed without eating anything" and asks students to locate his feeling on the emotion poster, citing the illustration's expression and the word "wild."
