# Shadowing (for behavior adjustment)

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Shadowing assigns a dedicated adult to stay within arm's reach of a child throughout the day, intervening early to prevent unsafe or disruptive behavior before it occurs. The adult uses the close proximity to coach replacement behaviors in the moment, teach coping strategies, and gather detailed observation data on the behavior's antecedents. Shadowing continues until the child is roughly two weeks incident-free, then fades.

## Design Implications

Shadowing is a prevention-first behavior support: by interrupting the antecedent–behavior–consequence chain at the antecedent stage, it prevents the reinforcement history of the problem behavior from being reactivated while replacement skills are taught [~M]. Its effectiveness depends on pairing proximity with active teaching — an adult who only blocks behavior manages risk but does not build skills, whereas in-the-moment coaching of alternatives produces durable behavior change [Coaching that includes modeling and feedback improves implementation of new practices.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Because the adult is present at the moment of escalation, feedback is immediate and task-focused, the condition under which feedback is most effective [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A dedicated adult who can remain with the child for extended periods without displacing other duties
- Knowledge of the child's typical triggers, escalation signs, and behavior patterns, ideally from a functional behavior assessment
- A plan for what the adult does in the moment: prompt replacement behavior, model coping strategies, reinforce positives
- Discretion — the child should not feel singled out or punished, and peers should not perceive the shadowing as surveillance
- A clear exit criterion (e.g., two weeks incident-free) and a fading plan

#### Constraints
- Labor-intensive: requires an adult resource most classrooms do not have, limiting scalability [-W]
- Proximity alone suppresses behavior without teaching it; when the shadow is removed, behavior often returns unless replacement skills were explicitly built [-M]
- Can create adult dependence — the child may learn to regulate only when the adult is present, so support must be systematically faded [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility to the learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- If the child experiences shadowing as punishment or stigmatization, it can damage the adult–child relationship and increase avoidance or escalation [-M]
- Does not by itself address underlying causes (skill deficits, unmet needs, family stressors); it must be embedded in a broader behavior support plan

#### Implementation Variability
- **Intensity gradient**: full shadowing (arm's reach, all settings) → zone supervision (same room, reduced proximity) → scheduled check-ins
- **Same-adult consistency**: using one familiar adult builds trust and makes prompts more effective; rotating adults weakens the relationship
- **Data-collection emphasis**: some implementations prioritize structured ABC (antecedent–behavior–consequence) observation to inform a functional behavior assessment, with coaching secondary
- **Peer-invisible framing**: the adult can be positioned as a helper or station-based volunteer so peers do not perceive targeting

### Target Learners
- Young children (typically preschool through early elementary) whose behavior poses a safety risk to themselves or others, such as biting, hitting, or eloping [~M]
- Children who have not responded to whole-class behavior supports and need individualized intensity
- Less appropriate for older learners, for whom constant adult proximity is stigmatizing and less effective than self-monitoring approaches [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral self-regulation: replacing unsafe behavior with taught coping strategies
- Emotional recognition and communication: identifying triggers and asking for help before escalation
- Classroom safety and participation: maintaining access to instruction and peer interaction while the behavior is addressed

### Instructions
1. **Assess first.** Observe and document antecedents and consequences of the target behavior to identify its function before assigning a shadow.
2. **Assign a consistent adult.** Choose one adult the child trusts; brief them on triggers, escalation signs, and the replacement behaviors to teach.
3. **Shadow with purpose.** Stay within arm's reach, pre-correcting before known triggers and coaching replacement behavior in the moment ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)); reinforce positive behavior immediately and specifically.
4. **Use brief check-ins.** At natural transitions, hold short conversations about how the child is feeling and what strategy they can use next ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md)).
5. **Fade systematically.** After roughly two weeks incident-free, reduce proximity (same room → across the room → scheduled check-ins), transferring monitoring responsibility to the child through self-monitoring tools [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M].

## Examples
- **Preschool biting/hitting**: a paraprofessional shadows a four-year-old during free play and transitions, pre-correcting before high-risk moments and coaching the child to request a turn or a break instead of biting; support fades to across-the-room monitoring after two incident-free weeks.
- **Incredible Years (Webster-Stratton)** — teacher training in this program includes individualized, close adult support for children with persistent aggressive behavior alongside classroom-wide social-emotional instruction ([http://www.incredibleyears.com](http://www.incredibleyears.com)).
- **Elopement in early elementary**: a shadow walks with the child between settings, teaching a "walk with the line" routine and a break-request card, then fades to checkpoint supervision.
