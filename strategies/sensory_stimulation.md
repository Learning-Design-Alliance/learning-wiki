# Sensory Stimulation

## Description
Sensory stimulation involves providing experiences that engage a person's senses — tactile, proprioceptive, vestibular, visual, or auditory — to improve regulation, attention, and focus. In classrooms this typically takes the form of fidget tools, movement breaks, weighted or textured materials, and sensory-rich activities that help learners manage sensory defensiveness or satisfy sensory-seeking needs. The premise is that addressing sensory needs first reduces fidgeting, off-task behavior, and dysregulation, creating conditions for learning rather than teaching content directly.

## Design Implications

Sensory stimulation is best understood as a **regulation support**, not an instructional method: it prepares learners for instruction rather than delivering it. Evidence for academic gains is thin and mostly indirect; the strongest support is for individualized, therapist-guided sensory integration with children with sensory processing difficulties [Sensory integration interventions show modest, context-dependent gains in self-regulation for children with sensory processing challenges.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+W]. Generic classroom fidget programs show weaker and more mixed effects on attention [~W]. Importantly, stimulation that is *irrelevant* to the task can impose extraneous load rather than relieve it [Decorative or irrelevant sensory input does not improve learning and can distract.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to sensory tools (fidgets, textured objects, movement options) matched to individual sensory profiles
- Clear rules and guidelines for use, so tools serve regulation rather than becoming toys
- Ongoing conversation with learners about whether a given tool is actually helping ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
- For learners with significant sensory processing needs, consultation with an occupational therapist

#### Constraints
- Unstructured fidget use can become a distraction for the user and peers, degrading rather than supporting attention [-W]
- Effectiveness is highly individual: a tool that calms one learner may over-stimulate another; there is no reliable "sensory profile" assessment that predicts which tool will work [~W]
- Broad claims that sensory stimulation improves learning outcomes for typically developing learners are not well supported [-W]
- Adding stimulating materials to already dense instructional materials increases extraneous load [Cognitive overload degrades learning when extraneous input competes for working memory.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Individual fidget tools** — small tactile objects used discreetly during seated instruction
- **Movement-based stimulation** — standing desks, movement breaks, or [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) activities that embed proprioceptive input in the lesson itself
- **Environmental modification** — reducing aversive stimulation (noise, harsh lighting) via [Acoustics and Noise Management](acoustics_and_noise_management.md), which is often more effective than adding stimulation
- **Formal sensory integration therapy** — therapist-delivered protocols (e.g., Ayres Sensory Integration), distinct from classroom accommodations

### Target Learners
- Learners with identified sensory processing needs, including many autistic learners and learners with ADHD [+W]
- Sensory seekers who fidget or leave their seats during seated work [~W]
- Typically developing learners benefit mainly from *reduced* aversive stimulation, not added stimulation [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation and emotional/behavioral stability as a precondition for academic goals
- Attention and on-task behavior during sustained seated activities
- Not a strategy for content acquisition itself — pair with instructional methods ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Application](../elements/application.md)) for learning goals

### Instructions
1. Identify learners whose attention or regulation difficulties appear sensory-driven; consult occupational therapy records or [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) plans where they exist.
2. Offer a small menu of sensory options and let the learner choose ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)); autonomy improves the likelihood the tool is used purposefully.
3. Establish explicit rules: when the tool may be used, what it may not become (a toy), and how to request a change.
4. Schedule regular [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) to evaluate whether each tool is improving focus; retire tools that are not working.
5. Pair regulation supports with actual instruction — sensory input prepares learners for [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and [Attention](../elements/attention.md) to content; it does not replace them.

## Examples
- **Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI)** — a therapist-delivered protocol with the strongest research base, used primarily with autistic children; see the [AOTA evidence brief](https://www.aota.org).
- **Elementary classroom fidget programs** — schools provide tactile cubes and fidget bands on chairs during seated instruction, with explicit use rules; products like the [Skoog](https://skoogmusic.com) tactile cube add acoustic feedback to support exploration.
- **Movement breaks** — structured proprioceptive activities (wall pushes, stretching) between instructional blocks, common in [GoNoodle](https://www.gonoodle.com)-style classroom routines.
