# Text Based Discussion Protocols

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Text based discussion protocols are structured talk routines — such as Socratic seminars, reciprocal teaching, Accountable Talk, and literature circles — that organize student discussion around a common text. Each protocol specifies who speaks, when, with what prompts, and toward what goal, converting the unpredictable dynamics of open discussion into a repeatable structure. The shared text anchors the conversation, requiring participants to ground claims in textual evidence rather than personal opinion.

## Design Implications

Structured discussion improves comprehension and reasoning more reliably than recitation or unstructured talk, because it requires learners to articulate, defend, and revise interpretations [Structured discussion improves reading comprehension more than recitation.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. The protocol is the mechanism: without norms for evidence-based turns, discussion collapses into IRE (initiate–respond–evaluate) exchanges dominated by the teacher and a few students. Protocols that require argumentation — claims supported by text evidence, challenges to peers' reasoning — produce the largest gains [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A text rich enough to support multiple defensible interpretations; protocols stall on texts with a single obvious reading
- Explicit teaching of the talk moves and norms before the first discussion, with modeling ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) of an evidence-based turn)
- A preparation phase in which students read and annotate the text ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)) so discussion starts from shared ground
- A discussion question or problem that is genuinely open — not a disguised comprehension check

#### Constraints
- Protocols fail when the teacher reverts to evaluating answers rather than facilitating; IRE patterns suppress the peer-to-peer reasoning the protocol is designed to elicit [-M]
- Groups without adequate reading fluency spend working memory on decoding, leaving too little for discussion; scaffolding the text first is necessary [-M]
- Overly rigid protocols can become procedural performance — students follow turn-taking rules without genuine engagement with ideas [~W]
- Dominant speakers and status hierarchies persist unless the protocol actively redistributes voice (e.g., assigned roles, talking pieces) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Socratic seminar**: whole group, open-ended question, students question each other; teacher silent
- **Reciprocal teaching**: small groups rotate predictor, questioner, clarifier, summarizer roles
- **Accountable Talk** (Resnick et al.): teacher-orchestrated with specific talk moves (revoicing, pressing for reasoning, challenging)
- **Literature circles**: role-based small groups with written preparation sheets
- **Fishbowl**: inner circle discusses while outer circle observes and gives feedback, then swaps

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through adult learners; requires sufficient decoding fluency to read the anchor text
- Especially valuable for learners who rarely participate in unstructured discussion — protocols redistribute airtime
- English language learners benefit from sentence frames and norms that make participation expectations explicit [+W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension and interpretation of complex texts
- Argumentation and evidence-based reasoning [Argumentation improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Academic language and oral discourse skills
- Perspective-taking through exposure to peers' interpretations

### Instructions
1. Select a text with interpretive richness and design one open, text-dependent question.
2. Have students read and annotate ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)), collecting evidence for discussion.
3. Teach the protocol explicitly: model an evidence-based turn and a respectful challenge ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
4. Run the discussion with the protocol; teacher facilitates with talk moves (pressing for evidence, revoicing) rather than evaluating answers.
5. Debrief both the content and the quality of the talk, then fade the protocol scaffolds as norms internalize ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Examples
- **Accountable Talk** (University of Pittsburgh Institute for Learning, https://ifl.pitt.edu) — teacher training in talk moves such as revoicing and pressing for reasoning, implemented across district-wide literacy programs.
- **Paideia Socratic Seminars** (National Paideia Center, https://www.paideia.org) — formal seminar cycle of pre-seminar text work, 15–45 minute open discussion, and post-seminar writing.
- **Literature Circles** (Harvey & Daniels) — role-based small-group discussion widely used in K–12 ELA curricula.
