# Watch Emotion Videos

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The teacher shares a short video that names and explains "big emotions" (anger, anxiety, frustration, excitement) and models simple regulation strategies. Video serves as a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of emotional vocabulary and coping steps, followed by a structured debrief in which students connect the content to their own emotional experiences and rehearse the regulation steps.

## Design Implications

Video is effective here because it combines visual and verbal channels to convey both emotion concepts and modeled coping behavior [~M], consistent with dual coding of emotional content (see [Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md)). Prosocial and socio-emotional media content reliably increases children's prosocial behavior and emotional understanding when the target skill is clearly depicted [~M]. However, watching alone is insufficient: learning depends on the debrief and on guided application, since passive viewing without prompts produces shallow engagement and illusions of understanding [-M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to video playback and time for a debrief discussion
- A video whose emotion and regulation strategy are explicit and age-appropriate — not merely entertaining
- A debrief structure that asks students to identify the emotion, name the strategy shown, and connect it to their own experience ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- A follow-up application moment, such as a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) where students use the strategy during the day

#### Constraints
- Poorly chosen videos (fast-paced, high sensory intensity, or ambiguous content) can dysregulate rather than teach; some students need alternative sensory activities [-M]
- Videos that depict emotions without showing regulation steps model arousal without coping [-M]
- Watching without debrief does not produce transfer to real emotional situations [-M]
- Cognitive overload from dense narration or multiple simultaneous strategies degrades learning, especially for young children [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Grade-level matching: simpler, single-emotion videos for K–2; videos with more complex social situations for grades 3–5
- Pause-and-predict: stop the video at emotional peaks and ask students to name the feeling and predict a strategy before it is shown
- Repeated viewing of the same short video across a unit builds fluency with the vocabulary and steps
- Students can later create their own emotion videos, shifting from [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) to [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)

### Target Learners
- K–5 learners, who benefit from concrete, visual models of internal states they cannot yet name [~M]
- Students with limited emotional vocabulary or with autism/ADHD who benefit from explicit, repeatable modeling
- Less effective for adolescents without adaptation; older students often find cartoon-style emotion videos patronizing [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional literacy: identifying and naming emotions in self and others
- Procedural knowledge of regulation: knowing the steps of a coping strategy (e.g., stop–breathe–name the feeling–choose an action)
- Attitude and empathy goals: normalizing big emotions and building perspective-taking (see [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))

### Instructions
1. Preview and select a short (2–5 minute) video that names one emotion and demonstrates one clear regulation strategy.
2. Activate prior experience: ask students to recall a time they felt the target emotion ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Play the video, pausing at key moments to ask "What is this character feeling? What could they do?"
4. Debrief with [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md): name the emotion, restate the strategy steps, and connect to students' own lives.
5. Rehearse the steps as a class ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)), then post the steps as a visual reference.
6. Embed a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) later the same day asking students to try the strategy and report how it went.

## Examples
- **[Daniel Tiger's Neighborhood](https://pbskids.org/danieltiger)** (PBS) — each episode includes an explicit strategy song (e.g., "When you feel so mad that you want to roar, take a deep breath and count to four"); research links active mediation of this content to preschoolers' use of the strategies.
- **[Sesame Street](https://www.sesamestreet.org)** emotional well-being segments — short clips modeling naming feelings and calming strategies, with printable debrief guides for teachers.
- **Classroom routine**: watch a 3-minute clip on managing frustration, debrief the steps, then use the same steps during a subsequent [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) before a challenging task.
