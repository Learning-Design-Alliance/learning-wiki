# Morphology Instruction

## Description
Morphology instruction teaches learners that words are built from meaningful units — base words, roots, prefixes, and suffixes — and gives them procedures for decomposing unfamiliar words into those units. It is carried out through explicit teaching of common morphemes, guided word analysis (e.g., "un + predict + able"), and structured word inquiry that tests hypotheses about word origins and relationships (e.g., *sign* → *signal* → *signature*).

## Design Implications

Morphology instruction works because it converts thousands of arbitrary word-learning tasks into a smaller generative system: knowing a handful of high-frequency affixes unlocks families of related words. Meta-analytic evidence shows it improves literacy outcomes, with the strongest effects on morphological awareness itself and smaller but reliable transfer to decoding, spelling, and comprehension [Goodwin & Ahn, 2013]. It functions as a form of [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md): a polymorphemic word held as two or three meaningful units imposes less working-memory load than the same word held as a string of letters [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit teaching of a small set of high-frequency, generative morphemes before expecting independent analysis
- Word matrices and word sums (structured word inquiry tools) that make decomposition visible
- Connected text in which taught morphemes recur, so analysis is applied during real reading ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
- Attention to phonological shifts and spelling changes when morphemes combine (e.g., *sign* → *signal*)

#### Constraints
- Effects shrink or reverse when morpheme analysis is taught as rote affix memorization detached from meaning and word families [-M]
- Less effective for learners who have not yet automated basic decoding; decomposition adds load rather than reducing it when letter-sound mapping is still effortful [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [~M]
- Transparent, consistent morphemes (e.g., *un-*, *-ing*) yield larger gains than low-frequency or semantically opaque ones (e.g., the *-mit* in *submit*) [~M]
- English's mixed orthography means morphology explains spelling only partially; overreliance on morphological rules produces errors on irregular words [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Embedded in whole-class vocabulary lessons vs. targeted small-group intervention for struggling readers
- Structured word inquiry (real spelling) vs. conventional prefix/suffix/root lists
- Oral-only morphological awareness activities for younger learners vs. written word analysis from mid-primary onward

### Target Learners
- Struggling readers in upper elementary and secondary school, who gain the largest decoding and comprehension benefits [Goodwin & Ahn, 2013] [+M]
- English learners, whose first language often supplies cognate morphemes that transfer (e.g., Spanish *-ción* → English *-tion*) [+M]
- Beginning decoders benefit less until word recognition is partially automatic [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Vocabulary growth: inferring meanings of unfamiliar polymorphemic words in content-area text
- Decoding and spelling of multisyllabic words
- Word consciousness and independent word-analysis strategies

### Instructions
1. Select a high-frequency, generative morpheme set tied to current reading material.
2. Model decomposition aloud with a word sum, making the reasoning visible (a [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) move within [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md)).
3. Guide learners through [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) — contrasting word pairs that share a morpheme (*sign/signal*) versus pairs that merely look similar — since structured comparison improves generalization [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M].
4. Use [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) to connect new morphemes to known words and, for English learners, to cognates in the home language.
5. Have learners generate their own word families and test hypotheses about spelling-meaning connections, then apply analysis during connected-text reading ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).

## Examples
- **Structured Word Inquiry** (Bowers & Kirby) — classrooms use word matrices and word sums to investigate morphological families; see https://www.wordworkskingston.com
- **REWARDS** (Archer, Gleason & Vachon) — a widely used intervention teaching adolescents to decompose multisyllabic words by affixes; see https://www.voyagersopris.com
- **Morpheme-based vocabulary apps** such as Vocabulary.com reinforce morphological families through repeated exposure across related words.
