# SEL Videos with Discussion

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy involves showing short video clips on Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) topics — emotion recognition, conflict resolution, empathy, self-regulation — followed by facilitated discussion or related activities. The video provides a shared, concrete referent; discussion connects the depicted situations to learners' background knowledge and personal lives, making SEL concepts relevant and actionable.

## Design Implications

Video is effective when it functions as a model of social-emotional behavior rather than mere entertainment; learners extract more from modeled scenarios when attention is directed to the relevant cues and when the video is followed by active processing [Multimedia learning improves when extraneous material is excluded and essential material is highlighted.](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [+M]. Discussion converts observation into elaboration: articulating what characters felt, why, and what they could do next requires learners to generate and organize the content themselves [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The discussion, not the video, is where most of the learning happens — the video's role is to supply a common, emotionally safe case that all learners can discuss without exposing personal experience directly.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Short (2–5 minute) video clips depicting emotionally salient, developmentally appropriate scenarios
- Prepared discussion prompts that move from identification ("What did the character feel?") to inference ("Why?") to application ("What would you do?") ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- A facilitator who can manage disclosure carefully — discussion should center on the video's characters, not require students to volunteer personal experiences
- A follow-on activity that asks learners to apply the concept ([Application](../elements/application.md)), e.g., role-play or written response

#### Constraints
- Videos that do not reflect learners' cultural backgrounds or lived experiences can reduce relevance and engagement [-M] — mismatched scenarios undermine the "connection to personal lives" goal
- Unstructured viewing produces shallow processing; students attend to plot, not social-emotional cues, unless prompted [Multimedia learning improves when extraneous material is excluded and essential material is highlighted.](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) [-M]
- Discussions dominated by a few voices, or that drift into personal disclosure without facilitation, can harm classroom belonging rather than build it [Belonging interventions improve outcomes when they normalize difficulty and include all students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M]
- One-off video lessons without a sustained SEL program show weak effects; benefits compound when embedded in a broader sequence [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Animated clips for younger learners; documentary or dramatized clips for adolescents and adults
- Think-pair-share before whole-group discussion to increase participation equity
- Pause-and-predict formats: stop the video at a decision point and have learners predict or advise the character ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) as a follow-up)
- Learner-selected clips to increase personalization and relevance

### Target Learners
- K–12 students, especially elementary learners still building emotion vocabulary and recognition
- Adolescents who respond better to vicarious scenarios than direct personal questioning
- Adult learners in professional development (e.g., teachers, healthcare workers) analyzing interpersonal situations
- English language learners — video supplies nonverbal emotional cues that support comprehension

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotion identification and vocabulary building
- Empathy and perspective-taking: inferring others' mental and emotional states [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Social problem-solving: generating and evaluating behavioral responses to conflict
- Norms and expectations: establishing shared classroom language for discussing emotions

### Instructions
1. Select a short video depicting a clear social-emotional scenario aligned to the target competency; preview it and identify the key moments to pause on.
2. Activate prior knowledge before viewing with a brief prompt ("Tell me about a time you saw someone upset") ([Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md)).
3. Show the video, pausing at decision points to ask identification and inference questions ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) — the video models the social behavior).
4. Facilitate structured discussion moving from observation → inference → application ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)); use pair-talk first to broaden participation.
5. Close with an application activity: role-play an alternative ending, write advice to the character, or set a personal behavioral goal ([Application](../elements/application.md), [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)).
6. Revisit the scenario in later lessons to reinforce and transfer the concept.

## Examples
- **[Second Step](https://www.secondstep.org)** (Committee for Children) — K–8 SEL curriculum built around short video scenarios of peer conflict, each followed by scripted discussion questions and role-play activities.
- **[RULER](https://medicine.yale.edu/education/ruler/)** (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) — uses media and the Mood Meter to anchor classroom discussion of emotions within a sustained whole-school approach.
- **iLearn with Poko: Emotions and Colors!** — after viewing a short animated story, young learners identify the emotions experienced by the characters, developing emotion understanding through guided identification.
