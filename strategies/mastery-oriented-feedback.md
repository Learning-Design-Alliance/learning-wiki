# Mastery Oriented Feedback

## Description
Mastery oriented feedback communicates how a learner's current performance relates to their learning goals and what strategies will move them forward, rather than judging ability, awarding global praise, or ranking students against peers. It treats errors as information about the learning process, keeping attention on the task instead of the self.

## Design Implications

Feedback is among the most powerful influences on achievement, but its effects are highly variable — poorly framed feedback can reduce performance [Feedback effects on learning are highly variable and sometimes negative.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~S]. Mastery orientation directs attention to the task and the process, which supports the competence needs that drive intrinsic motivation [Autonomy- and competence-supportive conditions increase intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Effective mastery feedback is specific to criteria, actionable, and delivered in a context where learners act on it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clear learning goals and success criteria the feedback can reference ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) aligned to visible criteria)
- Timeliness — feedback must arrive while learners can still use it on the task
- Opportunities to revise or retry, so the feedback has somewhere to go ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- A classroom climate where error is treated as normal, so feedback does not trigger defensiveness

#### Constraints
- Feedback that directs attention to the self ("you're so smart") or to social comparison undermines mastery orientation and can depress performance for struggling learners [Feedback effects on learning are highly variable and sometimes negative.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [-S]
- Feedback without a chance to act on it produces little learning gain [Feedback effects on learning are highly variable and sometimes negative.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [-S]
- Overly detailed feedback can overwhelm novices; it must be chunked to working memory limits [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]
- Public feedback can threaten belonging for students who already feel marginal, converting an informational message into an identity threat [Brief belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Written comments on drafts vs. oral conferencing vs. automated hints in adaptive systems
- Whole-class feedback on common errors preserves privacy while retaining specificity
- Rubric-referenced self- and peer-assessment trains learners to generate mastery feedback for themselves

### Target Learners
- Struggling learners benefit most from process-focused feedback; ability-focused feedback is especially harmful for them [Feedback effects on learning are highly variable and sometimes negative.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [-S]
- High-achievers may need less feedback; excessive praise or correction can disengage them [Feedback effects on learning are highly variable and sometimes negative.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M]
- Learners with low prior knowledge need feedback paired with reduced task complexity to avoid overload [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Skill refinement: closing the gap between current and target performance
- Self-regulated learning: building learners' capacity to monitor and adjust their own strategies
- Motivational goals: sustaining engagement and persistence through setbacks

### Instructions
1. Establish success criteria before the task so feedback has a shared reference point ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
2. Observe performance and identify the gap between current work and the goal — not the gap between the student and peers
3. Deliver feedback that is specific to the task or the strategy ("your thesis doesn't match your evidence — try X"), avoiding person-level judgments
4. Pair feedback with an immediate opportunity to revise ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)) or a plan for the next attempt
5. Follow up with a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) to confirm the learner understood and acted on the feedback

## Examples
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — hints and mastery-tracking messages frame errors as steps toward mastery levels rather than grades, encouraging retry without penalty.
- **Writing conferences** — a teacher reviews a draft against the rubric, names one strength and one high-leverage revision, and the student revises in class; the grade comes only after revision.
- **[ASSISTments](https://www.assistments.org)** — delivers immediate, task-specific feedback on math problems so students correct errors during practice rather than after grading.
