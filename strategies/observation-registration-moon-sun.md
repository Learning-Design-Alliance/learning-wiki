# Center the sequence on naked-eye observation and registration of the Moon and Sun, using ephemeris tables and free sky simulators when direct observation is not possible

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
The chapter prescribes observation and registration as the central procedures of a topocentric sequence: naked-eye tracking of the Moon's illuminated face, its apparent movement across the sky vault, and comparison of the apparent arcs of Moon and Sun. Ephemeris tables and free astronomical simulators support the analysis; the authors name Stellarium as free, easy to use and accessible even to small children. These resources become fundamental when city construction restricts direct observation to high angular heights near the zenith.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Introduce local angular-height and azimuth coordinates to specify the position of celestial bodies during early observations
#### Constraints
- In large cities the horizon may not be visible, and building density can restrict direct observation to angular heights near the zenith

### Target Learners
- primary and secondary science students

### Target Learning Goals
- record how the Moon's illumination changes day to day and hour to hour
- compare rise, culmination and setting times of the Sun and Moon
