# Introducing Yourself Via Video

## Description
The instructor records and shares a short personal video introduction at the start of a course — covering who they are, their connection to the subject, and something humanizing (interests, teaching philosophy, a story). The goal is to establish [social presence](../principles/social-presence.md) and instructor credibility before text-based interaction begins, so that subsequent discussion, feedback, and email exchanges are read as coming from a person rather than an anonymous system.

## Design Implications

Video introductions build the instructor's social presence, which the [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) framework identifies as a necessary condition for deep learning in online environments — cognitive presence rarely develops without it [~M]. Seeing and hearing the instructor also leverages voice and face as social cues that increase engagement with subsequent course communication [Media combinations affect recall and retention.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [~M]. A related benefit is persistence: students who perceive their instructor as a real, approachable person report higher belonging, and belonging predicts persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A short (1–3 minute) video — long enough to feel personal, short enough to respect attention
- Content that goes beyond the syllabus: personality, motivation for teaching, a humanizing anecdote
- A transcript or captions for accessibility
- A parallel structure inviting students to introduce themselves (e.g., a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) or discussion thread), so the introduction opens a two-way channel rather than a broadcast

#### Constraints
- A polished, over-produced video can increase perceived distance; moderate informality and small imperfections improve perceived warmth [~W]
- A one-way introduction with no student response opportunity has little measurable effect on engagement — the strategy works as the opening move of a reciprocal exchange, not as a standalone artifact [-W]
- Instructor video consumes bandwidth and production time; in very large courses, individualized follow-up (not the video itself) is what sustains connection
- Poor audio quality undermines the effect more than poor video quality — voice carries most of the social-presence signal [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Talking-head intro** — direct address to camera; strongest social-presence signal
- **Narrated slideshow or screencast** — lower production cost; weaker but still useful presence cue
- **Course-tour video** — combines introduction with orientation to course structure
- **Student video introductions** — the same strategy applied peer-to-peer, often using tools like Flip (formerly Flipgrid) or VoiceThread, to build community among learners rather than between learner and instructor

### Target Learners
- Fully online learners who otherwise never see the instructor and may go an entire term without synchronous contact [~M]
- First-time online students, who are most likely to experience online courses as impersonal and to disengage early
- Students in large sections where individual instructor contact is scarce — the video is a scalable substitute for a first impression

### Target Learning Goals
- Community building and social presence rather than content objectives
- Setting norms for communication and participation
- Humanizing feedback: students interpret written feedback more charitably once they can attach a face and voice to it [~W]

### Instructions
1. Record a 1–3 minute video introducing yourself: name, connection to the discipline, one personal detail, and what you hope students will take from the course.
2. Add captions or a transcript for accessibility.
3. Post it in the course's opening module alongside a welcome message.
4. Pair it with a reciprocal activity — a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) thread or [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) where students post their own introductions — and respond personally to at least a sample of them.
5. Refer back to the introduction ("as I mentioned in my intro video…") in early feedback to reinforce the personal connection.

## Examples
- **Flip (https://info.flip.com)** — commonly used for video introduction threads where instructor and students post short intro videos in a shared grid.
- **VoiceThread (https://voicethread.com)** — supports asynchronous video/audio introductions with threaded commenting.
- A typical asynchronous online course opens Week 1 with the instructor's video, a syllabus quiz, and a "post your own intro video or written intro" discussion — the video models the format students are asked to follow.
