Model Reflection¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · no claims cited

Description¶

Students need to encounter reflective role models. Modeling reflection can involve reflecting on a workshop session: teachers share journal excerpts that model evidence of good listening, probing for clarity, asking thoughtful questions, and voicing metacognitive thinking. Through experience and continuous modeling, the class learns to use habits of mind in reflective conversations, strengthening transfer to the internal voice of reflection.

Design Implications¶

Reflection is a skill that must be demonstrated before it can be internalized; observing an expert's reflective process gives learners a template for their own metacognitive self-talk Social modeling is a primary route through which cognitive and metacognitive strategies are acquired. [+M]. The model must make reasoning visible — not just conclusions — so learners can see how an expert notices, questions, and revises their thinking Modeling in cognitive apprenticeship makes tacit expert processes observable and learnable. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Time for reflection and a willingness to share experiences and insights
  • Authentic artifacts — real journal excerpts, decision points, mistakes — rather than polished exemplars
  • A follow-on structure that invites learners to practice the modeled moves (Class Discussion, journals, or Coaching)
  • The instructor must actually engage in their own learning and reflection; a model performing reflection they don't practice rings hollow and undermines credibility
  • Authentic material: reflect on real decisions, real student work, or real failures — not sanitized examples
  • Structured language for reflection (e.g., "What did I do? What happened? What will I change?") so learners hear a repeatable frame, not just musings
  • A follow-on structure where learners practice the same moves (Individual Reflection, Think-Aloud)

Constraints¶

  • Requires vulnerability; a teacher unwilling to share genuine uncertainty or error produces models students read as performance, not reflection [-W]
  • Overly polished or idealized models can imply that reflection is a talent rather than a practice, discouraging novices [~W]
  • If modeling is never faded into student-led reflection, learners remain dependent on external prompts [~M]
  • If the instructor is not themselves engaged in learning and reflection, the modeling is performative and learners detect it [-W]
  • Modeled reflection that is too polished or expert-level can intimidate novices or set an unreachable standard [~W]
  • Modeling alone, without structured learner practice and feedback, produces appreciation of reflection but not the habit [-M] — observation must be paired with Practice
  • Overly long or unstructured modeling consumes time and can blur into storytelling with little transfer value [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Live think-aloud reflection after a lesson or workshop versus pre-written shared journal excerpts
  • Peer models: advanced students modeling reflection can be more attainable than expert models for some learners [~M]
  • Written, audio, or video formats; video preserves tone and hesitation, which are themselves part of reflective practice
  • Live modeling: instructor reflects aloud in the moment after a lesson, task, or discussion
  • Retrospective modeling: instructor revisits a past decision (e.g., "why I redesigned this unit") with artifacts as evidence
  • Peer modeling: students who reflect well share their process publicly, providing nearer-peer exemplars
  • Written modeling: instructor shares annotated reflective journals or post-mortems, as in teacher-education programs where teacher educators model the reflection they ask of student teachers

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who have no internal template for what reflection "sounds like"
  • Pre-service and in-service teachers learning reflective practice as part of professional growth
  • Students developing self-regulated learning habits Self-regulated learners benefit from explicit modeling of planning, monitoring, and evaluation strategies. [+M]
  • Novices who have never seen what substantive reflection looks like and default to "I did the work and it went fine" [+W]
  • Pre-service teachers and other professional learners, where reflective practice is itself a target competency [+M]
  • Learners in Cognitive Apprenticeship settings, where articulation of expert thinking is a core phase
  • Less necessary for learners already fluent in self-regulated learning, who may find modeled reflection redundant

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Metacognitive skill development: noticing one's own understanding, confusion, and strategy use
  • Professional dispositions: habits of mind such as listening, probing, and revising positions
  • Transfer of reflective practice from guided conversation to independent internal dialogue
  • Metacognitive skill: learning to monitor and evaluate one's own understanding and performance
  • Dispositional change: establishing reflection as a habitual professional practice, not a compliance task
  • Self-regulated learning: seeing how self-evaluation feeds into strategy adjustment

Instructions¶

  1. Select an authentic recent experience (a lesson, workshop, or project) with genuine uncertainty or a mistake worth examining.
  2. Model the reflection aloud or in writing, making each move explicit — evidence of listening, probing for clarity, questioning assumptions, and metacognitive commentary (a Think-Aloud applied to reflection itself).
  3. Share artifacts such as journal excerpts so students can revisit and compare reflective moves.
  4. Invite students to practice the same moves in structured Class Discussion or journals, with the teacher Coaching and giving feedback.
  5. Gradually fade the model: shift from teacher-modeled to student-led reflective conversations, then to private reflection.

Related Strategies¶

  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the general mechanism; model reflection applies it to metacognitive and professional practice
  • Journaling — the artifact and practice medium through which modeled reflection becomes student reflection
  • Check-Ins — brief structured moments where modeled reflection can be practiced at low stakes

Examples¶

  • A teacher educator reflects on a workshop session and shares journal excerpts with teacher candidates, annotating where she noticed a missed question or revised an assumption.
  • Medical education uses chart-review sessions in which an attending physician verbalizes diagnostic reasoning and self-correction, modeling clinical reflection for residents.
  • Writing conferences in which the teacher rereads their own draft aloud, questioning word choices, model revision as reflective practice.
  • Teacher education (e.g., Utrecht's IVLOS approach) — teacher educators explicitly model the reflective practice they require of student teachers, including publicly analyzing their own lessons that went poorly (Lunenberg, Korthagen, & Swennen, 2007).
  • Writing workshop (Calkins' Units of Study) — the teacher writes in front of students, revising and reflecting on word choices aloud, then students apply the same moves to their own drafts.
  • Project post-mortems in CS courses — after a team project, the instructor models a retrospective on their own course design ("I underestimated the debugging time; next iteration I'll add a checkpoint"), then teams run the same retrospective on their collaboration.
