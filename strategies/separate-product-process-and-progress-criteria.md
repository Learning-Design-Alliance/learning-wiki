# Separate Product, Process, and Progress Criteria

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Rather than collapsing achievement, effort, behavior, and growth into a single grade, this strategy grades each criterion separately: **product** (what students know and can do against standards), **process** (learning behaviors such as practice quality, revision, and collaboration), and **progress** (improvement over time). Each criterion is reported separately with its own rubric, so the achievement grade remains a clear signal of mastery while behaviors and growth are still acknowledged and incentivized.

## Design Implications

Blending non-achievement factors into achievement grades distorts the grade's meaning as a communication of learning, and grading practices such as averaging early failures with later mastery reduce the validity of grades as measures of achievement [Grades that mix behavior with achievement lose validity as indicators of learning.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M]. Separating the criteria preserves the motivational value of recognizing effort and growth without contaminating the mastery signal, and supports [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) by making each grade actionable — students know *what* to improve academically versus *how* to improve their learning behaviors.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Separate, detailed rubrics for each criterion, with descriptors students can understand ([Rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md))
- A reporting structure (gradebook categories, report card fields, or two-grade systems) that keeps criteria visibly distinct
- Clear communication with students and families about what each grade means
- Evidence collection for process and progress: practice records, revision artifacts, baseline and later work samples ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))

#### Constraints
- Significantly more grading and record-keeping effort than a single blended grade; teachers without protected planning time often revert to blending [-W]
- Process and progress criteria are more subjective and prone to bias (e.g., grading "effort" differently across students) unless anchored in observable behaviors and artifact evidence [~M]
- Progress grades can disadvantage students who start strong and plateau, or advantage low baselines, if improvement is measured naively rather than against grade-level expectations [~M]
- In systems that permit only one reported grade, separation requires institutional change and may create confusion if families expect a single number [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Two-grade model** (e.g., academic grade + citizenship/work-habits grade), common in standards-based grading systems
- **Three-category gradebook** with weighted but separate product/process/progress categories
- **Report-card separation**: one transcript grade (product) with narrative or indicator-scale reporting for process and progress
- Progress can be measured against the student's own baseline or against grade-level mastery trajectories; the latter is more defensible for equity

### Target Learners
- Struggling students and those with low initial performance, who benefit when early low scores do not permanently depress their achievement grade [Grades that average early failure with later mastery underrepresent final learning.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]
- High-achievers who plateau, for whom a progress grade removes pressure to inflate growth artificially
- Students developing self-regulation, since separate process grades make learning behaviors an explicit object of feedback [Separate process feedback supports self-regulated learning behaviors.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Mastery of content standards (product) — the primary achievement signal
- Development of learning behaviors and work habits (process): revision, practice quality, collaboration
- Growth orientation and long-term improvement (progress), supporting a mastery rather than performance orientation

### Instructions
1. Define the **product criteria**: unpack standards into [clear, mastery-focused rubrics](../elements/rubrics.md) and grade only evidence of achievement against them.
2. Define **process criteria** as observable behaviors (completing practice, using feedback, contributing to [collaborative work](../elements/collaborative-learning.md)) with their own rubric; grade them from artifacts, not impressions.
3. Define **progress criteria**: collect baseline work samples and compare later work against grade-level expectations, not just the baseline.
4. Set up separate gradebook categories or reporting fields so criteria are never averaged together.
5. Deliver feedback aligned to each criterion using [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) that names which criterion it addresses.
6. [Assess performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) periodically and communicate to students and families what each grade means and how to act on it.

## Examples
- A middle school using standards-based report cards (e.g., many districts implementing Marzano's model) reports a 1–4 academic grade per standard plus separate 1–4 indicators for work completion and growth.
- A writing course grades the final research paper on a product rubric, grades the draft–feedback–revision cycle on a process rubric, and compares the final paper to the student's first-week writing sample for a progress rating.
- **[ActiveGrade](https://www.activegrade.org)** and **[JumpRope](https://www.jumprope.org)** — standards-based gradebook platforms whose category structure enforces separation between achievement and behavior indicators.
