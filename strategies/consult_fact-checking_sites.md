# Consult Fact-Checking Sites

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners develop the habit of consulting professional fact-checking services — FactCheck.org, Snopes.com, and PolitiFact.com — to verify the truth of claims, and use reverse image search (Google Images, TinEye) to trace the origin and authenticity of photographs. The strategy treats verification as an external, routinized check rather than an ad hoc judgment, shifting the burden from internal reasoning to reliable institutional resources.

## Design Implications

Consulting expert fact-checkers is a form of "lateral reading": leaving an unfamiliar source to see what independent, professional evaluators say about it. Research on civic online reasoning shows that lateral reading is what professional fact-checkers do and what students typically fail to do spontaneously — students tend to stay on the page and evaluate its surface features instead [Wineburg & McGrew found fact-checkers outperform historians and students by reading laterally.](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811921900102) [+S]. The strategy works best when checking is framed as a routine step in any research or sharing workflow, not as a remedial activity triggered only by suspicion.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to fact-checking sites (FactCheck.org, Snopes.com, PolitiFact.com) and reverse image search tools (Google Images, TinEye)
- Explicit instruction in *when* to check — a trigger heuristic such as "any surprising claim, statistic, or image gets checked before use or sharing"
- A short modeled demonstration of a check ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)) so learners see the actual search moves, not just the outcome
- A follow-on task requiring learners to perform and document checks themselves ([Application](../elements/application.md))

#### Constraints
- Fact-checking sites cover a narrow slice of claims; learners who treat "not on Snopes" as "verified" develop false confidence [-M]
- Checking individual claims does not build the broader disposition to evaluate sources; without lateral-reading instruction, learners revert to judging credibility by site design and polish [-S]
- Politically charged topics can trigger motivated reasoning: learners discount fact-check verdicts that conflict with prior beliefs, especially for high-knowledge, high-identity groups [~M]
- Reverse image search fails on AI-generated or heavily edited images, where no prior version exists to find [-W]
- Overhead is real — checking every claim is impractical, so learners need triage heuristics or the habit collapses under load [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Full workflow: verify claim → check source laterally → reverse-search images → record verdict and evidence
- Lightweight version: a "two-minute check" rule applied before sharing on social media
- Prebunking variant: expose learners to common fact-check patterns (debunked viral claims) to inoculate against manipulation techniques
- Peer variant: learners fact-check each other's claims in a [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), normalizing verification as a shared norm rather than an accusation

### Target Learners
- Secondary and postsecondary students conducting research for the first time, who default to within-page evaluation cues [+S]
- General adult audiences who share content on social media; brief accuracy prompts reduce sharing of false headlines [~M]
- Less effective for learners with strong partisan identity on identity-charged claims without additional motivation support [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Media literacy: evaluating the credibility of online sources and claims
- Civic online reasoning: evidence-based judgment before acting on or sharing information
- Procedural skill: executing a repeatable verification routine (search, trace provenance, record evidence)

### Instructions
1. Model a check with a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md): take a current viral claim, search it on Snopes or FactCheck.org, narrate query choices and how to read the verdict and cited evidence.
2. Teach lateral reading explicitly: open new tabs and check *who else* says something about the source, rather than reading the source's own about page.
3. Demonstrate reverse image search on a real miscaptioned photo; show how to trace the original context and date.
4. Have learners [Apply](../elements/application.md) the routine: verify 2–3 claims of their choosing and document the verdict, evidence, and residual uncertainty.
5. Close with [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) on edge cases — claims no fact-checker has covered — and what to do when professional verification is unavailable.

## Examples
- A civics class takes three viral political claims, checks each on PolitiFact and FactCheck.org, and writes a short verdict memo citing the fact-check's own evidence chain.
- A media literacy unit uses a known miscaptioned photo (e.g., shark "swimming" on a highway during floods) and has students trace it to its original source with Google Reverse Image Search and TinEye.
- The [Stanford History Education Group's civic online reasoning curriculum](https://cor.stanford.edu/) embeds fact-checking and lateral-reading tasks across classroom-ready lessons.
