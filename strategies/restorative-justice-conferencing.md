# Restorative Justice Conferencing

## Description
Restorative Justice Conferencing (RJC) brings together the person who caused harm, those harmed, and sometimes family or community members, in a structured meeting facilitated by a trained coordinator. Participants describe what happened, discuss its impact, and jointly agree on steps to repair the harm — apologies, restitution, service, or behavioral commitments. In schools, the same structure replaces exclusionary discipline with accountability conversations that keep students in community.

## Design Implications

RJC works as a learning intervention because it converts a disciplinary event into an opportunity for perspective-taking, moral reasoning, and social-emotional skill practice. Face-to-face dialogue with those harmed produces genuine remorse and reintegration rather than stigmatizing exclusion [Sherman & Strang's trials find restorative justice conferencing reduces repeat offending and increases victim satisfaction.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11292-014-9225-3) [+M]. The mechanism is *reintegrative shaming* — disapproving the act while reaffirming the person — which Braithwaite showed reduces reoffending compared with stigmatizing punishment [~S]. Empathic, relationship-preserving responses to misbehavior reduce suspensions, particularly for at-risk students [Brief empathic discipline interventions cut suspensions among Black and Latino students.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A trained, neutral facilitator who manages turn-taking, emotional intensity, and the agreement process
- Voluntary participation by the harmed party; coercion undermines the legitimacy on which the process depends
- Follow-through on the repair agreement — unmonitored commitments erode trust in the process
- School-wide alignment: RJC fails when teachers simultaneously use exclusionary discipline for the same offenses [~M]

#### Constraints
- Ineffective when the offender denies responsibility or the power imbalance between parties is severe (e.g., chronic bullying, violence) — victims can be re-traumatized by face-to-face contact [-M]
- Requires significant facilitator training and time; poorly run conferences can devolve into coerced apologies [-W]
- Less effective for repeat or systemic harm where the underlying conditions (climate, inequity) are unaddressed [~M]
- Can be perceived as "soft" by staff and families, undermining buy-in unless outcomes data are shared [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-school model**: conferencing embedded in a broader restorative practices framework (circles, mediation, restorative questions), as in Denver Public Schools and Oakland Unified
- **Diversion model**: conference as an alternative to suspension or court referral, triggered by the offense
- **Community model**: victim-offender conferencing run by justice agencies (e.g., restorative justice panels in Vermont)

### Target Learners
- Adolescents developing moral reasoning and perspective-taking capacity; the structured dialogue scaffolds empathy that lecture-based anti-bullying programs do not [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Students at risk of exclusionary discipline, for whom relationship-preserving responses improve belonging and outcomes [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Less appropriate for very young children without facilitation adaptations, or for offenders with no remorse or readiness to take responsibility [-W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional learning: empathy, perspective-taking, conflict resolution
- Moral reasoning: understanding harm and responsibility rather than rule compliance
- Community repair: rebuilding trust and reintegration after conflict

### Instructions
1. **Prepare separately.** The facilitator meets offender and victim individually to assess readiness, explain the process, and secure voluntary consent.
2. **Open the conference.** Facilitator sets ground rules; each party describes what happened from their own perspective ([Active Listening](active-listening.md)).
3. **Surface impact.** Participants articulate the harm's effects on themselves and others, prompting perspective-taking ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md)).
4. **Acknowledge responsibility.** The offender names the harm and its causes without deflection; the facilitator guards against minimization.
5. **Agree on repair.** The group co-constructs concrete, time-bound repair actions ([Action Planning](../strategies/action_planning.md)).
6. **Follow up.** The facilitator monitors completion and reconvenes if commitments are unmet ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).

## Examples
- **Oakland Unified School District** ([https://www.ousd.org](https://www.ousd.org)) — whole-school restorative justice program that reduced suspensions substantially after replacing exclusionary discipline with conferencing and circles.
- **Denver Public Schools Restorative Justice Program** — peer-led conferences and accountability agreements as an alternative to suspension.
- **RISE (Restorative Initiatives for Safe Environments), UK Youth Justice** — victim-offender conferencing delivered by youth offending teams.
