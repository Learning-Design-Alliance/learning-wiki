Growth Mindset Feedback¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 9 studies (3 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 theoretical, 1 causal), q1–q4 · 3 of 9 report an effect size

Description¶

Growth mindset feedback directs learners' attention to the processes that produce learning — effort, strategy selection, persistence, and revision — rather than to fixed traits like intelligence or talent. It treats errors as informative ("good mistakes") and frames grades as information about current performance, not judgments of ability. The approach operationalizes Dweck's mindset theory at the level of day-to-day feedback language, complementing broader Self-Regulated Learning instruction.

Design Implications¶

Feedback that addresses the task and the learning process produces stronger learning outcomes than feedback directed at the person, and person-level praise (e.g., "you're so smart") can actively undermine motivation and persistence after failure Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]. Process-focused feedback works because it gives learners actionable information about what to do next, whereas trait praise gives them nothing to act on and raises the stakes of future failure (Mueller & Dweck, 1998) [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Feedback language that names specific strategies and behaviors ("your diagram made the relationship clear") rather than generic effort praise ("you worked hard")
  • A classroom norm that errors are expected and useful — e.g., analyzing common mistakes publicly, valuing revision
  • Instructor consistency: a single trait-praising comment can undo the effect of otherwise process-oriented practice (Mueller & Dweck, 1998) [+S]
  • Alignment with assessment practices; if grades reward only outcomes, process feedback rings hollow (Assessment for Learning)

Constraints¶

  • Generic "effort praise" without strategy content is ineffective and can feel hollow to struggling learners; effort feedback must be coupled with strategy instruction [~M] — praising effort when the learner's approach is wrong reinforces unproductive persistence
  • Effects of standalone mindset interventions are small on average and shrink for already-advantaged or high-achieving students; benefits concentrate among lower-achieving and at-risk students (Yeager et al., 2019) [~S]
  • In competitive, grade-dominated environments, process feedback is easily overridden by outcome salience [-M]
  • Learners can perceive effort praise as low expectations ("praise for trying" signaling "you can't do better") [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written feedback on work vs. verbal in-the-moment responses vs. rubric language that embeds process criteria
  • Whole-class framing (norms, error analysis routines) vs. individual conferencing
  • Digital systems can deliver process-oriented hints and normalize errors at scale (e.g., ASSISTments' "wrong answers are useful data" messaging)

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling and lower-achieving students benefit most; large-scale studies show mindset effects concentrated in this group (Yeager et al., 2019) [~S]
  • Learners low in academic confidence, for whom process feedback builds perceived competence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Novices, who benefit from process and strategy focus over outcome focus Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Motivational: persistence, resilience, willingness to attempt difficult tasks
  • Metacognitive: strategy awareness and self-monitoring (Self-Regulated Learning)
  • Long-term achievement gains, primarily via increased engagement with challenging work (Yeager et al., 2019) [~S]

Instructions¶

  1. Audit existing feedback language; replace trait praise ("brilliant!") with strategy-and-process commentary tied to specific work Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]
  2. When a learner succeeds, name the strategies and effort that produced the success; when a learner struggles, pair effort acknowledgment with a concrete next strategy or a hint (Coaching)
  3. Frame errors as data: have learners identify what an error reveals and how to revise (Practice with revision cycles)
  4. Align grading and classroom norms with the message — reward improvement and revision, not just correct first attempts (Assessment for Learning)
  5. Support autonomy alongside process feedback; mindset messages work best in autonomy-supportive climates Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M] (Self-Determination Theory)

Related Strategies¶

  • Formative feedback practices — process-level feedback is the core mechanism of effective formative assessment
  • Mastery-oriented grading — structural counterpart that makes process feedback credible
  • Self-explanation prompts — a strategy that growth mindset feedback can direct learners toward
  • Cultivate positive academic mindsets through instructional practices aligned with students' psychological needs
  • Growth-mindset-informed math teaching practices: norms, no fixed labels, mixed-ability grouping, process praise, and formative assessment with retakes
  • Teachers cultivate approach-oriented goals and help students manage AI-feedback anxiety in AI-assisted language learning
  • Embed growth-mindset messages framing technological competence as developable into existing curricula as a low-cost, broad-reach intervention
  • Values-aligned teacher training that connects growth mindset-supportive practices to teachers' core professional values
  • Phrase praise modestly and focus on strategies rather than effort to prevent ability-denigrating inferences

Examples¶

  • Mueller & Dweck (1998) studies — Children praised for effort after success subsequently chose harder tasks and persisted longer after failure than children praised for intelligence [+S]
  • ASSISTments — Online math homework platform that gives immediate process-oriented feedback and treats errors as learning opportunities rather than penalties
  • PERTS Mindset Kit — Free research-based lessons and feedback-language resources for teachers implementing growth mindset practices
  • National Numeracy (UK) — Adult numeracy campaign explicitly built on "everyone can learn maths" messaging to counter math anxiety and fixed-ability beliefs
