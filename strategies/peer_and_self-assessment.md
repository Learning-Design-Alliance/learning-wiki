# Peer and Self-Assessment

## Description
Peer and self-assessment ask students to evaluate their own or classmates' work against explicit criteria, producing feedback and — more importantly — developing the capacity to judge quality. The act of applying criteria to someone else's work builds the internal standards students then apply to their own drafts. This positions assessment as a learning activity rather than only a measurement event, enacting [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md).

## Design Implications

Peer and self-assessment work because evaluating work against criteria requires the same domain knowledge as producing good work; the evaluation task is itself practice in quality judgment [Formative feedback and classroom assessment practices produce substantial learning gains.](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969595980050102) [+S]. Rubrics and exemplars are the critical supports: transparent criteria improve the validity of student judgments and reduce anxiety about the assessment process [Rubrics support consistent judgment and reduce assessment anxiety.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2013.01.002) [+M]. Peer marks correlate reasonably well with instructor marks when the task is well-structured and criteria are explicit, though agreement weakens for complex, open-ended work [Peer and teacher marks agree more closely with explicit criteria and simpler tasks.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543070003287) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Explicit criteria: a rubric or checklist students can actually apply, not just read ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
- Exemplars of work at varying quality levels so students can calibrate their judgments before applying criteria to live work
- Training or calibration activity — students practice judging a sample and compare their judgment with the instructor's or with peers'
- A structure for delivering feedback that is actionable rather than merely evaluative ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))

#### Constraints
- Untrained students default to leniency, severity, or halo effects; without calibration, peer marks diverge substantially from expert marks [Peer assessment reliability depends on training and judgment dimensions.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543068003249) [~M]
- Students often rate their own work against effort rather than quality, inflating self-marks for weak submissions [~M]
- When peer assessment carries high stakes, students game the system (reciprocal inflated grading) or experience anxiety that undermines the formative benefit [-M]
- Feedback that only assigns a score produces little learning; feedback must address the task and process, not the self [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Less effective when students lack the domain knowledge to recognize quality — novices cannot judge what they cannot yet perceive [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Formative-only**: peer feedback informs revision; instructor retains grading authority — lowest risk, most common entry point
- **Graded peer assessment**: peer marks contribute to grades, typically moderated or averaged across multiple reviewers to dilute individual bias
- **Self-assessment with rubrics**: students score their own drafts before submission, predicting instructor marks; accuracy itself becomes a graded metacognitive skill
- **Two-stage peer review**: students assess anonymous drafts, revise, then resubmit — closing the feedback loop

### Target Learners
- Higher education and secondary students with enough domain exposure to recognize quality dimensions [Peer assessment is feasible and beneficial across college and university settings.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543068003249) [+M]
- Students developing self-regulation: judging their own work against criteria strengthens monitoring and revision habits [Self-assessment supports self-regulated learning.](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]
- Students with low self-efficacy may initially underrate their work; calibration against exemplars helps correct this [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluative judgment: knowing what quality looks like in a domain, not just how to produce it
- Self-regulation: monitoring, revising, and taking responsibility for work quality
- Domain knowledge deepening: applying criteria requires articulating why work succeeds or fails
- Feedback literacy: giving, receiving, and acting on critique

### Instructions
1. Share the rubric and 2–3 anonymized exemplars at different quality levels before students begin their own work ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
2. Run a calibration exercise: students judge an exemplar, then compare their scores and reasoning with the instructor's judgment in a whole-class debrief ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
3. Have students exchange drafts anonymously and apply the rubric, writing comments that identify a specific strength and a specific improvement ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)).
4. Ask students to self-assess their own draft against the same rubric before receiving peer feedback.
5. Build in revision time so feedback is actually used — assessment without the opportunity to act on it is wasted [Formative assessment requires follow-up action to affect learning.](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969595980050102) [+S].
6. Compare peer and self-marks with instructor marks over time and discuss discrepancies to sharpen judgment.

## Examples
- **Calibrated Peer Review (CPR)** (https://cpr.molsci.ucla.edu) — a tool in which students first calibrate their judgment against instructor-graded exemplars, then review peers' anonymous submissions; calibration accuracy gates whether a student's reviews count.
- **Peergrade / PeerStudio-style platforms** — algorithmically distribute submissions so each student reviews several peers, aggregating reviews to moderate individual bias.
- **Self-assessed draft submission** — students attach a completed rubric with their essay predicting their grade; the instructor's mark and the student's mark are compared, and prediction accuracy is discussed in conference ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
