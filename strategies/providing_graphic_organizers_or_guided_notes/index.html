Providing Graphic Organizers or Guided Notes¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q3 · 1 of 7 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

During lectures, the instructor supplies a partially completed visual structure — a graphic organizer (matrix, hierarchy, concept map) or guided notes (skeleton outline with blanks, headings, and cues) — that students complete during the session. The materials externalize the lecture's structure, signaling which points matter and how they relate, so learners spend limited working memory on comprehension rather than on deciding what to record.

Design Implications¶

The strategy works because it offloads the selection and organization stages of note-taking, which compete with listening for working-memory resources Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]. Graphic organizers are most effective when they make relationships (compare/contrast, hierarchy, sequence) visible rather than merely listing topics; matrices that force learners to map facts across categories outperform outlines for retention and integration Graphic organizers that structure relationships improve learning from lectures. [+M]. Guided notes also raise the quality of students' own notes and subsequent test performance compared with unstructured note-taking [+M], and provided organizers can outperform learner-constructed notes when learners lack the skill to organize on the fly [~M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A structure that mirrors the actual logical organization of the lecture content, not a generic template
  • Deliberate blanks or incomplete cells that require active completion — fully pre-filled handouts invite passive receipt [-M]
  • Alignment between the organizer's categories and later assessment or application tasks
  • Brief instruction in how to use the organizer, especially for learners unfamiliar with the format

Constraints¶

  • Fully completed notes or organizers reduce engagement and produce shallower learning than partial versions requiring completion [-M]
  • A poorly matched structure (e.g., a hierarchy imposed on content that is actually comparative) can mislead encoding and impair later recall [-M]
  • Overly dense organizers can themselves overload working memory, reproducing the problem they were meant to solve [~M]
  • For learners with strong prior knowledge or fluent note-taking skills, provided structure adds little and may reduce generative processing Expertise reverses the benefit of instructional support. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Partial guided notes: headings and key terms provided; definitions, examples, and relationships left blank for completion
  • Matrix organizers: rows and columns for comparing cases, theories, or events — especially strong for integration across items
  • Advance organizer + guided notes: a global preview before the lecture, then a completion scaffold during it
  • Student-completed organizers: learners fill in an empty framework as a generative activity, shifting from scaffold to task
  • Post-lecture organizers: provided after note-taking for learners who need to practice independent structuring first

Target Learners¶

  • Novices who cannot yet distinguish central from peripheral content in a lecture Emphasis relevance directs attention during learning. [+M]
  • Learners with processing-speed or working-memory challenges, who lose lecture content while transcribing
  • Students with weak or untaught note-taking skills; benefit diminishes as note-taking fluency grows [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension and retention of structured expository content
  • Integration: seeing relationships among concepts, not just isolated facts
  • Note-taking as a study skill — guided notes model what good notes look like, supporting eventual independent structuring

Instructions¶

  1. Analyze the lecture content to identify its core structure (hierarchy, sequence, comparison) and the handful of ideas that matter most.
  2. Design a partial organizer or guided-note skeleton that makes that structure visible, leaving blanks at the points you most want attended and encoded — blank placement is the attentional signal.
  3. Present a brief orientation to the organizer before the lecture begins, connecting it to prior knowledge where possible (Advance Organizers).
  4. During the lecture, pause at completion points so students can write; prompt brief elaboration of what they record, since generative completion beats verbatim transcription (Annotating).
  5. Keep segments digestible so the organizer's chunks align with the lecture's chunks (Chunking).
  6. Use the completed organizer as a retrieval or application tool afterward — a review prompt, a basis for discussion, or a pre-writing structure (Practice — if unavailable, use application activities directly).

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating prior knowledge — a front-loaded organizer serves the same preparatory function before content begins
  • Annotating texts — the same external-structuring logic applied to reading rather than listening
  • Structured lecture pauses — pauses give learners the time the organizer assumes
  • Teacher-provided graphic map as review
  • Brief lectures on intellectual history illustrated with films, records, and print excerpts
  • Use the coaching framework as a structured discussion guide with a note-taking grid and study group questions

Examples¶

  • Cornell-style guided notes in large introductory lectures: cue column and summary section pre-printed, notes area completed live; widely used in developmental-education and UDL implementations.
  • History matrix organizer: students complete a comparison grid of World War I combatants (goals, alliances, outcomes) during a lecture, then use it to draft an essay.
  • Biology concept-map skeleton: an instructor provides the linked nodes of a photosynthesis map with the connecting propositions blank; students supply the relational terms.
  • UDL-aligned course materials (e.g., CAST exemplars) pair guided notes with slides so learners with processing-speed differences can follow structure without full transcription.
