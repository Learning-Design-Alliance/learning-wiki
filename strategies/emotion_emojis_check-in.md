# Emotion Emojis Check-In

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students express their current feelings by drawing or sharing an emoji that represents their emotional state. Volunteers then share their emoji and explain how it reflects their current feelings. The activity functions as a low-stakes [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) that surfaces affect at the start of a session and builds a shared emotional vocabulary.

## Design Implications

Naming an emotion — even via a simple visual symbol — supports affect labeling, which reliably reduces the intensity of felt emotion and improves self-regulation [~M]. The emoji format lowers the barrier to entry: students who would not volunteer a verbal disclosure can still participate non-verbally, making the activity a practical entry point for social-emotional learning. Its value depends on voluntary sharing and a supportive climate; compulsory disclosure can backfire.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to emojis (drawn or digital) and a shared display surface (whiteboard, slide, LMS poll)
- 2–3 minutes for individual reflection before any sharing
- A classroom climate where declining to share is explicitly acceptable

#### Constraints
- Coerced or graded sharing undermines the safety the activity depends on and suppresses honest responses [-M]
- A limited emoji set can flatten complex or mixed emotions; students may disengage when no option fits [~W]
- Without follow-up from the teacher (acknowledging, adjusting the lesson), the check-in becomes ritual and students stop taking it seriously [-W]
- Repeated public sharing can expose vulnerable students; anonymity options (private submission, aggregate display) mitigate this

#### Implementation Variability
- Anonymous digital polls (Mentimeter, Google Forms) with only aggregate results shown
- Mood meters or emoji scales (e.g., the RULER Mood Meter) replacing single emojis for finer granularity
- Private journal variant: students select an emoji in a notebook, sharing only with the teacher
- Entry/exit ticket format to track emotional trends across a unit

### Target Learners
- All ages; especially effective for pre-K through middle grades where emotional vocabulary is still developing [+W]
- Students with limited verbal expression skills or language barriers, for whom the visual format removes a participation obstacle
- Less suitable, without adaptation, for adolescents who may find public emoji sharing juvenile; anonymous or small-group variants work better [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional awareness and literacy: building vocabulary for internal states
- Self-regulation: affect labeling as an early step in managing emotion [~M]
- Classroom community and belonging: normalizing the expression of feelings [belonging interventions improve outcomes](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Formative awareness for the teacher: gauging readiness to learn before instruction

### Instructions
1. Display a curated set of 6–10 emojis representing a range of emotional states and intensities.
2. Give students 2–3 minutes of individual reflection to select or draw the emoji that best matches their current state.
3. Invite — never require — volunteers to share their emoji and explain their choice; model this first with your own.
4. Acknowledge what was shared and, where warranted, adjust pacing or tone in response.
5. Close the loop later in the session or at dismissal with a brief re-check to reinforce emotional monitoring.

## Examples
- Elementary teachers use a daily emoji board (e.g., a whiteboard grid students initial) as a morning routine to gauge class readiness and surface students needing support.
- Online instructors open synchronous sessions with a one-click emoji poll in Zoom or Mentimeter, displaying aggregate results so individuals are not exposed.
- Secondary advisory programs use private emoji check-ins via Google Forms, with the advisor following up individually with students who report persistent negative states.
