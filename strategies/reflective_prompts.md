# Reflective Prompts

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Reflective prompts are structured questions posed at the end of a lesson, upon submission of major work, or at milestones within a project. They direct learners to articulate what they learned, what strategies worked, what they would do differently, and what questions remain. By requiring learners to generate responses rather than select them, prompts trigger the self-explanation and self-monitoring processes that turn raw experience into transferable understanding.

## Design Implications

Reflection prompts work because they force the generative processing that passive experience skips: articulating *why* something worked or *how* one's understanding changed produces better conceptual learning than re-exposure alone [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. Prompts that target process — what strategies were used, how effort was allocated — also build self-regulatory habits, since learners who monitor their own approach adjust it more effectively over time [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Prompt design matters more than prompt frequency: specific, process-focused questions ("What was the hardest step, and why?") outperform generic ones ("How did that go?").

### Context
#### Requirements
- Protected time at the end of a lesson, project milestone, or submission point — reflection squeezed into leftover minutes produces surface responses
- A small set of varied, specific prompts matched to the learning goal (content, process, or affect)
- A response format: written, verbal, or digital; sentence stems scaffold learners new to reflection
- A classroom culture where honest reflection (including admitting confusion) is safe and valued

#### Constraints
- Generic or repetitive prompts quickly become ritual; learners produce formulaic answers with no generative processing [-M]
- Reflection without any follow-through — prompts whose answers are never revisited or acted on — teaches learners that reflection is performative [-W]
- Novices may lack the vocabulary and metacognitive awareness to reflect meaningfully without stems or modeled examples; unstructured "reflect on your learning" tasks can produce vague responses [~M]
- Over-frequent reflection adds time cost with diminishing returns; brief, spaced prompts outperform long end-of-unit essays [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Exit tickets**: one prompt at lesson end, collected and scanned for instructional adjustment
- **Process portfolios**: prompts answered at milestones and revisited at project close, making growth visible
- **Oral or digital formats**: audio reflections, discussion-board threads, or one-minute papers for learners who struggle with writing
- **Prompt menus**: learners choose from several prompts, increasing relevance and ownership

### Target Learners
- Learners old enough to verbalize their own thinking; younger learners need stems and modeled responses
- Students developing self-regulated learning habits, who benefit from prompts that direct attention to strategy and effort [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Advanced learners may gain less from content-recap prompts and more from prompts about transfer and open questions [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition: awareness of one's own comprehension, strategies, and misconceptions [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's approach
- Consolidation and retention: articulating learning strengthens memory traces
- Formative insight for the instructor: responses reveal student thinking and misconceptions, informing subsequent instruction

### Instructions
1. Choose 1–3 prompts matched to the goal — content ("What is one thing you now understand that you didn't before?"), process ("What strategy did you use, and would you use it again?"), or forward-looking ("What question do you still have?").
2. Allocate dedicated time at lesson end or submission; treat it as part of the task, not an add-on.
3. Provide sentence stems for learners new to reflection, and model a response aloud once.
4. Collect responses and act on them — address common confusions next session or feed them into [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) — so learners see that reflection has consequences.
5. Vary prompts across sessions to prevent ritualization; revisit earlier responses at project close to make growth visible.

## Related Strategies
- **Exit tickets** — the briefest implementation: one prompt, one minute, collected at the door
- **Learning journals** — sustained written reflection across a term, of which single prompts are the atomic unit
- **One-minute papers** — a research-validated classroom variant focused on "what was the muddiest point?"

## Examples
- At the end of a history lesson, students respond to "What is one thing you learned, and one question you still have?" on an exit ticket; the teacher sorts responses to plan the next session's opening.
- Before submitting a science project, students answer "What is one part of your work you are proud of, and what would you do differently next time?" — combining affective and process reflection.
- **BloomBoard / edX reflective assessments**: MOOC and professional-learning platforms embed end-of-module prompts ("How will you apply this?") that learners answer before receiving the next unit.
