Evaluating Online Sources¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Evaluating online sources is a strategy that teaches learners to judge the credibility of web information by leaving the source itself to investigate it — checking who is behind it, what other outlets say about it, and whether claims are corroborated. The core routine is lateral reading: professional fact-checkers open new tabs and search for information about a source rather than reading deeper within it Wineburg & McGrew, 2019 [+S].

Design Implications¶

Lateral reading outperforms the checklist approaches (e.g., CRAAP tests) historically taught in schools, because checklists evaluate surface features of a page while expert fact-checkers evaluate the source's reputation and track record across the web [+S]. Instruction should model the strategy with authentic, messy web content — including sources designed to look credible — rather than sanitized examples Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]. Learners benefit from Think-Aloud modeling of the evaluation moves before independent practice.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to the live web (or a curated replica) so learners can actually leave the page and search
  • A small repertoire of concrete moves: find the source's About page, search the organization's name plus "funding" or "bias," check claims against independent fact-checkers
  • Instructor modeling of expert evaluation decisions, not just a rubric
  • Practice with deliberately varied source types — advocacy sites, news, peer-reviewed work, sponsored content

Constraints¶

  • Checklist-style evaluation (currency, relevance, authority surface features) produces poor discrimination between professional and amateur sources [-S] — students taught only checklists rate professionally designed misinformation as credible
  • Lateral reading requires prior knowledge of the information ecosystem; learners unfamiliar with news outlets, think tanks, or domain suffixes cannot interpret what they find [~M]
  • Effectiveness drops when practice uses only obviously credible or obviously fake sources; learners need near-miss cases to build calibration
  • Heavy evaluation routines can discourage engagement with legitimate but imperfect sources, producing blanket skepticism rather than calibrated trust [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Lateral reading routines (Stanford History Education Group's Civic Online Reasoning curriculum): short, repeated lessons with real websites
  • Corroboration tasks: learners triangulate a claim across three independent sources before using it
  • Reverse image search and provenance checks for visual media
  • Error analysis with planted misinformation: learners evaluate sources containing deliberate flaws, similar to Erroneous Examples in problem solving

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and post-secondary students, who encounter most information through search and social media [+S]
  • Effective across ability levels; low-prior-knowledge learners gain the most from explicit modeling of the moves [~M]
  • Less transfer for very young learners without the search fluency to execute lateral moves independently

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Information literacy: judging source credibility and bias
  • Critical reading: distinguishing evidence from assertion
  • Civic reasoning: making informed judgments about contested public claims

Instructions¶

  1. Model the strategy with a Think-Aloud: open an unfamiliar website and verbalize each evaluation move — who is behind this, what do others say about them, is this claim corroborated?
  2. Contrast cases: present a professional-looking advocacy site and a plain-looking news report; have learners evaluate both, then reveal the actual credibility of each Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]
  3. Have learners perform lateral reading on new sources, then Self-Explanation their verdicts — articulating why a source earned or lost trust Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
  4. Fade support: move from guided whole-class evaluation to independent evaluation with a brief prompt card, consistent with Scaffolding and Fading
  5. Integrate into authentic tasks — research papers, Argumentation exercises, or Case Studies — so evaluation is applied, not isolated

Related Strategies¶

  • A Finder's Guide to Facts — a complementary framework for assessing factual claims
  • 3-Source Rule — a corroboration heuristic that operationalizes triangulation
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — background knowledge of the information ecosystem determines what learners can infer during evaluation

Examples¶

  • Stanford History Education Group — Civic Online Reasoning — Free curriculum built on lateral reading research; lessons use real websites and professional fact-checker strategies [+S]
  • NewsGuard — Browser extension applying journalist-written credibility ratings; used in media literacy instruction as a scaffold for lateral reading
  • Snopes / FactCheck.org practice tasks — Learners verify a viral claim by tracing it to its origin and checking independent fact-checker verdicts
