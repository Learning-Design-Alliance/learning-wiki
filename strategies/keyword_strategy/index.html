Keyword Strategy¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 10 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 causal, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

The keyword strategy is a two-stage mnemonic for learning vocabulary and paired associations. First, the learner identifies a familiar keyword that sounds similar to (or otherwise cues) the unfamiliar term — e.g., car for the Spanish carne (meat). Second, the learner forms an interactive mental image linking the keyword to the meaning — a picture of a steak in a car. The strategy works by converting an arbitrary verbal association into a concrete, imageable one, exploiting the superior durability of visual and elaborative encoding Dunlosky et al. rate imagery-based mnemonics as useful for paired-associate learning. [+M].

Design Implications¶

The keyword strategy is a form of elaborative encoding: it attaches new, arbitrary material to well-established knowledge structures, giving retrieval two independent pathways (sound-alike cue and image) instead of one. Its effectiveness depends on the quality of the keyword — familiar, concrete, and phonologically close — and on the interactivity of the image; two objects merely placed side by side in a picture produce far weaker recall than one acting on the other [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A familiar, concrete keyword with acoustic or orthographic overlap with the target term
  • An interactive image in which the keyword and the meaning act on each other, not merely co-occur
  • Explicit teaching of the procedure itself — learners must be shown how to generate keywords and images (Demonstration), since self-generated mnemonics without instruction often fail
  • Regular retrieval practice to maintain the association over time Spaced retrieval maintains associations that mnemonics alone do not. [+S]

Constraints¶

  • Keyword recall can outlast meaning recall: learners may remember the keyword and image but forget the actual foreign word, producing a retrieval failure at the critical link [~M]
  • Benefits often shrink on delayed tests relative to rote learning in some studies, so the strategy should be paired with spaced review rather than treated as sufficient [-M]
  • Poorly chosen keywords (abstract, unfamiliar, or phonologically distant) yield little benefit; some words simply have no good keyword
  • Less suitable for abstract concepts, complex syntax, or conceptual understanding — it supports recall of associations, not comprehension or transfer Self-explanation, not imagery, drives conceptual understanding. [~M]
  • Image generation consumes working memory; for learners with weak vocabularies in the target domain, the keyword itself may not be firmly encoded Chunking reduces working memory load only when the chunk is familiar. [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Teacher-provided vs. learner-generated: teacher-supplied keywords and images are more reliable for novices; learner-generated versions produce slightly better recall when learners have been trained in the procedure [+M]
  • Visual vs. verbal elaboration: pictures work better than verbal elaboration for concrete nouns; verbal sentence elaboration can substitute for abstract terms
  • Group vs. individual: works in whole-class formats (teacher displays the image) and as an independent study technique
  • Extended keyword method: keywords can also cue grammatical gender or parts of speech (e.g., all feminine nouns pictured with a female character)

Target Learners¶

  • Learners acquiring new vocabulary, especially a second language, where large bodies of arbitrary paired associations must be memorized [+S]
  • Elementary and secondary students, and students with learning disabilities or weak rote-memory skills, for whom the strategy produces some of the largest gains [+M]
  • Less valuable for advanced learners who can encode vocabulary through rich semantic context; the extra imagery step can become redundant overhead [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Recall of paired associations: foreign-language vocabulary, scientific terminology, definitions
  • Rapid initial acquisition of large vocabularies where meaning must be anchored before deeper processing
  • Not appropriate as the sole goal for conceptual understanding, application, or transfer

Instructions¶

  1. Select the target term and identify a familiar, concrete keyword that sounds like part of it.
  2. Demonstrate the full procedure on one or two examples, thinking aloud: keyword → image → meaning.
  3. Present or elicit an interactive image linking keyword and meaning; have learners describe what is happening in the picture (Annotating or drawing the image strengthens encoding).
  4. Have learners generate their own keywords and images for subsequent terms, with feedback on keyword quality.
  5. Follow with retrieval practice — recall the meaning from the term and the term from the meaning — distributed over time Spaced retrieval maintains associations that mnemonics alone do not. [+S].

Related Strategies¶

  • Acronyms and Acrostics — the other major verbal mnemonic family; organizes lists rather than paired associations
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the keyword strategy works only when the keyword itself is firmly anchored in prior knowledge
  • Structured discussions that anchor new vocabulary in students' personal experience

Examples¶

  • To teach carline (witch), use the keyword car and show a picture of a witch in a car: recall the keyword (car), the image (a witch driving a car), and retrieve the meaning (witch).
  • Raugh and Atkinson taught Spanish vocabulary with the keyword method (e.g., pato → pot → a duck wearing a pot) and found substantially better recall than rote rehearsal on both immediate and delayed tests.
  • Anki and similar spaced-repetition flashcard systems can host keyword-plus-image cards, combining the mnemonic with spaced retrieval practice.
