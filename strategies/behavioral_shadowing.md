# Behavioral Shadowing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
An adult shadows a child closely (within arm's reach) to prevent specific harmful behaviors (e.g., biting, hitting) before they occur. The adult remains unobtrusive, avoiding special attention or punishment, while ensuring the safety of the child and classmates. The goal is to break the cycle of harmful behavior and teach alternative strategies, drawing on functional behavior assessment and positive behavior support principles [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md).

## Design Implications

Behavioral shadowing is a safety-first intervention that also functions as a data-collection and teaching opportunity: proximity lets the adult interrupt the behavior chain early, identify antecedents, and prompt replacement behaviors before escalation [Cooper et al.'s applied behavior analysis framework treats antecedent manipulation as a primary intervention strategy.](../theories/behaviorism.md) [+M]. Because attention can inadvertently reinforce problem behavior, the shadow must respond to harmful acts neutrally and reserve warmth and praise for prosocial alternatives [Feedback is most effective when it targets the process and replacement behavior rather than the person.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A dedicated adult (teacher, aide, or trained volunteer) with time to maintain close proximity for extended periods
- A clear operational definition of target behaviors and a shadowing protocol specifying when to block, prompt, and reinforce
- Consistent, unobtrusive observation with data collection (ABC records) to identify triggers and patterns
- A plan for teaching and reinforcing alternative communication and interaction strategies during shadowing ([Modeling](../elements/modeling.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))

#### Constraints
- Labor-intensive; requires staffing that many classrooms cannot sustain, which limits scalability [-M]
- If the shadow's presence or reactions become a source of attention, proximity itself can reinforce the target behavior [~M]
- Does not address underlying causes (communication deficits, trauma, skill gaps) without a parallel intervention; shadowing alone produces limited maintenance once withdrawn [-M]
- Can be perceived as stigmatizing or intrusive by the child or family if not explained and framed as support
- Effectiveness drops sharply if implementation is inconsistent across staff or settings [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Intensity can be faded over time — from arm's-reach shadowing to proximity supervision to independent monitoring — as the child sustains incident-free periods [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility to the learner.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- Shadowing can target specific high-risk contexts only (transitions, free play) rather than the full day
- Peers or family members can be coached to apply the same prompts, generalizing the intervention beyond the shadow

### Target Learners
- Young children (preschool or early elementary) exhibiting behaviors that can harm themselves or others, such as biting or hitting
- Children with limited verbal skills who may be using aggression as a form of communication; shadowing creates teachable moments for replacement communication
- Children whose behavior has not responded to whole-class supports alone (Tier 2/3 in a positive behavior support framework)

### Target Learning Goals
- Prevent harmful behaviors and ensure the safety of all children in the classroom
- Identify triggers and antecedents for aggressive behavior through close observation
- Teach alternative strategies for communication and interaction, breaking the cycle of negative behavior patterns

### Instructions
1. Define the target behavior(s) operationally and conduct a brief functional assessment to hypothesize triggers and functions.
2. Assign a trained adult to shadow the child within arm's reach during identified high-risk periods, remaining neutral and unobtrusive.
3. Block or interrupt the behavior chain early; respond to harmful acts with minimal attention and no punitive display.
4. Prompt and [Model](../elements/modeling.md) a replacement behavior (e.g., words, a gesture, or a break card) at the first sign of the trigger, and reinforce it immediately.
5. Provide calm, specific [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) on the replacement behavior rather than the child's identity or the misbehavior.
6. Collect ABC data throughout; adjust the protocol as triggers become clearer.
7. [Fade](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) proximity gradually as the child sustains incident-free periods, transferring monitoring to the classroom routine.

## Examples
- A preschool teacher shadows a child who has bitten classmates on two occasions, blocking attempts and prompting the child to use words or a "help" card to express frustration. Shadowing is faded from full days to transitions only after two incident-free weeks.
- An aide shadows a kindergartner during recess who hits peers when excluded from games; the aide prompts entry phrases ("Can I play?") and reinforces successful joins, then coaches peers to invite the child.
