# Explicit Strategy Instruction

## Description
Explicit strategy instruction names a specific procedure learners can use to accomplish a task (e.g., summarizing, self-questioning, drawing a diagram), demonstrates its use, and provides guided practice until learners can apply it independently. Crucially, it teaches not just the *how* but the *when* and *why* — conditional knowledge about which strategy fits which task — because strategies taught without conditions are rarely transferred [Strategy instruction that includes conditional knowledge produces better transfer than strategy instruction alone.](../claims/strategy-instruction-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The goal is not the strategy itself but self-regulated use: learners who select, monitor, and adjust strategies on their own.

## Design Implications

Strategy instruction works because it converts tacit expert procedures into teachable steps, reducing the unguided search that overwhelms novices [Example-problem sequences reduce cognitive load for novices.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the full cycle — naming, modeling, guided practice, independent use — being completed; teaching a strategy's steps without modeling and practice produces knowledge *about* the strategy but not skill *in* using it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A well-specified strategy with a small number of memorable steps
- [Modeling](../elements/coaching.md) of the strategy in use, ideally with the instructor verbalizing decisions ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
- Guided practice with feedback before independent application ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Feedback](../elements/assessment.md))
- Explicit discussion of when the strategy applies and when it does not (conditional knowledge)

#### Constraints
- Strategies taught as rote procedures, without conditional knowledge, show weak transfer to new tasks and content [Strategy instruction effects are weaker when conditional knowledge is not taught.](../claims/strategy-instruction-improves-learning.md) [-M]
- Effects fade when instruction is brief; multi-session programs outperform single interventions [Meta-analyses find stronger effects for extended strategy programs.](../claims/strategy-instruction-improves-learning.md) [~M]
- For learners with high prior knowledge, strategy instruction can be redundant and even depress performance [The expertise-reversal effect means guidance that helps novices can hinder experts.](../claims/expertise-reversal-guidance-hurts-experts.md) [~M]
- Time-intensive: modeling and guided practice consume instructional time that competes with content coverage

#### Implementation Variability
- **Embedded vs. standalone:** strategies taught within authentic content tasks transfer better than those taught in separate "study skills" lessons [~M]
- **Reciprocal formats:** learners take turns applying the strategy with peer feedback, as in reciprocal teaching
- **Strategy menus:** learners build a repertoire and practice *selecting* among strategies, not just executing one
- **Self-regulated framing:** pair strategy steps with self-monitoring prompts so learners evaluate whether the strategy is working ([Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md))

### Target Learners
- Novices and struggling learners who lack effective default approaches to a task [Strategy instruction benefits low-achieving learners most.](../claims/strategy-instruction-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Younger learners, who rarely invent effective strategies spontaneously
- Less beneficial for experts, who already possess and automate effective procedures [The expertise-reversal effect means guidance that helps novices can hinder experts.](../claims/expertise-reversal-guidance-hurts-experts.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural fluency in domain tasks (comprehension, problem solving, writing)
- Metacognitive skill: monitoring comprehension and performance
- Transfer and self-directed learning: independent strategy selection and adaptation

### Instructions
1. **Name and justify the strategy** — state what it is called and why it works, connecting to a task learners recognize ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can frame where the strategy fits)
2. **Model it in use** — demonstrate on a real task while verbalizing decisions and self-monitoring ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
3. **Clarify conditions of use** — discuss explicitly what kinds of tasks the strategy fits and what alternatives exist ([Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) can map the strategy onto familiar situations)
4. **Guide practice** — learners apply the strategy with prompts and feedback, with support gradually withdrawn ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
5. **Transfer to independent use** — learners select and apply strategies on their own, with debriefing on what worked ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) — learners explain their choices)

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching** (Palincsar & Brown, 1984): seventh-grade struggling readers, taught summarizing, questioning, clarifying, and predicting through teacher modeling followed by student-led group practice, gained up to two years in comprehension over ~20 sessions.
- **Self-regulated strategy development (SRSD) in writing** (Graham & Harris): teachers model planning strategies like POW+TREE for persuasive essays, then fade support as students write independently; a widely replicated approach in special education.
- **A teacher names "get unstuck" moves in math**: after presenting a problem designed to stall students, the teacher elicits and labels what students did ("You pulled the important information out and organized it into a chart"), building a shared, named repertoire of strategies the class can later invoke deliberately.
