# Learning Strategy & Tool "Fair"

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A Learning Strategy & Tool "Fair" is a structured exploration event — inspired by an instrument "petting zoo" — where learners circulate among stations, each featuring a different learning strategy or tool, and try each one briefly on authentic material. The goal is low-stakes experimentation: learners discover what strategies exist, experience using them firsthand, and leave with informed judgments about which to adopt. Product developers can embed the same structure digitally by surfacing suggested strategies based on learner needs and performance.

## Design Implications

The fair operationalizes learner autonomy: choice over which strategies to sample supports intrinsic motivation and investment in the learning process [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Because learners try each strategy on real content rather than hearing about it, the format pairs brief [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) with immediate hands-on [Application](../elements/application.md), which produces far better retention of a procedure than observation alone. The exploration phase also builds metacognitive knowledge — learners compare strategies against their own experience, which is the raw material for later self-regulated strategy selection [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated set of 5–8 strategy/tool stations, each with a one-minute orientation and a small authentic task to try it on
- Time allocated for circulation and experimentation (typically 30–60 minutes)
- A reflection or debrief structure where learners record which strategies they found useful and why ([Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md))
- For digital implementations: adaptive recommendations that surface candidate strategies based on learner performance data

#### Constraints
- Too many options without curation induces choice overload; learners sample superficially and retain nothing [-M] — cap stations and provide a sampling guide
- One-off exposure rarely changes behavior: without follow-up prompts to apply a chosen strategy, adoption rates are low [-M]
- Strategies demonstrated out of context (on toy tasks) may be rejected or misjudged; each station must use material from the actual course or domain
- Preparation cost is high — gathering diverse, quality stations is the main implementation burden

#### Implementation Variability
- **Physical fair:** stations around a room, learners circulate in small groups with a passport/record sheet
- **Digital fair:** a "strategy carousel" in an LMS or product, with short interactive trials and adaptive suggestions
- **Course-embedded version:** each station tied to an upcoming assignment, so learners immediately apply one chosen strategy
- **Student-run fair:** learners who have mastered a strategy staff the stations, deepening their own understanding through teaching

### Target Learners
- Learners who are unsure which strategies work best for them — the exploration format builds the comparison data they lack
- Students entering a new domain or level (first-year, new discipline) where prior study habits may not transfer
- Learners with low task value benefit from trying strategies on personally relevant material [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Less useful for learners who already have a well-developed strategy repertoire; the fair may feel redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive knowledge: knowing what strategies exist and under what conditions each is effective
- Self-regulated learning: building a personal repertoire from which to make deliberate strategy choices
- Motivation and engagement: autonomy and low-stakes experimentation increase willingness to try new approaches [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Curate 5–8 stations, each featuring one strategy or tool (e.g., [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md), [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md), spaced-repetition flashcards, concept mapping) with a brief [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of the strategy in use
2. At each station, learners apply the strategy immediately to a small piece of authentic course material ([Application](../elements/application.md)) — 5–7 minutes per station
3. Learners record a quick rating and note at each station: what the strategy demands, what it felt like, when it might be useful
4. Close with [Individual Reflection](../elements/individual-reflection.md): each learner selects one or two strategies to adopt, states when they will use them, and schedules the first application
5. Follow up in subsequent sessions with [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) on whether learners actually used their chosen strategies, and prompt [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) of what worked and what didn't

## Related Strategies
- Strategy instruction and modeling sessions — a fair complements explicit teaching by adding experiential sampling before formal instruction
- Portfolio or learning-log approaches — the reflection records from a fair feed naturally into ongoing self-monitoring artifacts

## Examples
- **First-year seminar study-skills fair:** stations for the [SQ3R reading method](https://www.mindtools.com/aordocj/sq3r-reading-method), Anki spaced-repetition flashcards ([https://apps.ankiweb.net](https://apps.ankiweb.net)), Cornell note-taking, and concept mapping, each tried on a shared course reading
- **Digital strategy carousel:** a learning platform presents short interactive trials of strategies (retrieval practice, interleaving, dual coding) matched to the learner's recent performance data, with prompts to commit to one for the next assignment
- **Library "tool petting zoo":** academic libraries run hands-on sessions where students sample citation managers (Zotero, [https://www.zotero.org](https://www.zotero.org)), note-taking tools, and focus apps on their own research projects
