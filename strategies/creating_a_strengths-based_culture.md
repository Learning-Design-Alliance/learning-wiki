# Creating a Strengths-Based Culture

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A strengths-based culture positions learner assets — talents, interests, and existing competencies — as the starting point for instruction rather than deficits to be remediated. Instructors make strengths visible and usable: learners identify and discuss strengths with peers, set goals that deploy those strengths, work on passion projects, and see varied assessment options that let different strengths count. The aim is to maximize motivation, contribution, and confidence by building on what learners do well while they tackle what is hard.

## Design Implications

A strengths orientation supports motivation through competence and autonomy: learners who see their strengths valued experience greater perceived competence and choose to invest effort more willingly [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. It also functions as a belonging intervention — publicly recognizing each learner's contribution signals that every member has a valued place in the classroom community [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. However, strengths talk must connect to academic work; celebration alone, without goal-setting and application, produces positive affect but little learning gain.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Structured opportunities for learners to identify, name, and share strengths (e.g., character-strength inventories, peer interviews)
- Goal-setting routines that explicitly link strengths to academic targets
- Varied assessment options so different strengths can demonstrate the same learning goal ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md))
- Materials and time for passion projects and learner choice ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md))

#### Constraints
- Strengths focus without corrective feedback leaves errors unaddressed; feedback is most effective when it targets the task and process, not the person [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [~M] — praise framed as trait praise ("you're so smart") can undermine persistence compared to process feedback
- In classrooms with wide ability ranges, public strength-sharing can become invidious comparison if the same narrow strengths (speed, correctness) are the only ones celebrated
- Requires sustained instructor mindset shift; one-off "strengths week" activities decay without routines ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- Time spent on strengths identification competes with instructional time; benefits depend on the strengths being *used* in academic tasks, not merely catalogued

#### Implementation Variability
- Whole-class routines: "expert of the week" shares, strengths walls, peer strength-spotting during [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)
- Individual level: strengths-linked goal-setting conferences, passion projects with [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) matched to learner profiles
- Assessment level: choice of product (essay, podcast, model, presentation) against common criteria

### Target Learners
- Struggling or disengaged learners who have accumulated histories of deficit framing; asset framing rebuilds perceived competence [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]
- Learners at risk of marginalization, for whom visible valued contribution supports belonging [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- All learners benefit, but effects are strongest where prior experiences of failure have suppressed motivation

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-management and self-knowledge: recognizing one's own assets and deploying them deliberately
- Motivation and engagement: building confidence to attempt challenging work
- Collaboration: learners learn to draw on complementary strengths in group work

### Instructions
1. Open with routines that surface strengths: peer interviews, strength inventories, or a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) that asks what learners did well.
2. Make strengths public and celebrated — strengths wall, "expert of the week," strength-spotting during [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md).
3. Have each learner set a goal and specify *how* a named strength will be used to reach it; revisit in [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) conferences.
4. Offer [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) and passion projects so learners work from strength on authentic tasks.
5. Vary assessment options so the same learning goal can be demonstrated through different strengths ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
6. Pair strengths recognition with process-focused feedback on growth areas; model this by naming your own strengths *and* current challenges.

## Related Strategies
- Growth-mindset framing — strengths talk should emphasize developable capacities, not fixed traits
- Passion-project and inquiry approaches — the primary vehicle through which strengths get used rather than merely named

## Examples
- **Positive education at Geelong Grammar School** — whole-school adoption of character-strengths identification and use, part of the program evaluated in Seligman et al. (2009).
- **"Expert of the week"** — a rotating learner shares a talent and teaches peers, making diverse (non-academic) strengths publicly valued.
- **Strengths-linked goal conferences** — learners name a strength, set an academic goal, and write how the strength will be deployed; revisited at mid-unit check-ins.
