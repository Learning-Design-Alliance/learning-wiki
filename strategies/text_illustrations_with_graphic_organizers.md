# Text Illustrations With Graphic Organizers

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Text illustrations with graphic organizers pair written expository or argumentative text with a spatial diagram — a matrix, hierarchy, flowchart, or concept map — that represents the text's key ideas and their relationships. The organizer may be provided by the instructor as a reading aid or constructed by learners after reading as a summarization activity. Unlike decorative illustrations, these organizers encode relational structure (comparisons, sequences, classifications) that prose conveys only linearly.

## Design Implications

Pairing text with a structure-preserving graphic exploits dual-channel processing: verbal and visual representations are encoded separately and can be integrated, yielding richer mental models than either alone [Multimedia materials that combine words and pictures improve learning over words alone.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+S]. Organizers are most effective when they mirror the *rhetorical structure* of the text (comparison → matrix; sequence → flowchart) rather than serving as generic decoration. Learner-constructed organizers generally outperform passive viewing of instructor-provided ones, though provided organizers help novices most [Providing learners with graphic organizers can be less effective than having them construct their own.](../claims/learner-constructed-graphic-organizers-outperform-provided.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Text with an identifiable relational structure worth externalizing (compare/contrast, hierarchy, causal chain, classification)
- An organizer type matched to that structure, not chosen arbitrarily
- Brief instruction in how to read or build the organizer, so the diagram itself does not become a decoding burden
- For learner-constructed versions: a model or worked example of organizer construction ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))

#### Constraints
- Poorly matched organizers add extraneous load: learners must reconcile a diagram that contradicts or distorts the text's structure [Organizers that duplicate or distort text structure impose extraneous processing.](../claims/mismatched-graphic-organizers-increase-extraneous-load.md) [-M]
- Decorative or redundant graphics that merely repeat text verbatim can depress learning by encouraging shallow processing [Graphics that are decorative rather than explanatory do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-M]
- Overly dense organizers with excessive detail can overload working memory; keep to the text's core structure [Chunking information into organized units reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]
- For learners with strong prior knowledge, provided organizers can be redundant and interfere with fluent text processing [Guidance becomes less effective — and can reverse — as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Provided (instructor-built)**: embedded before or alongside reading as an advance organizer; best for novices and dense texts
- **Learner-constructed (post-reading)**: functions as generative summarization; best for consolidation and transfer
- **Partially completed (skeleton) organizers**: learners fill in nodes or links; a middle ground that fades support
- **Collaborative construction**: pairs or groups negotiate the organizer, adding elaborative discussion

### Target Learners
- Novices reading unfamiliar expository text, who lack the schemas to extract structure unaided [Organizing text structure externally supports comprehension for low-prior-knowledge readers.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Struggling readers and learners with disabilities, for whom explicit structure display reduces processing demands [Mnemonic and structured instruction improves recall for students with disabilities.](../claims/mnemonic-instruction-improves-recall-for-students-with-disabilities.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for expert readers of a domain, who build structure representations automatically [Guidance becomes less effective — and can reverse — as learner expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of relational structure: comparisons, hierarchies, causal sequences in expository text
- Summarization and knowledge consolidation after reading
- Transfer-appropriate organization of knowledge for later writing or argumentation

### Instructions
1. Identify the dominant rhetorical structure of the text (comparison, sequence, hierarchy, cause–effect).
2. Select the organizer type that maps onto that structure; sketch it before the lesson ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
3. Decide the support level: fully provided, skeleton, or blank, based on learner expertise ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).
4. Introduce the organizer with a brief demonstration of how it maps onto a sample passage ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
5. Have learners read the text with the organizer, or complete it after reading ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
6. Debrief by having learners explain relationships shown in the organizer in their own words, converting the spatial representation back into elaborated verbal knowledge.

## Examples
- **Reading apprenticeship in secondary science** — students complete a compare/contrast matrix after reading paired passages on photosynthesis and respiration, making the shared structure visible.
- **[Newsela](https://newsela.com)** — leveled expository articles paired with teacher-provided graphic organizer templates keyed to article structure.
- **[Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org)** — published library of text-structure organizers (cause/effect chains, problem/solution frames) for K–12 expository reading instruction.
- **History argument writing** — students map claims, evidence, and counterarguments from a source set onto an argument diagram before drafting an essay.
