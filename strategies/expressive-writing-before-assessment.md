# Expressive Writing Before Assessment

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Expressive writing before assessment asks students to spend roughly 10 minutes immediately before an exam or high-stakes task writing openly about their worries, fears, and anxious thoughts about the upcoming performance. The act of externalizing worry is thought to "offload" intrusive ruminations that would otherwise consume working memory during the assessment, freeing cognitive resources for the task itself. It is typically unstructured and private — students are told their writing will not be graded or read.

## Design Implications

Test anxiety impairs performance by occupying working memory with task-irrelevant worry, and degrading effects of cognitive load on performance are well established [Cognitive overload degrades learning and performance.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Expressive writing targets this mechanism directly: Ramirez and Beilock found that students high in test anxiety who wrote about their worries before a final exam improved their grade by roughly one letter grade step relative to controls, while control writing topics (describing an unrelated image or event) produced no benefit [+S]. The effect is strongest when the writing is timed to sit *between* the anxiety-inducing situation and the assessment, so worries are expressed once they are activated but before they can interfere.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuinely high-stakes or evaluative situation — the intervention targets anxiety, so there must be anxiety to reduce
- 7–10 minutes of protected time immediately before the assessment
- Privacy assurances: students must believe the writing will not be graded, shared, or evaluated
- A prompt that directs attention to worries about the *upcoming* performance ("write about your thoughts and feelings regarding this exam"), not to unrelated topics
- A classroom norm where the exercise is framed as a proven performance tool, not a remedial or therapeutic activity

#### Constraints
- Benefits are concentrated among students high in test anxiety; for low-anxiety students the exercise shows no benefit and can slightly *hurt* performance, possibly because it induces worry where none existed [-M]
- Writing about an unrelated topic or about the exam's importance (rather than one's worries) does not produce the effect [-S] — the mechanism is worry expression, not generic writing warm-up
- If administered too early (e.g., the night before) or too late (during the exam), the offloading window is missed [~M]
- Unstructured venting that becomes rumination — dwelling on catastrophic outcomes without the closure of an imminent task — can amplify rather than reduce anxiety [~W]
- One-shot use in a low-stakes quiz is unlikely to show measurable effects; the effect sizes in classroom studies come from genuinely consequential exams [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Timing:** immediately before the exam (Ramirez & Beilock protocol) or at the start of a test-taking session; some implementations repeat the exercise across multiple exams in a term with sustained benefits
- **Domain-specific versions:** math-anxious students benefit from writing about worries before math tasks specifically, with effects tied to reducing math anxiety rather than general anxiety [+M]
- **Group vs. individual:** the standard protocol is individual and private; group administration works if privacy of content is preserved
- **Complementary framing:** can be paired with arousal reappraisal ("your racing heart is your body preparing to perform") as an alternative or additive emotion-regulation route

### Target Learners
- Students high in test anxiety — the effect is moderated by anxiety level, with anxious students showing the largest gains [+S]
- Math-anxious students facing quantitative assessments [+M]
- High-achieving students under pressure, who tend to have the working-memory capacity that worry most readily displaces [~M]
- Not recommended as a universal routine for low-anxiety students, who gain nothing and may lose a little [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Performance protection: ensuring that already-learned material is retrieved accurately under pressure, rather than adding new knowledge
- Emotional self-regulation: building a reusable strategy students can deploy independently across their academic lives
- Assessment validity: reducing the gap between what students know and what they demonstrate under evaluative conditions

### Instructions
1. **Frame the exercise** — tell students that research shows writing about worries before a test improves performance, and that their writing is completely private and ungraded. Framing matters: the exercise works partly because students understand its purpose.
2. **Protect 7–10 minutes immediately before the assessment** — the timing window between anxiety activation and test start is critical.
3. **Give the prompt** — "Take out a piece of paper and write for the next ten minutes about your thoughts and feelings regarding the exam you are about to take. What are you worried about? What is on your mind?"
4. **Guarantee privacy** — collect papers face down or have students keep them; never grade, read, or comment on content. If a [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) routine exists in the course, keep this separate from it.
5. **Proceed directly to the assessment** — do not insert discussion or debrief between writing and testing.
6. **Repeat across assessments** — treat it as a reusable [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) routine rather than a one-time fix, so students internalize the strategy for independent use.

## Examples
- **Ramirez & Beilock (2011) classroom study** — Ninth-grade students in a U.S. high school wrote about their exam worries for 10 minutes before their first final exam; anxious students' performance improved such that the anxiety–performance relationship was eliminated, with effects persisting on a follow-up exam.
- **Park, Ramirez, & Beilock (2014) math protocol** — College students high in math anxiety wrote about their worries before a math exam and significantly outperformed anxious controls, with reduced math anxiety mediating the gain.
- **Pre-exam routines in test-prep courses** — Some university learning-strategy centers (e.g., [University of Michigan's Science Learning Center study strategies](https://slc.umich.edu)) incorporate brief pre-exam worry-writing into test-preparation workshops alongside practice testing.
