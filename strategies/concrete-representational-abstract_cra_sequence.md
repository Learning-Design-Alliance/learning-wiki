# Concrete Representational Abstract (CRA) Sequence

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The CRA sequence teaches a concept in three linked stages: first with **concrete** manipulable objects (counters, base-ten blocks, fraction tiles), then with **representational** drawings or diagrams of those objects, and finally with **abstract** symbols (numerals, operators, equations). Each stage models the same underlying structure, so the transition from one to the next is a translation rather than a leap. It is carried out by explicitly connecting the stages — the same problem is solved at each level and the links between representations are named aloud.

## Design Implications

CRA manages the transition from embodied, intuitive understanding to symbolic fluency by distributing the conceptual load across representations [Reducing extraneous cognitive load during instruction improves learning outcomes.](../claims/cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Its effectiveness depends on the *connections* between stages: concrete materials alone do not guarantee learning, and can even burden working memory if learners must simultaneously discover the concept and decode the materials [Manipulatives improve learning only when learners are helped to make the connection between the object and the concept it stands for.](../claims/manipulatives-require-connection-to-concept.md) [+M]. The sequence should be faded deliberately — lingering in the concrete stage too long can leave learners dependent on materials and unable to operate symbolically.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Concrete materials that genuinely map onto the target structure (e.g., base-ten blocks whose place-value grouping mirrors the algorithm)
- Explicit, teacher-led linking language at each transition ("two tens is written 20 because…")
- A plan for fading: criteria for moving from concrete to representational to abstract, not a fixed timetable
- Substantial [Practice](../elements/practice.md) at each stage, especially the abstract stage, since fluency with symbols is the end goal

#### Constraints
- Poorly designed manipulatives (intrusive, novel, or richly decorated objects) can become the focus of attention and *increase* cognitive load rather than reduce it [~M]
- Concrete knowledge does not automatically transfer to abstract notation; without explicit bridging, learners may perform well with materials but fail on symbols [-M]
- Extended concrete-only instruction can promote procedural manipulation of objects without conceptual insight [~M]
- Less effective for learners who already reason symbolically; starting concrete can be redundant and slow [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **CRA-I** (CRA with explicit instruction): teacher demonstration at each stage followed by guided practice — the most researched variant for students with learning disabilities
- **Gradual release within stages**: full demonstration → guided → independent at each representation level
- **Simultaneous linking**: showing concrete, drawn, and symbolic forms side by side rather than strictly sequentially [~M]
- **CSA (Concrete–Semiconcrete–Abstract)**: an older variant where "semiconcrete" uses pictures of real objects rather than schematic drawings

### Target Learners
- Students with learning disabilities or mathematics difficulties, for whom CRA has the strongest evidence base [~S]
- Novices encountering a new symbolic system for the first time [Reducing extraneous cognitive load during instruction improves learning outcomes.](../claims/cognitive-load-reduction-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Younger learners whose prior knowledge of the domain is thin and who benefit from [Activation](activation.md) of informal, embodied understanding
- Less beneficial for advanced learners, who can often begin at the representational or abstract stage [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual foundations for procedural skills: place value, regrouping, fraction operations, integer operations, early algebra
- Representational flexibility: mapping between physical, pictorial, and symbolic forms
- Procedural fluency with symbols, once the abstract stage is reached and practiced

### Instructions
1. **Select a structure-mapping concrete material** — the object's behavior must mirror the target concept (e.g., algebra tiles' area model mirrors binomial multiplication). Avoid decorative or high-interest objects that compete for attention.
2. **Teach the concrete stage with explicit modeling** — demonstrate, then guide, then release, following [Explicit Instruction](../principles/explicit-instruction.md) routines.
3. **Bridge to the representational stage** — draw exactly what the materials showed, using the same colors and layout, and name the correspondence aloud.
4. **Bridge to the abstract stage** — record the symbols alongside the drawing first, then fade the drawing; connect each symbol to the action it encodes.
5. **Build fluency at the abstract stage** — the goal is efficient symbolic work; provide distributed [Practice](../elements/practice.md) without materials once accuracy is established.
6. **Fade deliberately** — monitor accuracy and speed; return to a previous stage only for specific error patterns, not wholesale.

## Examples
- **Witzel's CRA algebra curriculum** — algebra tiles used to teach linear equation solving to middle school students with math difficulties, moving from tile manipulation to drawn area models to symbolic steps.
- **Butler et al. fraction intervention** — fraction tiles and circles to teach equivalent fractions and fraction addition, with measured gains over representational-only instruction.
- **[Bridges in Mathematics](https://www.mathlearningcenter.org/curriculum/bridges)** — a published K–5 curriculum that systematically sequences hands-on models, visual models, and symbolic notation across units.
- **[Numicon](https://global.oup.com/education/content/children/series/numicon/)** — structured number tiles used in UK primary classrooms as the concrete stage of early arithmetic instruction.
