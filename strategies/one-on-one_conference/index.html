One-on-One Conference¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 5 claims (5 for) · 12 studies (4 review, 3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 2 of 12 report an effect size

Description¶

One-on-one conferences involve providing individualized feedback to a learner in a private setting. This approach allows for personalized attention, facilitates open communication, and provides an opportunity for the learner to ask questions. Conferences are typically structured around a shared artifact (a draft, a project, a portfolio) and a small set of goals, with the learner doing substantial talking — explaining their reasoning, self-assessing, and setting next steps — rather than passively receiving the instructor's evaluation.

Design Implications¶

Conferences concentrate the conditions under which feedback works best: it is dialogic, task- and process-focused, and delivered at a moment when the learner can act on it Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels rather than the person. [+S]. Because the instructor can probe understanding and adjust support in real time, conferencing is a natural vehicle for contingent scaffolding — support that responds to the learner's current state rather than a predetermined script Scaffolding that is contingent on learner responses improves learning outcomes. [+M]. The private, relational setting also builds the learner's belief that effort will pay off, supporting persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Protected time and a private (or at least low-distraction) setting; short, frequent conferences often beat long infrequent ones
  • A concrete artifact or performance to anchor the conversation — feedback attached to specific work outperforms general praise Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels rather than the person. [+S]
  • A structure that makes the learner an active participant: self-assessment first, then instructor input, then jointly set next steps (Self-Assessment, Goal-Setting)
  • Instructor skill in Active Listening — questioning and eliciting before telling

Constraints¶

  • Highly time-intensive; at scale, conference time crowds out other instruction, so frequency must be rationed or the format adapted (peer conferences, small-group conferences, brief check-ins)
  • Effectiveness drops when conferences become one-way evaluation delivery; students who perceive grades as the focus engage superficially Grades can undermine the learning-oriented response that comments are meant to produce. [~M]
  • Power asymmetry can suppress honest disclosure; without deliberate rapport-building, learners may hide confusion rather than surface it
  • Benefits depend on follow-through — a conference that ends without a concrete next action rarely changes behavior

Implementation Variability¶

  • Writing conferences (Atwell, Calkins): brief, frequent, focused on one revision move per conference
  • Reading conferences: learner discusses independent reading, instructor checks comprehension and coaches strategy use
  • Advising/mentoring conferences: longer horizon, focused on goals and self-regulation rather than a single artifact
  • Peer conferences: learners use the same structure with each other, trading instructor time for rehearsal of evaluative judgment
  • Micro-conferences / check-ins: 2–5 minute conversations during work time, preserving individualization at scale

Target Learners¶

  • Developing writers and other learners producing complex, revisable work, who benefit most from process-level guidance Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels rather than the person. [+S]
  • Learners with weak self-regulation, for whom the conference externalizes monitoring and planning Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Anxious or disengaged learners who will not ask questions in public settings; the private format lowers the social cost of admitting confusion
  • Less necessary for learners with strong self-regulation, who can extract the same information from written feedback

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Improving the quality of complex products (writing, projects, portfolios) through iterative revision
  • Self-regulated learning: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own work Self-monitoring improves self-regulation. [+M]
  • Metacognitive and reflective skill: articulating one's own reasoning and learning needs
  • Relationship-building and motivation, particularly self-efficacy Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Schedule and anchor. Set a predictable cadence and ask the learner to bring a specific artifact and one question (Check-Ins).
  2. Open with the learner's self-assessment. "Tell me about your process — what went well, what's giving you trouble?" (Self-Assessment, Reflection).
  3. Probe before advising. Use questions to locate the actual source of difficulty before offering input (Coaching).
  4. Give one or two process-level feedback points. Focus on strategy and next moves, not the person or the grade Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels rather than the person. [+S] (Provide Feedback).
  5. Co-set a concrete next step. End with one specific, achievable action the learner owns (Goal-Setting).
  6. Follow up. Open the next conference by checking on the agreed action, closing the feedback loop (Assessment).

Related Strategies¶

  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — a compressed variant that makes conferencing feasible at class scale
  • Active Listening — the core interactional skill that determines conference quality
  • Check-Ins — the lightweight cousin; brief individual contact between full conferences

Related Elements¶

  • Coaching — conferencing is coaching's primary delivery format; the conference is where diagnosis and tailored guidance happen
  • Provide Feedback — the central move of the conference, most effective at task and process levels
  • Self-Assessment — opening with the learner's own evaluation makes the conference dialogic rather than evaluative
  • Reflection — conferences prompt and model the reflective moves learners later internalize
  • Scaffolding — conference support should fade as learners take over monitoring and revision decisions Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility. [+M]

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Formative Assessment — the conference is the richest individual-level formative assessment event
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — the coaching phase, where the expert observes and guides individual performance
  • Writing Workshop — conferencing is a defining structural component of workshop models

Examples¶

  • Nancie Atwell's writing workshop (Center for Teaching and Learning, Maine): the teacher holds brief daily conferences with individual student writers, focusing each exchange on a single revision decision. See https://www.c-t-l.org
  • Lucy Calkins' Units of Study (Teachers College Reading & Writing Project): conferring routines with scripted structures ("research-decide-teach") are embedded in published K–8 curricula. See https://www.unitsofstudy.com
  • PhD advising: regular one-on-one meetings between advisor and advisee, where feedback on drafts and goal-setting for the next work cycle mirror the conference structure at an adult level.
