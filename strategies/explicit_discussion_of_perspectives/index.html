Explicit Discussion of Perspectives¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

The instructor deliberately surfaces, names, and compares multiple perspectives on a contested or multi-faceted topic — including perspectives students do not hold — and structures discussion so learners must articulate, evaluate, and respond to viewpoints other than their own. The strategy goes beyond open discussion: the instructor makes the existence of alternative framings explicit and models how to characterize a position fairly before critiquing it.

Design Implications¶

Explicitly comparing perspectives supports abstraction and transfer, because learners must identify what varies across viewpoints and what remains constant Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]. The strategy also builds argumentation skill: learners who are prompted to generate and address counterarguments produce stronger, more defensible reasoning Strategies for teaching critical thinking, including perspective-taking and argument analysis, produce meaningful gains. [+M]. Structured controversy — requiring learners to argue for an assigned position and then switch — improves achievement and perspective-taking relative to debate-without-switching Cooperative learning with structured controversy improves achievement and relationships. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A topic with genuine, defensible disagreement or multiple legitimate framings
  • Instructor preparation: knowing the major positions well enough to represent them accurately, including the strongest version of each (Steelmanning)
  • Discussion structures that require every learner to engage, not just volunteers (Class Discussion)
  • Norms for respectful disagreement established before contested content is introduced

Constraints¶

  • If perspectives are presented as a list without comparison or evaluation, learners encode them as isolated facts rather than as positions in tension [~M]
  • Learners with low prior knowledge may lack the schema to evaluate competing claims, producing confusion rather than discrimination [~M] — pairing with Case Studies or Advance Organizers mitigates this
  • Unstructured discussion of polarized topics can entrench existing views rather than broaden them; assigned positions and structured turn-taking reduce this risk [~S]
  • Overloading learners with too many perspectives at once exceeds working memory limits; two to three well-chosen viewpoints outperform an exhaustive survey [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Instructor-led comparison: instructor presents two or three positions and models the comparison aloud (Think-Aloud)
  • Assigned-position controversy: learners argue an assigned side, then switch sides and re-argue (Johnson & Johnson's constructive controversy)
  • Case-embedded perspectives: a Case Study presents stakeholders with conflicting interests; learners analyze each stakeholder's reasoning
  • Written perspective-taking: learners write the strongest counterargument to their own position before responding (Argumentation)

Target Learners¶

  • Intermediate learners who have enough domain knowledge to evaluate claims but have not yet encountered the field's major disagreements [~M]
  • Learners prone to premature closure, who benefit from prompts to consider alternatives before committing to an answer Strategies for teaching critical thinking, including perspective-taking and argument analysis, produce meaningful gains. [+M]
  • Less effective for complete novices, who cannot yet distinguish strong from weak claims across perspectives [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Critical thinking: evaluating evidence and reasoning across competing claims
  • Conceptual understanding: understanding why a discipline frames a problem in multiple ways
  • Perspective-taking and empathy: recognizing the internal logic of positions one rejects (Building Empathy)
  • Argumentation: constructing and defending a position while addressing counterarguments

Instructions¶

  1. Select a topic with genuine disagreement and identify two to three defensible perspectives.
  2. Activate relevant prior knowledge before introducing the tension (Activation).
  3. Present each perspective in its strongest form, clearly labeled; use a Think-Aloud to model how an expert compares them.
  4. Assign learners to articulate a perspective — ideally one they do not hold — using structured Argumentation or a Debate format.
  5. Require learners to state the strongest counterargument to their own position before responding (Self-Explanation [+M]).
  6. Debrief by having learners synthesize: what does each perspective explain well, and what does it miss?

Related Strategies¶

  • Structured academic controversy — a formalized version using assigned positions and side-switching
  • Socratic questioning — instructor questioning that surfaces hidden assumptions behind a position
  • Jigsaw — each learner masters one perspective or piece, then teaches it to peers

Examples¶

  • Constructive controversy in teacher education (Johnson & Johnson): pre-service teachers are assigned positions on tracking vs. mixed-ability grouping, argue, switch sides, then synthesize a personal position — reliably improving achievement and attitude change over debate and individual study.
  • Stakeholder analysis in case teaching (Harvard Business School case method): learners discuss a business case by articulating the distinct interests and reasoning of managers, employees, customers, and shareholders before evaluating decisions.
  • History classrooms using document-based perspective work: learners compare primary sources from opposing sides of an event (e.g., Reading Like a Historian, Stanford History Education Group, https://sheg.stanford.edu), explicitly discussing how each author's position shaped the account.
