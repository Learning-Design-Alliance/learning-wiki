# Enrichment Through Art and Music

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Teachers integrate art and music from diverse cultures into their lessons to enrich learning, engage students, and promote cultural understanding. Artworks and musical selections serve as entry points, analogies, and evidence for academic content, while students are encouraged to share their own cultural art and music, fostering a sense of inclusion and connection.

## Design Implications

Art and music function as dual-coding supports: pairing verbal content with visual and auditory representations gives learners two memory pathways and improves recall [Pairing verbal information with relevant imagery improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]. Culturally responsive selection also matters for motivation and belonging — content that affirms students' identities and communities increases engagement and outcomes [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. The strategy works only when the art or music is *integral* to the learning goal; decorative use adds extraneous load without benefit [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to diverse, accurate art and music resources (recordings, reproductions, museum collections, community artists)
- Willingness to incorporate student input and invite students to share their own cultural materials
- Time for preparation, presentation, and discussion
- A clear link between the chosen artwork/music and the academic content being taught

#### Constraints
- Requires careful planning to avoid cultural appropriation, tokenism, or misrepresentation — surface-level "heroes and holidays" treatment can reinforce stereotypes rather than reduce them [~M]
- Decorative or tangential use of art and music adds cognitive load without improving learning [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [-S]
- Background music during independent work can impair reading comprehension and memory for verbal material, particularly for introverted learners [~M]
- May require additional resources, licensing awareness, and community consultation to support diverse artistic expressions

#### Implementation Variability
- **Content anchor:** art/music as a primary source to analyze (e.g., protest songs in history), as an analogy for a concept (e.g., form in music mirroring essay structure), or as a product students create to demonstrate understanding
- **Direction of sharing:** teacher-curated selections vs. student-contributed materials with family and community involvement
- **Modality:** listening, viewing, performing, or creating — creation demands the most instructional time but produces the deepest engagement

### Target Learners
- K–12 students, especially in culturally diverse classrooms where identity-affirming content strengthens belonging [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Learners who benefit from non-linguistic representations of abstract content [Pairing verbal information with relevant imagery improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]
- Teachers seeking to make lessons more engaging and culturally relevant; the strategy is not learner-age-specific but requires facilitation skill to manage cultural discussion

### Target Learning Goals
- Content understanding through multiple representations [Pairing verbal information with relevant imagery improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]
- Cultural appreciation and intergroup understanding [Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+M]
- Engagement, discussion, and connection of academic content to students' lived experiences

### Instructions
1. Identify the learning goal and select or invite art/music that genuinely connects to it — not merely decorates it.
2. Open with [Activation](../elements/activation.md): ask students what they notice, what it reminds them of, and what cultural context they bring [Activation improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].
3. Provide a brief [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of how to analyze the piece (e.g., how to "read" a painting's composition or listen for a musical motif), then move to guided analysis.
4. Structure [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) comparing cultural similarities and differences across the works shared.
5. Invite students to contribute art or music from their own cultures and, where feasible, [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) or perform — connecting content to personal experience.
6. Close by having students articulate the content concept through the artistic lens (e.g., explain the water cycle using a movement or song), consolidating the dual representation.

## Examples
- A middle-school history unit uses protest songs from multiple eras and cultures as primary sources; students analyze lyrics for rhetorical moves, then write and perform their own verse about a current issue.
- An elementary science lesson on seasons opens with [Activation](../elements/activation.md) using Vivaldi's *Four Seasons* and a Japanese woodblock print series, followed by students creating their own visual-musical representation of one season.
- [Smithsonian Folkways](https://folkways.si.edu) lesson plans pair regional music traditions with geography and social studies standards, with students sharing family musical traditions as part of the unit.
