# Mood Meters

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
The Mood Meter is a 2×2 grid (developed as part of Yale's RULER approach) on which learners plot their current emotional state along two axes: energy (low–high) and pleasantness (unpleasant–pleasant). The four quadrants are color-coded — red (high energy, unpleasant), yellow (high energy, pleasant), blue (low energy, unpleasant), green (low energy, pleasant) — and learners place themselves in a quadrant, often with a specific emotion word. The routine takes 1–5 minutes and is typically used at the start of a session or day.

## Design Implications

Naming an emotional state with precision (emotional granularity) supports self-regulation and gives the instructor a fast, low-stakes read on the room [Emotion labeling reduces amygdala reactivity and improves regulation.](../claims/emotion-labeling-improves-regulation.md) [+M]. The structured dimensions matter: asking "how are you?" produces generic responses, whereas forcing a position on two continuous dimensions requires appraisal and yields more differentiated vocabulary. The public, shared nature of the routine also functions as a brief [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) that builds relational trust and belonging [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visible grid (poster, whiteboard, or digital tool such as the How We Feel app) with the two labeled axes
- A growing emotion vocabulary per quadrant — students need words beyond "good/bad" for the labeling to build granularity
- A norm that participation is honest but not compulsory to share publicly; private or anonymous variants preserve validity
- A brief follow-up: acknowledgment by the instructor, or a regulation strategy offered for red/blue placements

#### Constraints
- Routine repetition without variation produces mechanical, socially desirable responses [-M] — students may plot the "expected" mood rather than their actual one
- Public plotting can pressure students to disclose or perform emotions in front of peers [~M]; anonymous or small-group formats mitigate this
- Without instructor follow-through (acknowledging, adjusting plans, offering regulation support), the check-in signals that emotional data is collected but ignored [-M]
- Less diagnostic for alexithymia or limited emotional vocabulary; these learners need explicit emotion-word instruction first [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Physical magnet/board version** — students move a marker; fast, visible, communal
- **Digital version** — the How We Feel app (Yale-derived) adds emotion-word taxonomies and trend tracking over time
- **Anonymous ticket version** — students submit quadrant + word on paper; instructor scans and reports the aggregate
- **Extended version** — students also name a *desired* mood and one strategy to move toward it, converting the meter into a self-regulation plan

### Target Learners
- K–12 students, where daily emotional check-ins are associated with improved climate and self-regulation [+M]
- Adolescents with low emotional granularity, who benefit most from forced differentiation of feeling states [+W]
- Adult learners and professionals (RULER is used in workplace training), though framing must shift from routine to reflective tool [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional learning: emotion recognition, labeling, and regulation
- Metacognition: noticing and monitoring one's own affective state as an input to learning
- Community building: normalized, low-stakes sharing that supports [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md)

### Instructions
1. Display the Mood Meter grid and briefly review the two axes (energy, pleasantness) and quadrant colors.
2. Give learners 1–2 minutes to place themselves in a quadrant and select or recall a specific emotion word — encourage precision ("frustrated," not "bad").
3. Collect placements (public, private, or anonymous, per class norms) and scan for patterns.
4. Acknowledge the aggregate ("I see a lot of blue today") and adjust or offer a regulation strategy — movement, breathing, reframing — for students in red or blue.
5. Periodically revisit: ask students to reflect on how their mood shifted across a task and what influenced it, linking the routine to [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md).

## Examples
- **RULER Approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence)** — the Mood Meter is one of four RULER anchor tools; implemented school-wide at hundreds of schools ([https://ei.yale.edu](https://ei.yale.edu))
- **How We Feel app** — free mobile app co-developed with the Yale center; students log mood on the grid with a 100+ word emotion vocabulary and track trends ([https://www.howwefeel.org](https://www.howwefeel.org))
- **Classroom magnet boards** — students move a labeled magnet to a quadrant on arrival; the instructor reads the distribution before planning the lesson's opening
