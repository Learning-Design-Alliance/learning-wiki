# Develop Observable Criteria

## Description
Developing observable criteria means translating learning goals into performance descriptors that are specific, measurable, and visible to learners — typically through objectives written with observable verbs and through rubrics or success criteria shared before instruction. Frameworks such as Bloom's Taxonomy (revised by Anderson & Krathwohl), Webb's Depth of Knowledge, and Marzano's Dimensions of Learning supply the vocabulary for specifying *what observable behavior* demonstrates learning and *at what cognitive level*. The strategy treats criteria as an instructional tool, not just an assessment tool: when learners know what quality looks like, they can self-monitor and self-assess against it.

## Design Implications

Explicit criteria support learning because they enable self-regulation: learners compare their work against a standard, detect gaps, and act on them [Formative assessment improves achievement when learners know the goal and can close the gap.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Rubrics and success criteria are most effective when they describe quality at the task and process level rather than merely assigning scores [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Criteria must be genuinely observable — a verb like "understand" cannot be assessed, while "classify," "justify," or "construct" can.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Objectives phrased with observable verbs tied to a cognitive-level framework (Bloom's, Webb's DOK, or Marzano's)
- Rubrics or success criteria with well-defined performance scales, shared with learners *before* they begin work
- Alignment: every assessment task must map to a stated criterion, and every criterion to an objective
- Examples of work at different quality levels so learners can calibrate their interpretation of the criteria

#### Constraints
- Developing high-quality, aligned criteria is labor-intensive; poorly written rubrics (vague descriptors, too many criteria) add cognitive load without improving work quality [~M]
- Overly detailed checklists can encourage surface compliance — learners optimize for the rubric rather than the underlying goal [-M]
- Criteria fixed in advance constrain emergent, creative, or exploratory work; for open-ended goals, co-constructed or evolving criteria work better [~W]
- Novice learners may not understand criteria language without worked examples of what "meets the criterion" looks like [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Analytic rubrics** (separate scales per criterion) vs. **holistic rubrics** (single scale) — analytic versions give more actionable information; holistic versions reduce load for simple tasks
- **Single-point rubrics** — criteria described only at proficiency, with space for notes above/below; reduces the "middle of the scale" problem
- **Co-constructed criteria** — learners help generate success criteria from exemplars, which deepens their understanding of quality [~M]
- **Developmental scales** — Webb's DOK or Marzano's proficiency scales used to show progression, not just judgment

### Target Learners
- Novices, who lack an internal model of what quality looks like and benefit most from external criteria [Formative assessment improves achievement when learners know the goal and can close the gap.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Learners developing self-regulation skills, who need explicit standards to self-assess against
- Less critical for experts, who already hold internal quality standards and may find detailed rubrics redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Skill and competency development where performance can be directly observed
- Conceptual understanding at specified cognitive levels (classify, explain, evaluate, create)
- Self-regulated learning: building learners' capacity to judge their own work against standards

### Instructions
1. **Define the objective** using an observable verb from Bloom's (revised) or Webb's DOK; replace "understand X" with "classify X," "justify X," or "construct X."
2. **Identify the evidence** — what a learner doing the objective would visibly say, make, or do ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)).
3. **Draft criteria and scales** — 3–5 criteria maximum, each with descriptors describing quality, not absence of faults.
4. **Calibrate with exemplars** — pair each criterion with samples of strong and weak work ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) sharpen interpretation of criteria).
5. **Share criteria before work begins** and teach learners to use them for self- and peer-assessment ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md), [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)).
6. **Use criteria for feedback** — reference specific criteria when giving feedback so learners can act on it ([Feedback](../elements/feedback.md)).

## Examples
- **Marzano proficiency scales** — a 4-point scale ("1 = with help, partial success … 4 = in-depth inferences and applications") published for thousands of K–12 standards; teachers post the scale so students know what "meeting the standard" means before instruction.
- **Writing instruction with exemplar calibration** — a teacher shares three anonymous essays, the class applies the rubric to each, and the discussion surfaces what the criteria actually mean before students draft.
- **Competency-based programs** — Western Governors University and similar programs define each competency with observable performance descriptors; assessment is pass/fail against those descriptors rather than norm-referenced grading.
