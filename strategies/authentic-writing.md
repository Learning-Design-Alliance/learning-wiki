# Authentic Writing

## Description
Authentic writing assigns composition tasks with genuine communicative purpose — writing for real audiences, real decisions, or real publication rather than producing text solely to demonstrate competence to an instructor. The task mirrors how writing functions outside school: to persuade, inform, document, or connect with someone who will actually read and respond. It is carried out by specifying an audience, a purpose, and often a real or simulated genre (letter, proposal, op-ed, wiki article), and by arranging for the work to reach that audience.

## Design Implications

Authentic audiences change how students write: they attend more to audience needs, revise more deliberately, and produce higher-quality work than when writing only for the teacher [Authentic audiences improve student work.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M]. The mechanism is motivational and rhetorical — a real reader gives students a reason to care about clarity, tone, and structure, converting writing from a performance of knowledge into an act of communication. Authenticity is a design property of the task, not the medium: a simulated client brief can be more authentic than a "real" essay no one reads.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A specified audience beyond the grader — real (community members, peers, online readers) or plausibly simulated
- A purpose the audience would actually care about (decide, act, learn, respond)
- A genre appropriate to that audience–purpose pair, with its conventions taught explicitly
- A feedback or response loop: the audience should react, reply, or use the text ([Peer Review](../elements/peer-review.md), publication, presentation)

#### Constraints
- Authenticity without scaffolding can overwhelm novices, who lack genre knowledge to adapt to unfamiliar audiences [-W] — task realism must be paired with models and genre instruction
- Publication to open online audiences raises privacy, safety, and moderation concerns, especially for minors [~W]
- If the "audience" is obviously fictional and never responds, students detect the pretense and the motivational benefit collapses [~M]
- High-stakes authentic tasks can shift attention from learning to impression management; low-stakes drafting should precede public writing

#### Implementation Variability
- **Real publication**: class blog, school newspaper, letters to editors or officials, Wikipedia editing
- **Simulated professional contexts**: client briefs, grant proposals, policy memos reviewed by outside professionals
- **Peer audiences**: writing exchanged and responded to within the class, using structured [Peer Feedback](../elements/peer-feedback.md) protocols
- **Service and community writing**: documents produced for community organizations ([Community-Based Learning](../principles/community-based-learning.md))

### Target Learners
- Secondary and post-secondary students, who benefit most from audience-awareness demands [Authentic audiences improve student work.](../claims/authentic-audiences-improve-student-work.md) [+M]
- Reluctant writers, for whom a real reader supplies purpose that grades do not [+W]
- Multilingual learners benefit when genre conventions are made explicit alongside the authentic task [~M]
- Young learners need narrower, safer audiences (classmates, families) rather than open publication

### Target Learning Goals
- Rhetorical competence: adapting content, tone, and structure to audience and purpose
- Writing quality: organization, elaboration, and revision behavior [Collaborative writing improves text quality.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]
- Disciplinary communication: writing as practitioners write in a field
- Motivation and writing self-efficacy [+W]

### Instructions
1. Identify a real or plausible audience and a purpose they hold — not "demonstrate learning."
2. Select the genre that audience would actually read, and show models of it ([Mentor Texts](mentor-texts.md)).
3. Teach the genre's conventions explicitly before drafting ([Explicit Instruction](../principles/explicit-instruction.md)).
4. Have students draft with the audience specified in the prompt ("readers who will decide whether to fund…").
5. Arrange structured peer or external response on audience-relevant criteria ([Peer Review](../elements/peer-review.md)).
6. Deliver the writing to its audience and have students observe the response; debrief what the audience reaction revealed about the text.
7. Use the response to drive revision, treating feedback as communication rather than evaluation ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).

## Examples
- **Letters to the editor / civic writing**: students draft op-eds on local issues and submit them to real news outlets; several published curricula (e.g., [The Learning Network](https://www.nytimes.com/section/learning) student contests) provide authentic publication venues with real editorial response.
- **Wikipedia editing**: the [Wiki Education](https://wikiedu.org) program has university students improve live Wikipedia articles, writing for millions of actual readers under real editorial norms.
- **Client-based technical writing**: engineering and business communication courses (e.g., Michigan Tech's long-running client-based first-year composition) have students produce deliverables for real nonprofit clients who use them.
