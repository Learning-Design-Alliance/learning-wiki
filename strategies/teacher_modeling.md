# Teacher Modeling

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Teacher modeling is the instructor's live demonstration of a cognitive or productive strategy — most commonly in writing instruction — by performing it in front of students while narrating the decisions, false starts, and revisions involved. Unlike a polished exemplar, modeling makes the *process* visible, including the teacher's own difficulty and self-correction. It is the classroom enactment of [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) combined with [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) of expert thinking.

## Design Implications

Modeling works because observing an expert perform a task — especially with verbalized reasoning — gives learners a template they can imitate before attempting the task themselves [van Gog & Rummel, 2010] [+M]. Its distinctive contribution over static examples is affective and metacognitive: seeing an expert struggle normalizes difficulty, supports self-efficacy, and reframes writing as revisable process rather than innate talent [Bandura, 1977] [+M]. Modeling is most effective when the teacher thinks aloud — naming what they are doing and why — rather than silently producing text [~S], and when it is immediately followed by guided student practice [Pairing worked examples with practice or fading supports transfer better than examples alone.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Teacher willingness to compose authentically in front of students, including sharing drafts and mistakes
- Narration that makes reasoning explicit ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)), not just visible action
- A supportive classroom climate where imperfection is treated as normal
- An immediate follow-on activity in which students apply the modeled strategy ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Modeling without subsequent practice produces the illusion of understanding; students who watched a fluent demonstration often overestimate their own readiness [-S]
- Over-polished modeling can intimidate rather than reassure — students may conclude the gap between expert and novice is unbridgeable [~W]
- Time-intensive; modeling every strategy crowds out student composing time
- Less effective for learners with strong existing skill in the strategy, for whom watching a full demonstration is redundant [Expertise-reversal-effect.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Live composed modeling** — the teacher writes new text in real time, showing genuine drafting and revision
- **Revising-a-draft modeling** — the teacher takes a pre-written (deliberately flawed) draft and revises it aloud, which lowers the fluency demands on the teacher and highlights revision decisions
- **Interactive modeling** — students supply words, decisions, or critiques during the demonstration, shifting it toward shared composition
- **Faded modeling** — the teacher demonstrates the full strategy first, then progressively hands over steps to students [Fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Novice and struggling writers who lack a mental model of how experienced writers work [+M]
- Low-confidence writers, for whom observing expert struggle reduces anxiety and supports persistence [Self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Less beneficial for proficient writers, who benefit more from brief targeted feedback than full demonstrations [Expertise-reversal-effect.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural knowledge of writing strategies: brainstorming, outlining, drafting, revising
- Metacognitive strategy use: monitoring comprehension, detecting problems, self-correcting [Self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Dispositional goals: risk-taking, growth mindset, tolerance for messy first drafts

### Instructions
1. Choose one narrow, immediately usable strategy (e.g., "re-reading a sentence to check for a dangling modifier") — modeling a whole genre at once overloads working memory [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md)
2. Name the strategy and when a writer would use it
3. Demonstrate it live while thinking aloud, verbalizing decisions, uncertainty, and self-correction ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md), [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
4. Deliberately make and repair a mistake so students see recovery, not just fluency
5. Have students apply the same strategy immediately on their own text with support available ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
6. Debrief in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md): what did students notice, what was hard, where else could the strategy apply
7. Fade the modeling over successive lessons — from full demonstration to shared composition to independent use [Fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]

## Examples
- **Writing workshop (Calkins' Units of Study)** — the minilesson opens with the teacher composing a short text live, naming craft moves, before students write independently ([Units of Study](https://www.unitsofstudy.com))
- **Interactive read-aloud with strategy demonstration** — the teacher pauses while reading to model inferring or questioning, then students try the move on the next passage
- **Revision demonstration** — the teacher projects a deliberately weak paragraph and revises it aloud, inviting students to suggest and justify changes
