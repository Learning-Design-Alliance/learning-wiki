# Sift Method

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 5 studies (3 causal, 1 review, 1 theoretical), `q2`–`q3` · 0 of 5 report an effect size

## Description
The SIFT Method, developed by Mike Caulfield, is a lightweight procedure for evaluating the credibility of online information: **S**top and check your emotional reaction, **I**nvestigate the source, **F**ind better coverage of the claim, and **T**race claims, quotes, and media to their original context. Its central move is *lateral reading* — leaving the page to see what other sources say about the source — rather than scrutinizing the page itself [Professional fact-checkers outperform academics and historians by reading laterally rather than vertically.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S].

## Design Implications

SIFT operationalizes the observed practices of professional fact-checkers, who spend seconds rather than minutes on initial triage and rely on the web to evaluate the web [Professional fact-checkers outperform academics and historians by reading laterally rather than vertically.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. Explicit instruction in these moves measurably improves students' evaluation of online sources [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online information.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. The method is deliberately designed to be fast and repeatable, treating evaluation as triage — deciding whether to invest further attention — rather than exhaustive analysis.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Live web access during instruction; the moves cannot be meaningfully practiced offline
- Authentic tasks using real web pages, social posts, and search results — not sanitized examples
- Instructor modeling of the moves ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of a live evaluation)
- Repeated short practice episodes; the goal is habitual response, not one-off performance
- Internet access during instruction so learners can practice leaving the page and searching for independent coverage
- Authentic web content for practice, including realistic but flawed sources — sanitized examples do not transfer
- Brief direct instruction in each move, followed by immediate [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on live pages

#### Constraints
- Fails when learners lack the background knowledge to interpret what lateral searches reveal — "find better coverage" presumes the ability to judge which coverage is better [~M]
- Less effective for evaluating specialized scholarly or technical claims, where quality signals require domain expertise rather than web triangulation [~M]
- The "Stop" move depends on learners recognizing their own emotional arousal; learners low in metacognitive awareness often skip it
- Over-reliance on surface heuristics (e.g., ".org" domains, professional design) persists if SIFT is taught as a checklist rather than a reasoning disposition [-M]
- Fails when learners lack basic search skills; "find better coverage" presumes fluency with query formulation and result scanning
- The "Stop" move depends on metacognitive awareness that novices often lack; without explicit modeling, learners skip it and react to content emotionally
- Time pressure and strong prior beliefs can override the strategy — motivated reasoning reduces willingness to investigate sources that confirm one's views [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Taught as a full four-move sequence or as individual micro-lessons (e.g., "trace the image" via reverse image search alone)
- Adapted into discipline-specific variants (evaluating statistics, scientific preprints, historical photographs)
- Embedded in [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) where learners evaluate conflicting sources on a real event
- Taught as a full sequence or as individual micro-moves ("just do the S and the I")
- Adapted for younger learners as simplified heuristics ("leave the page and see what others say")
- Embedded in discipline-specific contexts: evaluating journal articles in science, primary sources in history, datasets in statistics
- Delivered via Caulfield's open course materials, or integrated into library information-literacy instruction

### Target Learners
- Secondary and postsecondary students, who encounter most information through social media feeds [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of online information.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Adults in professional contexts requiring rapid information triage (journalists, librarians, health consumers)
- Less suited to novices in a specialized domain without accompanying disciplinary knowledge-building
- Adults in professional contexts (journalism, health, finance) who must triage sources quickly
- Less necessary for learners already fluent in lateral reading; instruction should fade as evaluation becomes automatic [Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases.](../claims/worked-examples-less-effective-with-expertise.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Source evaluation and media literacy: judging credibility of unfamiliar sources
- Civic online reasoning: making evidence-based judgments about public information
- Metacognitive regulation: interrupting automatic sharing/emotional responses before acting on content
- Source evaluation: judging the credibility of an unfamiliar source in seconds, not minutes
- Media literacy: recognizing when content is stripped of its original context
- Metacognitive regulation: pausing before sharing or trusting emotionally charged content

### Instructions
1. **Stop.** Pause before reacting or sharing; notice emotional response and check what you actually know about the source. ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) as a metacognitive interrupt)
2. **Investigate the source.** Open new tabs and search for what others say about the source or author — lateral reading, not vertical reading of the page's own "About" section.
3. **Find better coverage.** Search for the claim itself; prioritize reporting that cites original evidence and agrees across independent outlets.
4. **Trace to the original.** Follow quotes, images, and statistics back to their original context to check for distortion (reverse image search, following citations).

### Instructions
1. **Stop.** Pause when encountering an unfamiliar or emotionally provocative claim; notice the reaction before engaging. (Metacognitive [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
2. **Investigate the source.** Open a new tab and search for what known outlets say about the source or author — read laterally, not vertically.
3. **Find better coverage.** Search for the claim itself on fact-checking sites and trusted outlets; the best fact-check of a claim is often independent of the original source.
4. **Trace the claim.** Follow quotes, images, and statistics back to their original context using reverse image search and citation chasing.
5. Follow with distributed [Practice](../elements/practice.md) on fresh, authentic pages so the moves become automatic [Distributed practice improves retention.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
