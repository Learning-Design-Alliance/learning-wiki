# Addressing Stereotype Threat

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Stereotype threat is the situational anxiety and performance disruption that occurs when a learner fears their performance could confirm a negative stereotype about a group they belong to (Steele & Aronson, 1995). Addressing it means redesigning the learning environment — how tasks are framed, how ability is described, how feedback is given, and how belonging is signaled — so that group identity is not made salient as a performance risk at the moment of learning or assessment.

Stereotype threat is the concern that one will be seen through a negative, stereotyped lens for a group one belongs to (Aronson & Steele, 2005) — for example, women regarding mathematics performance. Its measurable effect on test performance is triggered by two specific conditions occurring together: the test is explicitly framed as high-stakes and as measuring intelligence or ability, and test-takers are reminded of their stereotyped identity beforehand (even something as minor as a demographic question before the test). The underlying mechanism is not a lack of genuine competence or effort — even individuals who believe themselves competent show the effect, because actively suppressing the negative stereotype consumes working memory capacity that would otherwise support the task itself. The effect is strongest, counterintuitively, for those who most want to do well, and its practical consequence is that a stereotyped group's standardized test scores can substantially underestimate their real competence specifically under high-stakes conditions — the same individuals often perform much closer to their actual ability on a low-stakes version of the same task. This mechanism is why strategies here should target the two triggering conditions directly (unnecessary high-stakes framing, unnecessary identity-reminder cues) rather than only offering general encouragement.

## Design Implications

Stereotype threat operates through working-memory load and physiological stress, so its reduction is a [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md) issue as much as an equity issue: threatened learners spend cognitive resources monitoring the situation rather than the task. Because the effect is situational rather than dispositional, small framing changes — describing a test as a problem-solving exercise rather than a diagnostic of ability — reliably reduce it [~S]. Interventions that build durable self-narratives, such as affirming personal values, produce longer-lasting gains for threatened groups [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Awareness of which groups face negative stereotypes in the domain (e.g., gender in mathematics, race in standardized testing)
- Framing of tasks and assessments that emphasizes learnability and growth rather than fixed ability
- Feedback practices that separate the work from the person and communicate high standards plus assurance of capacity to meet them
- Visible signals of belonging: diverse exemplars, inclusive language, and normalization of struggle

#### Constraints
- Single-shot "diversity messaging" without structural follow-through can backfire or be discounted [-W]
- Explicitly naming a stereotype in class discussion can increase its salience for learners who had not been threatened [~W]
- Interventions transferred from research settings without adaptation to local identity dynamics show attenuated effects [~M]
- Removing challenge altogether (lowering standards) signals low expectations and can worsen outcomes [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Wise feedback**: pairing critical feedback with an explicit statement of high standards and belief in the student's capacity to reach them [+M]
- **Values affirmation**: brief structured writing about personally important values before high-stakes assessments [+M]
- **Task reframing**: describing assessments as non-diagnostic practice or problem-solving rather than ability tests [~S]
- **Identity-safe environments**: long-term cultivation of belonging through role models, peer community, and normalized difficulty [+W]

### Target Learners
- Students from groups stereotyped as underperforming in the domain at hand, especially in high-stakes or evaluative moments [+S]
- Students new to a domain or institution, where identity threat is strongest before belonging is established [+M]
- Students with low [self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md), for whom threat and efficacy interact to shape persistence [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Performance protection: enabling learners to demonstrate what they actually know on assessments
- Persistence and retention: sustaining engagement in stereotyped domains (e.g., women in STEM)
- Belonging and identity: building a durable sense of "people like me succeed here"

### Instructions
1. Audit tasks and assessments for identity-salient framing; remove diagnostic-of-ability language and demographic questions before high-stakes items.
2. Frame difficulty as normal and learnable for everyone ("these problems are hard for all students"), using [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) so ambiguity does not become a threat cue.
3. Deliver [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) using the wise-feedback format: high standards + explicit affirmation of the student's capacity [+M].
4. Offer a brief values-affirmation writing activity before major assessments, integrated routinely rather than as a one-off [+M].
5. Use [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and belonging surveys to monitor whether students feel they fit, and follow up with [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) where structural barriers persist.

## Examples
- **University of Colorado Boulder's inclusive assessment redesign** — removing gender/ethnicity items from physics concept inventories and reframing them as feedback tools reduced salience of identity at testing ([CU Boulder Inclusive Teaching](https://www.colorado.edu/sei/)).
- **Wise feedback studies (Cohen et al., 1999; 2006)** — seventh-grade teachers attaching a standards-plus-assurance note to essay feedback substantially improved revision rates and grades for Black and Latino students.
- **Values affirmation at scale (Borman et al., 2016)** — a brief in-class writing exercise administered across an entire district reduced achievement gaps for Latino students.
