# Supporting Self-Regulation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Supporting self-regulation means helping students plan, monitor, and control their emotional, behavioral, and cognitive responses during learning. The strategy focuses on leveraging student strengths rather than remediating deficits: teachers scaffold self-management in emotionally safe contexts, embed reflection routines such as journaling, and teach concrete regulatory techniques (goal-setting, self-monitoring, mindfulness) so that regulation becomes a learnable skill rather than an assumed prerequisite.

## Design Implications

Self-regulation is not a fixed trait but a cyclical process of forethought, performance monitoring, and self-reflection that can be explicitly taught [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+S]. Students who regulate their own learning persist longer and achieve more, in part because regulation builds self-efficacy [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. Effective support pairs external structure (routines, check-ins, scaffolds) with a deliberate transfer of control to the learner, so that regulation is eventually internalized [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Observation and identification of each student's regulatory strengths and challenges before selecting supports
- Emotionally safe classroom norms, so students can practice managing responses without fear of penalty
- Explicit teaching of regulatory strategies (goal-setting, self-monitoring, reflection), not just expectations of self-control
- Structured reflection tools such as journals or [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) that make thinking and feeling visible
- Time: regulatory routines must recur regularly to become habitual

#### Constraints
- Generic self-control training without connection to a specific academic task shows weak transfer; strategy instruction is most effective when embedded in subject-matter content [-M]
- Strategies imposed without student choice can undermine the autonomy that self-regulation depends on [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [-M]
- Young students and students with significant attentional or emotional challenges need more external scaffolding and longer fade periods; expecting independent regulation too early produces failure and disengagement [-M]
- Requires sustained teacher time for observation and monitoring; one-off workshops or lessons show little durable effect [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Journaling** — reflective writing tailored to age and modality; can be private, dialogic (teacher responds), or structured with prompts
- **Mindfulness and pause routines** — brief breathing or refocusing practices used before demanding tasks or after conflict
- **Goal-setting frameworks** — process goals ("I will check my work after each problem") outperform outcome goals for novices [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]
- **Scaffolded task management** — breaking work into [achievable micro-goals](../strategies/achievable_micro-goals.md) with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md), then fading support as competence grows [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- **Choice structures** — [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) and [Academic Choice](academic-choice-planning-working-reflecting.md) let students practice planning and self-evaluation within safe boundaries

### Target Learners
- Students with attentional, emotional, or executive-function challenges who need explicit regulatory scaffolds and [Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md)
- Novices of any age, who benefit from externally provided goals and monitoring structures [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M]
- All students benefit from self-monitoring routines, which improve regulation across ability levels [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+S]
- Less support needed for students who already self-regulate well; excessive external control can feel constraining [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation skills: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own learning behavior
- Emotional and behavioral control: managing impulses and responses in social and academic contexts
- Metacognitive awareness: recognizing one's own understanding, confusion, and strategy use
- Academic persistence and task completion, supported by growing self-efficacy [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Observe and identify.** Note each student's regulatory strengths and challenges across tasks and emotional contexts; involve the student in identifying them.
2. **Establish a safe, predictable environment.** Use [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) and [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) so expectations and transitions are predictable.
3. **Teach a regulatory strategy explicitly.** Model goal-setting, self-monitoring, or a calming routine through [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and think-aloud demonstration before expecting independent use.
4. **Provide structured practice.** Embed the strategy in real academic tasks using [Individual Reflection](../strategies/activities_for_student_self-reflection.md) or journaling; set process goals rather than outcome goals [Process goals outperform outcome goals for novices.](../claims/process-goals-outperform-outcome-goals-for-novices.md) [+M].
5. **Give process-focused feedback.** Feedback about strategies and self-regulation, not just the person or the grade, drives improvement [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
6. **Fade support and transfer control.** Gradually shift planning, monitoring, and evaluation from teacher to student, using [Action Planning](../strategies/action_planning.md) and student-led [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

## Examples
- **Responsive Classroom** (https://www.responsiveclassroom.org) — daily [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md), goal-setting conferences, and logical routines that build self-management across elementary grades.
- **Zimmerman's self-regulation cycle in writing instruction** — students set process goals for a draft, self-monitor with a checklist while writing, and self-evaluate against criteria before revision; this forethought–performance–reflection structure is the canonical classroom application.
- **"Pause, Refocus, Assess" meditation routines** (Adam Kuranishi, Edutopia) — brief mindfulness practices before assessments to help students manage arousal and refocus attention.
- **Digital Promise Learner Variability Project** (https://lvp.digitalpromiseglobal.org) — a research-mapped tool for identifying individual learner variability in self-regulation and matching supports.
