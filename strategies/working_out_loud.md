# Working Out Loud

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Working out loud is the practice of narrating one's work in progress — sharing drafts, questions, failures, and reasoning in a public or semi-public venue — so that others can see, respond to, and build on it. Coined by Bryce Williams (2010) and developed by John Stepper (2015), it combines *visible work* with *relational generosity*: posting is directed at people who can help, not merely archived. In learning contexts, learners post artifacts, explain their thinking, and solicit critique from peers, mentors, or a wider professional network.

## Design Implications

Working out loud externalizes the learning process, converting private struggle into inspectable artifacts that invite feedback and social learning [~M]. Its effectiveness depends on the presence of an actual audience that responds: posting into a void produces compliance posting, not learning. Because it makes reasoning visible, it functions as a form of [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) in which learners, not just experts, model their thinking.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A low-friction sharing venue with a real audience (course forum, cohort channel, portfolio, professional network)
- Norms of psychological safety — learners must believe that showing unfinished work will not be punished
- Structured prompts for what to share (artifact + question + what I'd do differently), since unstructured "share your progress" prompts yield shallow updates
- A response mechanism: peers or instructors commit to commenting within a defined window

#### Constraints
- Without responses or visible uptake, sharing decays into performative updates and adds workload without learning benefit [-M]
- Public visibility raises evaluation anxiety for novices and learners with low sense of belonging; premature public critique can suppress risk-taking [-M]
- In competitive or graded contexts, learners hide difficulties and share polished work only, defeating the purpose [-M]
- High-volume feeds can produce shallow scanning rather than substantive peer engagement [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Cohort-internal**: sharing limited to a class or team — safest starting point for novices
- **Networked**: learners post to professional communities or public blogs, building identity and connections (Stepper's original framing)
- **Structured rituals**: weekly "demo days," standups, or learning journals with peer comment quotas
- **Artifact-focused vs. process-focused**: sharing finished work for critique vs. narrating decisions and dead ends as they happen

### Target Learners
- Adult and professional learners building networks and reputations alongside skills [+W]
- Learners in project-based or portfolio contexts where process documentation has intrinsic value
- Requires scaffolding for novices and learners from groups underrepresented in the field, for whom public visibility carries higher social risk; belonging supports moderate these effects [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition: articulating one's own reasoning and monitoring progress
- Feedback-seeking and revision: learning to solicit, interpret, and act on critique
- Professional identity and community participation: learning the discourse of a field by practicing it publicly
- Collaborative knowledge building: making work reusable and improvable by others

### Instructions
1. Establish the venue and norms: where work is shared, who responds, and how critique is offered (kind, specific, actionable).
2. Model the practice first — the instructor works out loud on their own project, narrating decisions and mistakes (see [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)).
3. Give a sharing template: *what I made, what I'm unsure about, what help I need*.
4. Schedule peer response: assign rotating comment partners so every post receives at least one substantive reply.
5. Progressively widen the audience — from cohort to program to public network — as learners build confidence and judgment about what to share.
6. Close the loop: learners revise based on feedback and post a brief "what changed and why" follow-up.

## Related Strategies
- Learning journals and portfolios — private counterparts that can seed public sharing
- Peer critique protocols — the response mechanism that makes sharing consequential
- Demo days and exhibitions — scheduled, synchronous variants

## Examples
- **Stepper's Working Out Loud circles** ([workingoutloud.com](https://workingoutloud.com)) — peer groups meeting weekly for 12 weeks, each member working toward a goal while sharing progress and making contributions to others.
- **GitHub pull requests and open-source contribution** — learners narrate proposed changes and receive expert review; the review thread is a durable record of reasoning.
- **DS106 (University of Mary Washington)** — an open digital storytelling course where participants post daily assignments to a public feed and comment on each other's work.
- **Agile standups and sprint demos** — workplace variants where teams narrate in-progress work to surface blockers early.
