# Life Back Then

## Description
"Life Back Then" is a historical persona strategy in which learners choose (or are assigned) a time period and a character who lived during it — an ordinary person rather than a famous figure works best. Learners research the character's daily life, social conditions, and worldview using primary and secondary sources, then present first-person accounts, artifacts, or dramatizations to the class. The strategy combines historical inquiry with perspective-taking, culminating in an [authentic audience](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) performance.

## Design Implications

The strategy leverages role-play and narrative to make abstract historical content concrete and personally meaningful, supporting both content learning and historical empathy — the evidence-based practice of reconstructing people's beliefs, values, and goals in context [Historical empathy improves when learners ground perspective-taking in sourced evidence rather than imagination alone.](https://www.socialstudies.org) [+M]. Because learners must justify their portrayal with evidence, the presentation functions as an argument from sources rather than pure invention. The research phase benefits from explicit scaffolding in source evaluation and [annotating](../principles/annotating.md), since novices tend to treat any web text as equally reliable.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to a curated set of primary sources (letters, photographs, census records, newspapers) appropriate to reading levels
- A research organizer that requires learners to cite evidence for each claimed detail of the character's life
- Clear criteria distinguishing evidence-based inference from invention
- Time for structured [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) after presentations to consolidate what the set of personas collectively reveals about the period

#### Constraints
- Without an evidence requirement, learners default to stereotype and presentism, importing modern values into their character's worldview [-M]
- Empathy activities that ask learners to imagine feelings without sources can reduce rather than improve historical understanding [-M]
- Weak readers can be overwhelmed by unmediated primary sources; unadapted documents raise extraneous load [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]
- Learners may remember the drama but not the underlying content if the debrief connecting persona details to period concepts is skipped [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Museum walk / living waxing museum:** learners freeze in costume and speak when visitors approach; suits younger learners and large classes
- **Letter or diary format:** learners write in-character documents, trading performance demands for writing demands
- **Panel of contemporaries:** several characters from the same period (different classes, regions, or roles) present together, enabling comparison of perspectives across social positions
- **Digital variant:** personas presented as blogs, podcasts, or short videos, widening the audience beyond the classroom

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through adult learners; the persona frame is accessible but the source work scales in sophistication
- Learners who benefit from concrete, narrative entry points into abstract or distant content [Narrative structure improves recall of historical content compared with expository structure.](https://www.jstor.org) [+M]
- Reluctant writers and speakers, for whom an in-character frame lowers the social risk of performance [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Historical content knowledge: daily life, social structures, and cause–effect relations in a period
- Historical empathy: reconstructing the beliefs, values, and goals of people in context from evidence
- Source literacy: evaluating, corroborating, and citing primary and secondary sources
- Communication: organizing and delivering a first-person account to an audience

### Instructions
1. **Frame the period.** Provide an overview of the target era with an [advance organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) — timeline, map, and key social categories — so learners have a skeleton to attach persona details to.
2. **Select the character.** Learners choose or draw a character (e.g., a mill worker in 1848 Lowell, a soldier's family in 1917). Require at least one dimension of difference from their own lives to stretch perspective-taking.
3. **Research with sources.** Learners gather evidence from a curated source set, using [annotating](../principles/annotating.md) and a citation-required organizer; model source interrogation with a [think-aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) before independent work.
4. **Draft the persona.** Learners compose their first-person account, distinguishing evidence-backed details from reasoned inference, and receive [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) feedback on historical plausibility.
5. **Present to an audience.** Deliver presentations in one of the formats above, followed by audience questions answered in character.
6. **Debrief out of character.** Lead a [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) comparing personas: what did the collection of characters reveal about the period that any single one could not? This step converts individual performances into collective conceptual understanding.

## Examples
- **Library of Congress Teaching with Primary Sources** (https://www.loc.gov/teachers/) — curated primary source sets (photos, oral histories, newspapers) that supply the research base for persona projects across American history topics.
- **"Living Wax Museum" projects** — a widely used elementary variant in which students portray historical figures and speak on cue; adaptable to ordinary-person personas for stronger empathy outcomes.
- **Imperial War Museum "Life in the Trenches" resources** (https://www.iwm.org.uk/) — soldiers' letters and photographs supporting WWI persona research in UK classrooms.
