# Self-instructions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Self-instruction is a strategy in which the teacher first models articulating their own thinking while solving a problem — verbalizing goals, decisions, and self-checks — and then prompts students to produce the same kind of talk for themselves. Rooted in Meichenbaum's cognitive-behavioral training work, the strategy moves learners from observing expert self-talk, to guided overt self-instruction, to covert private speech as competence grows. It serves a dual diagnostic function: students become aware of how they think, and teachers gain direct evidence of student reasoning and misconceptions.

## Design Implications

Self-instructions build metacognitive awareness by converting tacit problem-solving moves into explicit, imitable language [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]. The modeling phase matters as much as the prompting phase: students need to hear what expert self-monitoring sounds like before they can reproduce it. Prompting students to explain their own thinking produces better learning than listening to explanations alone, because generation forces the learner to integrate new steps with prior knowledge [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Time for the teacher to model problem-solving aloud and to prompt students to explain their thinking
- Careful listening and observation to interpret student thought processes and misconceptions
- Structured prompt frames (e.g., "What is my goal? What is my next step? Does this make sense?") for students who cannot yet generate self-questions
- A classroom climate where students feel safe sharing incomplete or erroneous thinking

#### Constraints
- Students who struggle to articulate their thoughts or have weak metacognitive skills may produce parroting rather than genuine self-explanation [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [~M] — prompts must be scaffolded or the talk becomes performative
- Overt verbalization adds working-memory load; for novices handling complex material, explaining while solving can disrupt performance [~M] — separate the modeling and explaining phases or reduce task complexity
- Requires a supportive environment; in evaluative climates students hide confusion, defeating the diagnostic purpose
- Gains fade if prompts are never faded — the goal is internalized self-talk, not permanent dependence on teacher questioning

#### Implementation Variability
- **Graduated fading**: teacher models aloud → students repeat with prompts → students self-instruct covertly, mirroring the [Fading](../elements/fading.md) logic of scaffold withdrawal
- **Written variants**: self-question checklists or margin annotations for students uncomfortable speaking aloud
- **Domain-specific frames**: reading comprehension (predict–question–clarify–summarize), mathematics (understand–plan–solve–check), writing (planning–revising self-talk)

### Target Learners
- K–12 students, especially those who attempt tasks impulsively without planning or monitoring [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Struggling learners who lack a repertoire of strategies; explicit self-talk gives them language for moves that proficient peers make silently
- Less beneficial for students who already self-regulate well, for whom prompted explanation can feel redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognition: planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's own problem solving [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Conceptual understanding: articulating why a step works, not just what to do [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Strategy acquisition: building a reusable repertoire of problem-solving moves

### Instructions
1. **Model the self-talk.** Solve a problem aloud using [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) with [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) narration — verbalize goals, false starts, and self-checks, not just correct steps.
2. **Name the moves.** Label each self-instruction type (problem definition, focusing, planning, self-evaluation) so students have vocabulary for their own talk, connecting to [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md).
3. **Prompt student explanation.** During guided practice, ask students to explain their thinking at decision points; listen diagnostically and respond with [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) targeted at the reasoning, not just the answer.
4. **Fade the prompts.** Shift from teacher questioning to student-generated self-questions, then to silent self-instruction as competence grows.

## Examples
- **Meichenbaum's self-instructional training** — children with impulsivity problems learned tasks by repeating teacher self-talk ("Okay, what is it I have to do?"), then whispering, then silently instructing themselves; the classic graduated sequence for the strategy.
- **Self-Regulated Strategy Development (SRSD) in writing** — Graham and Harris's approach has teachers model planning self-statements ("Who will read this? What do they need to know?") before students write, with students recording their own self-instructions on goal cards.
- **Reciprocal teaching in reading** — students rotate leading a small group through predicting, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing, effectively performing the teacher's self-instruction role.
