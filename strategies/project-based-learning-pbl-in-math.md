# Project-Based Learning (PBL) in Math

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Project-Based Learning (PBL) is a teaching method in which learners gain knowledge and skills by working for an extended period to investigate and respond to a complex, authentic question, problem, or challenge. In mathematics, this means anchoring concepts — ratio, functions, statistics, geometry — in real-world contexts such as designing a structure, auditing a budget, or modeling a community problem. Learners typically work in groups with substantial self-direction, while the instructor acts as facilitator, providing just-in-time instruction as the mathematics becomes necessary to the project.

## Design Implications

PBL enacts [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) by placing learners in the role of investigators rather than recipients, and its authentic contexts give mathematics a purpose beyond the classroom [Authentic audiences and purposes increase engagement and effort.](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) [+M]. However, the evidence base is more nuanced than PBL advocacy often suggests: outcomes depend heavily on the amount of structure and scaffolding provided, with well-structured implementations outperforming minimally guided ones [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A driving question or problem that genuinely requires the target mathematics to solve — not a word problem dressed up as a project
- Scaffolding structures: milestones, checkpoints, and just-in-time [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) as learners encounter the need for new concepts [Contingent scaffolding improves learning.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Explicit teaching moments embedded in the project, where the instructor delivers direct instruction on the mathematics the project surfaces
- Formative checkpoints that assess mathematical understanding, not just project completion [Assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)
- Time: multi-week blocks, and administrative support for deviating from pacing guides

#### Constraints
- Minimally guided versions produce weaker learning than structured versions, particularly for novices who lack the prior knowledge to direct their own inquiry productively [-S] — discovery without scaffolding burdens working memory with search rather than learning
- Projects can consume substantial time while touching fewer mathematical topics than direct approaches, creating coverage pressure [~M]
- Group work can allow skill asymmetry: stronger students do the mathematics while weaker students do the poster [-M]
- Assessment misalignment: standardized tests and pacing guides may not reward the depth-over-breadth tradeoff PBL makes
- Authentic contexts can add extraneous cognitive load if the real-world complexity (reading budgets, interpreting data sources) competes with the mathematics for working memory resources [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured PBL** (e.g., problem-based units with scripted scaffolds and embedded direct instruction) shows the strongest outcomes
- **Hybrid models** alternate project work with focused skill instruction and [Practice](../elements/application.md) sessions
- **Driver variations**: community-partnered projects, design/engineering challenges, data-investigation projects
- Duration ranges from single-week "mini-projects" to semester-long curricula (e.g., full PBL course sequences)

### Target Learners
- Middle and secondary students, where evidence is strongest; Boaler's longitudinal comparison found students in open, project-oriented mathematics programs outperformed traditionally taught peers on both open and closed assessments, with the advantage growing over three years [+M]
- Learners who benefit from seeing mathematics as purposeful; project success experiences can build mathematical self-belief that sustains persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Novices require more structure within the project; the approach is riskier for them in minimally guided forms [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Applied problem solving: selecting, combining, and transferring mathematical tools in non-routine contexts
- Conceptual understanding: understanding *why* procedures work, surfaced through the demands of an authentic task
- Mathematical modeling and data reasoning
- Collaboration and communication of mathematical reasoning
- Less efficient for building procedural fluency in specific techniques — pair with deliberate practice

### Instructions
1. **Design the driving problem first, backward from the target mathematics.** Identify the concepts students must learn, then find an authentic context where those concepts are genuinely necessary — not decorative. Present the problem with a [Challenge identification](../elements/challenge-identification.md) launch.
2. **Launch the project and surface prior knowledge.** Open with an entry event (data set, site visit, guest client) that makes the problem concrete and activates what students already know.
3. **Structure the inquiry with milestones.** Break the project into checkpoints with deliverables; at each milestone, assess mathematical understanding, not just artifacts.
4. **Embed just-in-time instruction.** When students hit the limits of their current knowledge, deliver focused mini-lessons or worked examples, then return them to application [Application](../elements/application.md) of the new tool within the project.
5. **Facilitate group work deliberately.** Assign and rotate roles, monitor for skill asymmetry, and use [Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md) structures that require every member to do the mathematics.
6. **Require articulation of reasoning.** Have groups present solutions and defend their mathematical choices [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md), ideally to an authentic audience beyond the teacher.
7. **Debrief and consolidate.** After the product is complete, explicitly connect the project experience to the generalizable mathematics, so knowledge detaches from the specific context.

## Related Strategies
- [Anchored Instruction](../patterns/anchored-instruction.md) — the broader pattern of situating learning in a realistic problem context; PBL in math is an extended form of it
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — shorter-cycle variant using cases rather than full projects
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — PBL projects create the authentic activity in which coaching and articulation occur
- [Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md) — a common pairing: direct instruction moves to video homework, freeing class time for project work

## Examples
- **[High Tech High](https://www.hightechhigh.org)** (San Diego) — project-based charter schools where mathematics is integrated into interdisciplinary projects (e.g., calculating dimensions, materials, and costs to build geodesic greenhouses).
- **[ConnectED / Linked Learning](https://www.connectedcalifornia.org)** — California initiative pairing secondary math pathways with industry-based projects.
- **Boaler's "Railside" and "Phoenix Park" studies** — three-year comparisons of project-oriented versus textbook-based secondary mathematics programs in England and the US, documenting achievement and equity advantages for the project-oriented approach.
- **Community-partnered projects** — students remedy a local food desert by planning a healthy restaurant, requiring cost modeling, unit rates, and nutritional data analysis.
