# Targeted and Specific Communication with Families

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Targeted and specific communication with families means tailoring the content, language, and channel of school-to-home communication to the particular population receiving it — non-native English speakers, families from different cultural backgrounds, families of students with disabilities — rather than sending generic messages to all. Messages focus on the individual student's progress or concrete steps families can take to support learning, and schools provide modes for two-way communication so families can respond, question, and contribute.

## Design Implications

Family engagement research consistently shows that *specific* invitations — naming a child, describing what the child is doing, and suggesting a concrete home activity — produce more contact and involvement than generic outreach [~M]. Communication is most effective when it lowers linguistic and cognitive barriers (plain language, translation, [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)) and when it positions families as partners rather than audiences, building trust through [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) and genuine two-way exchange.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Translation and interpretation services matched to the school's actual language demographics
- Communication platforms that support two-way exchange (e.g., TalkingPoints, ClassDojo, Remind) rather than one-way blasts
- Staff time and training for culturally responsive communication
- Accurate family contact and preference data (language, channel, accessibility needs)

#### Constraints
- Generic, one-way mass communication (newsletters, robo-calls about events) rarely changes family behavior and can actively disengage families who cannot read the language or see relevance to their child [-M]
- Translation that is literal rather than culturally adapted can distort meaning or tone, undermining trust [-W]
- Over-reliance on digital platforms excludes families without reliable internet, devices, or digital literacy [-M]
- Communication framed only around deficits or discipline (calls home about misbehavior) reduces family willingness to engage [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Text-messaging programs with automatic two-way translation (e.g., TalkingPoints) for linguistically diverse populations
- Home visits and relationship-first outreach (e.g., the Parent Teacher Home Visits model) for families with historically low engagement
- Individualized progress updates tied to specific learning goals for families of students in special education, aligned with IEP communication plans
- Printed, translated materials and phone calls as low-tech alternatives to app-based communication

### Target Learners
- Families of multilingual learners, who benefit most from translated, plain-language messages [~M]
- Families of students with disabilities, for whom individualized, progress-focused communication supports coordination with IEP goals
- Families with low prior engagement, where specific, personalized invitations are more effective than generic outreach [~M]
- Students indirectly: family engagement is associated with improved attendance, behavior, and achievement, particularly when engagement connects directly to learning at home [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Family capacity-building: equipping families to support specific learning behaviors at home
- Student self-efficacy and persistence, which are supported when families receive concrete, encouraging information about progress [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Partnership and trust between home and school as a precondition for other interventions

### Instructions
1. Segment the family population by language, communication preference, and student needs; collect this data at enrollment and update it regularly.
2. Draft messages that are specific to the individual student — name a recent accomplishment or a concrete next step — rather than generic announcements.
3. Translate and culturally adapt messages; use plain language and short sentences ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)).
4. Deliver through the family's preferred channel, ensuring at least one non-digital option.
5. Build in a two-way loop: invite responses, ask questions, and use [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) to confirm the message was understood and to learn from families.
6. Follow up on family responses with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md)-style guidance — specific suggestions for supporting learning at home — and adjust future messages based on what families report.
7. Periodically review participation data and family feedback to refine targeting.

## Related Strategies
- Positive phone calls and personalized progress messages — the most common classroom-level instantiation of this strategy
- Home visit programs — relationship-first variant for hard-to-reach families
- Two-way translated messaging platforms — the technology-mediated variant

## Examples
- **TalkingPoints** (https://talkingpts.org) — two-way translated text messaging between teachers and families, used widely in US districts with multilingual populations.
- **Parent Teacher Home Visits** (https://www.pthp.org) — structured home-visit program that builds relationships before academic communication begins.
- **Flamboyan Foundation** (https://flamboyanfoundation.org) — DC-based program training schools in relationship-building and individualized family communication, associated with gains in student outcomes in partnership studies.
