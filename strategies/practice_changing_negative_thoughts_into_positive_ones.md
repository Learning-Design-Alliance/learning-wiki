# Practice Changing Negative Thoughts into Positive Ones

## Description
This strategy asks learners to identify negative self-statements — their own or supplied examples — and deliberately transform them into more constructive, accurate alternatives. It operationalizes the cognitive reframing move from cognitive-behavioral theory: the thought itself is treated as the object of analysis, and the learner practices generating alternative interpretations. Sessions typically present negative statements (e.g., "I only did well on this test due to luck"), prompt learners to rewrite them, and then discuss the reframed versions with peers or a facilitator.

## Design Implications

Reframing practice works because it makes automatic self-talk explicit and gives learners a rehearsed procedure for challenging it, consistent with the cognitive model in which emotional responses follow from interpretations of events rather than the events themselves. The practice must target *believable* alternatives, not forced positivity — reframes that learners find implausible are dismissed and can increase cynicism about the technique [~M]. Pairing reframing with [Application](../elements/application.md) to real situations the learner faces converts an in-class exercise into a transferable self-regulation routine.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A set of negative self-statements, ideally drawn from learners' real academic or personal situations
- A facilitator who can model a reframe aloud before learners attempt their own ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
- A supportive, non-judgmental environment; learners are disclosing self-critical thinking
- A follow-up mechanism so reframing is rehearsed over time, not once ([Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md))

#### Constraints
- Learners with entrenched negative thinking patterns or clinical depression may find the exercise trivializing or be unable to generate alternatives without therapeutic support [-M] — this is a classroom strategy, not a treatment
- Shallow "positive affirmations" that contradict the learner's evidence (e.g., "I'm brilliant" after a failed exam) are rejected and can undermine credibility of the technique [-M]; effective reframes are realistic and evidence-based
- One-off practice without reinforcement produces little durable change; reframing skills decay without spaced rehearsal [~M]
- Group settings can inhibit honest disclosure of negative thoughts; private written formats may be needed first

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written thought records**: learners log a negative thought, the situation that triggered it, and a reframed alternative — the classic CBT format
- **Task cards**: prepared negative statements on cards for learners to reframe in pairs, useful when personal disclosure feels risky
- **Attribution reframing**: focus specifically on explanatory style (permanent/pervasive vs. temporary/specific), as in learned-optimism training
- **Peer discussion**: learners exchange reframes and evaluate which alternatives are most believable ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

### Target Learners
- Learners who experience negative self-talk that interferes with persistence, particularly before assessments [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Adolescents and young adults in transition contexts (new schools, first-year programs), where attributional style is still malleable [+M]
- Less appropriate as a standalone intervention for learners with significant mental health needs, who require professional support [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Self-regulation: recognizing and interrupting automatic negative thought patterns [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Attributional style: shifting from stable, global explanations of failure to specific, changeable ones
- Emotional literacy: naming the connection between thoughts, feelings, and behavior

### Instructions
1. **Model**: facilitator presents a negative statement and reframes it aloud, explaining why the reframe is believable ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
2. **Guided practice**: learners reframe supplied examples individually or in pairs ([Application](../elements/application.md))
3. **Personal application**: learners identify one of their own recurring negative thoughts and write a realistic alternative
4. **Peer discussion**: learners share reframes and test which alternatives hold up against evidence ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
5. **Reinforcement**: schedule brief follow-up check-ins where learners report on using reframing in real situations ([Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md))

## Related Strategies
- **Learned Optimism training (Penn Resiliency Program)** — the most researched structured curriculum built around this strategy
- **Growth mindset framing** — complements reframing by supplying the conceptual vocabulary ("not yet") for academic reframes
- **Journaling / thought records** — the private-written variant for learners reluctant to disclose aloud

## Examples
- **Penn Resiliency Program** ([https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu](https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu)) — school-based curriculum in which students practice detecting negative automatic thoughts and generating more accurate alternatives across 12 sessions; meta-analytic evidence shows small but reliable reductions in depressive symptoms [+M]
- **Test-anxiety reframe**: "I only passed because it was an easy test" → "I prepared well and the preparation paid off" — practiced before exams to protect self-efficacy
- **Positive thinking task cards**: pairs of learners draw a card with a negative statement ("I'm bad at math") and compete to produce the most believable reframe, then evaluate each other's versions
