# Systematic Synthetic Phonics

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Systematic synthetic phonics (SSP) teaches grapheme–phoneme correspondences (GPCs) in a deliberate, sequenced order — typically from high-frequency, simple correspondences to more complex ones — and teaches learners to *synthesize* (blend) phonemes into words for reading and segment words into phonemes for spelling. "Systematic" contrasts with incidental or embedded phonics: the sequence of instruction is planned, not left to emerge from texts. "Synthetic" contrasts with analytic approaches: learners build words from parts rather than analyzing whole words into parts.

## Design Implications

SSP is the most strongly evidence-supported approach to early reading instruction: meta-analytic findings show systematic phonics outperforms unsystematic or no phonics across outcome measures [Systematic phonics instruction produces larger gains in word reading than non-systematic or no phonics.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Its effectiveness rests on building automatic word recognition, which frees working-memory and attentional resources for comprehension rather than decoding [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Because blending and segmenting impose heavy demands on novice working memory, lessons should introduce few new GPCs at a time and provide cumulative review [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A planned GPC teaching sequence (simple, high-frequency correspondences first; complexity introduced incrementally)
- Explicit blending and segmenting routines, modeled by the instructor ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) with oral blending)
- Decodable texts matched to the GPCs taught so far, so practice targets taught correspondences rather than guessing
- Cumulative review and distributed [Practice](../elements/practice.md) until responses are automatic ([Automaticity](../elements/automaticity.md))
- Ongoing [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) of GPC knowledge and word reading to pace instruction

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops sharply when phonics is taught unsystematically or incidentally — teaching letter sounds only as they appear in trade books produces weaker word-reading gains [~S]
- Benefits for word reading are strongest in kindergarten and first grade; for older struggling readers, phonics alone yields smaller gains and must be paired with comprehension and fluency work [~M]
- Overly long, decontextualized drill on isolated GPCs without connected-text application can displace time better spent reading [~W]
- English's deep orthography means some correspondences are low-frequency; instruction must explicitly address irregular ("tricky") words rather than pretending all words are fully decodable

#### Implementation Variability
- Pace and scope: UK programmes (e.g., [Read Write Inc.](https://www.ruthmiskin.com/), [Floppy's Phonics](https://global.oup.com/education/content/children/series/oxford-reading-tree/floppys-phonics/)) teach 1–4 new GPCs per week; US programmes (e.g., [Wilson Reading System](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com/), [Reading Mastery](https://www.mheonline.com/)) vary in speed and in whether they add morphology early
- Embedded mnemonics (pictures for letter sounds) vs. bare symbols — embedded picture mnemonics show modest advantages for GPC acquisition [+W]
- Delivery: whole-class, small-group, or one-to-one tutoring; one-to-one and small-group formats show somewhat larger effects for struggling readers [+M]

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers (ages 4–7) in the earliest stages of learning to read [+S]
- Struggling readers and students at risk of reading difficulty, including those with dyslexia, for whom explicit systematic instruction is the recommended first line of support [+M]
- Less effective as a standalone intervention for adolescents with weak comprehension, whose decoding may be adequate but whose language comprehension lags [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Alphabetic principle: understanding that written spellings map systematically onto speech sounds
- Word-level reading accuracy and, through repetition, automatic decoding [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Encoding (spelling) through phoneme segmentation

### Instructions
1. Teach a small set of new GPCs explicitly, with letter formation and a pronunciation routine ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
2. Model oral blending: sounds first, then the whole word ("c–a–t → cat"), then have learners echo and blend independently ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
3. Practice the new GPCs in word reading and spelling, mixed with all previously taught GPCs (cumulative review).
4. Read decodable texts containing only taught GPCs plus a small set of taught irregular words.
5. Assess GPC and word-reading knowledge regularly; reteach gaps before introducing new correspondences ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).

## Examples
- **[Read Write Inc. Phonics](https://www.ruthmiskin.com/programmes/phonics/)** — widely used UK SSP programme with a fixed GPC sequence, daily speed-sounds lessons, and matched decodable storybooks.
- **[Wilson Reading System](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com/programs/wilson-reading-system/)** — structured literacy programme for struggling readers in grades 2–12, sequencing phonics with morphology and fluency work.
- **England's national curriculum** — since 2012 mandates systematic synthetic phonics and a Year 1 "Phonics Screening Check" assessing GPC-based decoding.
