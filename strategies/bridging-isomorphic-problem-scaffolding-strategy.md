# Use a bridging isomorphic problem matched to common student difficulties to scaffold transfer from a two-step to a three-step problem

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 10 claims (9 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (4 causal), `q2`–`q3` · 0 of 4 report an effect size · 9 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article proposes adding scaffolding beyond a single solved isomorphic problem when the transfer problem adds a solution step. In interviews with students E and F, a new problem (the "two-block problem" shown in Appendix C) that is isomorphic to the solved problem and the quiz problem was introduced; this two-block problem, consisting of two steps (an object going down, colliding, sticking and moving together with another object on the horizontal part of the track), was designed in light of the common student difficulties found in the quantitative data. Students first solved the two-block problem with the solved problem in hand, then attempted the putty problem, giving them a worked example closer in structure to the three-step target.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- The bridging problem must be isomorphic to both the solved problem and the target quiz problem, and its design must be based on documented common student difficulties with the target problem.
#### Constraints
- The article presents this strategy only as a modification explored with two interviewed students and lists it among possible strategies for future investigation, not as a validated classroom intervention.

### Target Learners
- introductory physics students (calculus-based and algebra-based courses)

### Target Learning Goals
- transferring physics principles (conservation of momentum and conservation of mechanical energy) across isomorphic problems with different numbers of solution steps
