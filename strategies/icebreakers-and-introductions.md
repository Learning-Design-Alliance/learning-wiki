# Icebreakers And Introductions

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Icebreakers and introductions are brief, low-stakes activities at the opening of a course, workshop, or session in which participants share information about themselves and learn about others. They range from simple name exchanges to structured prompts ("share one thing you're curious about") and small-group tasks. Their instructional function is social: they build the interpersonal foundation that later collaborative and discussion-based work depends on.

## Design Implications

Icebreakers work primarily by building belonging and psychological safety, which are preconditions for participation in discussion and collaboration [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. They are most effective when the content of the activity connects to the course subject matter, so that social bonding and cognitive [Activation](../elements/activation.md) happen simultaneously; purely social games consume time without priming course content. The effect is strongest early and fades if not reinforced — a single icebreaker followed by no further community-building does little.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prompt that is genuinely low-stakes (no grading, no disclosure pressure)
- Instructor participation — the instructor answering the same prompt models vulnerability and flattens hierarchy
- A structure that guarantees every voice is heard at least once (pair shares, round-robins, [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md))
- Follow-through: subsequent activities that reuse what was shared (grouping by interest, referencing introductions in discussion)

#### Constraints
- Forced or gimmicky activities can raise social anxiety, particularly for introverted learners or those from cultures where self-disclosure to strangers is uncomfortable [~M] — the activity becomes a barrier rather than a bridge
- Icebreakers that highlight differences in prior knowledge or status can undermine belonging for novices rather than build it [~M]
- Time spent on content-free activities competes with instruction; in short sessions the cost often exceeds the benefit [-W]
- Effects decay quickly without ongoing community-building ([Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md), recurring small-group work)

#### Implementation Variability
- **Content-linked prompts**: "What's a problem in this field you'd like to understand?" — combines introductions with [Activation](../elements/activation.md)
- **Repeated micro-introductions**: brief [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) at the start of each session rather than one large opening activity
- **Task-based icebreakers**: small collaborative challenges (e.g., a puzzle or ranking task) that generate interaction through shared work rather than self-disclosure
- **Asynchronous introductions**: discussion-board posts with video or text in online courses, with instructor replies to each

### Target Learners
- Students new to an institution or cohort, where peer networks do not yet exist [Belonging interventions improve academic outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- At-risk or first-generation students, for whom belonging uncertainty is a documented barrier to persistence
- Less valuable for established cohorts who already know each other, where the activity can feel redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Community and norms: establishing expectations for [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and collaboration
- Affective entry: reducing anxiety and building instructor approachability
- Activation: surfacing prior experience and interests that can be drawn on later

### Instructions
1. Choose a prompt that is low-stakes and, where possible, connected to course content (activation of prior experience or curiosity).
2. Model it first: the instructor answers the prompt briefly and authentically.
3. Use a structure that guarantees participation — pair-share, then whole-group harvest, or a round of [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md).
4. Record what is shared (names, interests) and visibly reuse it in later sessions — grouping, examples, references.
5. Follow with a low-stakes collaborative task so the social opening converts into academic interaction ([Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md)).

## Examples
- **First-day "course curiosity" round**: each student names one question they hope the course answers; the instructor collects these and returns to them throughout the term, linking introductions to [Activation](../principles/activation.md).
- **P2PU learning circles** ([https://www.p2pu.org](https://www.p2pu.org)) open each peer-led session with a brief check-in round, sustaining community in non-credit adult learning.
- **Asynchronous course intros in Canvas/Moodle forums**: students post a short video or written introduction; the instructor replies individually, establishing presence in online [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) settings.
