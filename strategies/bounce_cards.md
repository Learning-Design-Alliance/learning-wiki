# Bounce Cards

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Bounce cards are printed prompt cards that structure peer-to-peer discussion by giving students three explicit response moves: **Bounce** (elaborate on or extend a peer's idea), **Sum it up** (rephrase what a peer said and comment on it), and **Inquire** (ask a question). Teachers first model each move, then students use the cards to plan and conduct conversations on self-selected or assigned topics. The strategy converts the tacit norms of academic dialogue into concrete, teachable language, making equitable turn-taking and active listening observable and practicable.

## Design Implications

Bounce cards are a form of "talk moves" scaffolding: they externalize the discourse rules of productive classroom dialogue so that participation does not depend on students already possessing conversational skills [Structured academic discourse supports reasoning and comprehension gains.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. By requiring students to rephrase before responding, the cards enforce comprehension checking and elaboration, both of which deepen processing of the discussed content. The structure also redistributes airtime, countering the pattern where a few dominant voices carry whole-class discussion [Active participation structures outperform passive listening for learning outcomes.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Printed or posted prompt cards with sentence stems for each move (e.g., "That reminds me of…", "So what you're saying is…", "What would happen if…")
- Teacher modeling of each move before independent use ([Modeling](../elements/modeling.md)), ideally with think-aloud commentary on *why* the move helps the conversation
- Structured grouping of students and a discussion topic with enough intellectual substance to sustain exchange ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
- Repeated [Practice](../elements/practice.md) opportunities with feedback on conversational moves, not just content

#### Constraints
- Heavy upfront training is required; without modeling and rehearsal, students use the cards mechanically and dialogue quality does not improve [-M]
- Over-reliance on the cards can make talk formulaic; the scaffolds must be faded once conversational norms are internalized [Scaffolds that are not faded can suppress independent strategy use.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [~M]
- Less effective when topics are closed or factual, leaving nothing to bounce, summarize, or inquire about
- Students with limited language proficiency may need simplified stems or additional wait time; open-ended discussion can otherwise disadvantage them [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Physical cards, desk mats, or digital prompts (e.g., shared slides or chat macros in online discussion)
- Sentence stems can be simplified for early elementary or made more discipline-specific for secondary (e.g., "What evidence supports that claim?" in science)
- The three moves map directly onto the broader "talk moves" repertoire; teachers can add moves (challenge, connect to text) as students gain fluency

### Target Learners
- Elementary learners developing self-awareness, listening, and social skills; the concrete stems give novices language they would not generate spontaneously [+M]
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from scripted academic sentence frames [+W]
- Adaptable for secondary and adult learners to improve collaboration quality in group work

### Target Learning Goals
- Oral language and academic discourse: turn-taking, rephrasing, questioning
- Social-emotional learning: active listening, perspective taking, respectful disagreement
- Content elaboration: explaining, summarizing, and questioning ideas deepens conceptual understanding [Self-explanation and elaborative talk improve conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Select a discussion-worthy topic and form small groups ([Collaboration](../elements/collaboration.md)).
2. Introduce and model each of the three moves — Bounce, Sum it up, Inquire — with think-aloud commentary ([Modeling](../elements/modeling.md)).
3. Have students rehearse the moves in pairs with low-stakes content ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Run the discussion: a student shares an idea; responders draw a card and apply the corresponding move ([Peer Discussion](../elements/peer-discussion.md)).
5. Debrief with the whole class on both content and conversational quality ([Whole-Class Sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md)), then fade the cards as norms take hold.

## Examples
- **Elementary science:** Students discuss types of energy using bounce cards; a "Sum it up" stem ("So what you're saying is…") requires rephrasing before adding a new idea.
- **Middle school history:** Students debate causes of a historical event; "Inquire" stems push peers to ask for evidence rather than merely disagree.
- **Facing History and Ourselves** classroom routines (https://www.facinghistory.org) use comparable structured dialogue protocols to scaffold respectful discussion of contested topics.
