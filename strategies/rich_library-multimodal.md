# Rich Library: Multimodal

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (1 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 1 of 4 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
A rich multimodal library is a curated collection of learning resources spanning multiple formats and registers — canonical texts alongside blogs, song lyrics, videos, podcasts, web pages, online discussions, and fan-produced writing. Rather than restricting learners to a single textbook, the instructor assembles a deliberately varied resource set that represents the discipline's genres and connects them to learners' interests and out-of-school literacies. The strategy serves three functions at once: building background knowledge, increasing motivation through choice and relevance, and modeling the full range of texts through which a discipline actually communicates.

## Design Implications

Multimodal resources can support learning when verbal and visual channels are coordinated rather than redundant, consistent with [Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md) [Media combinations affect recall and retention depending on how channels are combined.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [~M]. The disciplinary-literacy rationale is that adolescents learn to read, write, and think like members of a discipline by encountering authentic texts from that discipline in varied forms [~M]. Choice among resources increases engagement and persistence, but only when options are genuinely equivalent in instructional value [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A curated set of resources vetted for accuracy, relevance, and readability — curation, not accumulation, is the design work
- An organizing structure (LMS module, annotated playlist, hyperlinked map) so learners can navigate the library without drowning in it
- Guidance on how to evaluate and compare sources of different reliability and register ([Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md))
- A task that requires learners to actually use multiple resources, not merely browse them
- A curated digital or physical collection spanning multiple formats and difficulty levels, aligned to learning objectives
- A browsing and selection mechanism ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md), tagged search, recommendation lists) so learners can locate relevant resources independently
- A purpose for engagement — a [Product](../elements/product.md), discussion, or [Application](../elements/application.md) task that makes use of what learners explore
- Sufficient independent reading/viewing skills, or scaffolds ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), guided questions) for learners who lack them

#### Constraints
- Unstructured choice among many formats can fragment attention and increase extraneous load; without [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and clear structure, learners skim widely and retain little [~M]
- Redundant presentation — identical text narrated over identical on-screen text — actively harms learning compared with complementary modalities [Media combinations affect recall and retention depending on how channels are combined.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [-S]
- Popular multimodal sources (fanfiction, social media) vary widely in disciplinary accuracy; uncritical inclusion can entrench misconceptions [-M]
- Assumes device and bandwidth access; a "rich library" that lives only online widens gaps for learners lacking home access unless school time and devices are guaranteed [-M]
- Uncurated access degrades into browsing without learning; without a task or accountability structure, engagement with the library does not translate into comprehension gains [-M]
- Multimodal redundancy harms learning: presenting identical content simultaneously as narration and on-screen text splits attention rather than supporting it [~S]
- Learners with weak self-regulation or low prior knowledge often select resources that are too easy, too hard, or off-topic; free choice alone does not produce productive engagement for these learners [-M]
- Volume of exposure without [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md), discussion, or retrieval practice yields shallow encoding regardless of how engaging the resources are [-S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Curated playlists**: instructor selects 5–10 resources per topic at varied reading levels and modalities
- **Choice boards**: learners select a minimum number of resources from a grid, with required and optional items
- **Student-contributed libraries**: learners nominate and annotate resources, building evaluation skills and ownership
- **Tiered libraries**: same content in multiple formats so learners can re-encounter ideas (video first, text second, or vice versa)
- **Curated playlists vs. open browsing:** tightly themed collections (e.g., all resources on ecosystems) suit goal-directed units; open libraries suit interest-driven reading time
- **Format balance:** audiobooks extend access for striving readers [Audiobooks](../elements/audiobooks.md); video suits procedural content; text remains superior for dense conceptual material
- **Structured vs. free choice:** offering a constrained set of 5–10 vetted options captures most motivational benefits while reducing mismatch risk

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners, especially adolescents whose out-of-school literacies (lyrics, fan communities, video) differ from school texts [~M]
- Learners lacking technology access at home, for whom the school library is the primary point of digital access [~W]
- Reluctant readers, who often engage first with non-traditional formats before approaching canonical texts [~W]
- Less effective as-is for learners with strong prior knowledge, who need depth and challenge rather than breadth of entry points [~M]
- Developing readers who benefit from high-interest, accessible formats that build volume of reading and background knowledge [~M]
- Learners with varied interests in a single class, where a single assigned text cannot engage everyone
- Striving readers and English learners, for whom audio and visual formats provide access to grade-level content while decoding skills develop [~M]
- Less effective as-is for novices in a fully unfamiliar domain, who need more guidance than a library provides

### Target Learning Goals
- Disciplinary literacy: recognizing the genres, registers, and epistemic moves of a field
- Background knowledge building: multiple encodings of the same concepts support retention [Media combinations affect recall and retention depending on how channels are combined.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+M]
- Source evaluation and critical reading across formats
- Composition: exposure to varied models improves learners' own writing range [~W]
- Background knowledge building: accumulating domain knowledge that supports later comprehension and transfer
- Motivation and reading engagement: developing interest, autonomy, and sustained reading habits
- Genre and format literacy: recognizing how different media present and structure information

### Instructions
1. Define the disciplinary concepts and genres the unit must cover; map each to candidate resource types.
2. Curate and vet resources for accuracy, level, and modality balance; annotate each with purpose and suggested use ([Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md)).
3. Structure the library with clear entry points and pathways — required core resources plus optional extensions, chunked by topic.
4. Model how to navigate and evaluate the resources, e.g., comparing a news article with a peer-reviewed summary ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
5. Activate relevant knowledge before learners dive in ([Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md)).
6. Assign a task that requires synthesizing across at least two formats, with [Provide Guidance](../elements/provide-guidance.md) on evaluation criteria.
7. Assess both the product and the process — which resources learners used, how they evaluated them, and what they gained from each modality.

## Examples
- **[Newsela](https://newsela.com)** — the same news article at multiple reading levels, pairing text with quizzes; a tiered-library implementation for mixed-readability classrooms.
- **[CommonLit](https://www.commonlit.org)** — free curated text sets pairing canonical literature with contemporary media and related nonfiction.
- **A science unit on climate** pairing an IPCC summary graphic, a documentary clip, a journalist's explainer, and a peer-reviewed abstract, with students comparing how each genre frames uncertainty.
- **An English unit on narrative voice** including a novel chapter, a fanfiction retelling from another character's perspective, and a song lyric, with students analyzing point of view across all three.
- **[Epic!](https://www.getepic.com)** — K–5 digital library of ebooks, audiobooks, and read-to-me titles; teachers assign collections or allow free browsing, with logs tracking reading volume.
- **[Newsela](https://newsela.com)** — Same news articles at multiple reading levels, letting a class read one topic in differentiated formats and discuss it together.
- **[Libby / OverDrive](https://libbyapp.com)** — Public-library ebook and audiobook lending used in classroom units to give every student access to a shared text list across formats.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — A curated multimodal library of videos, articles, and practice organized by topic, illustrating curation-plus-task structure.

4. Build in social processing: [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or peer sharing so learners encounter resources they did not choose.
5. Assess through the product and a brief comprehension or reflection check, not through resource-selection data alone.
