# Non-intensive Home Numeracy Environment (HNE) Intervention Program

## Description
A non-intensive HNE intervention is a low-burden, teacher-initiated program in which educators supply families with guidance, activity suggestions, and concrete materials (games, manipulatives, take-home kits) to enrich the math experiences children encounter at home. Rather than prescribing structured homework, the strategy aims to raise the *frequency* of informal, playful numeracy interactions — board games, counting during routines, measuring while cooking — that research links to children's mathematical development.

## Design Implications

The home numeracy environment — especially the frequency of parent–child activities involving number and calculation — predicts children's arithmetic and number knowledge over and above family income and parental education [Home numeracy activities predict children's early number and arithmetic skills.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2013.12.003) [+M]. Non-intensive interventions work by nudging this environment rather than replacing classroom instruction: they are additive, low-cost, and leverage the personalization that only families can provide. Effects are strongest when materials are concrete and the activities are genuinely playful rather than disguised worksheets [-W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A communication channel between teacher and family (newsletters, apps, take-home folders) with guidance in families' home languages
- Access to math games and manipulatives — lending libraries of classroom materials remove cost barriers
- Activity suggestions framed as play embedded in existing routines (cooking, shopping, travel), not as extra "schoolwork"
- Brief, concrete modeling of *how* to interact (e.g., a one-minute video of a parent playing a counting game), since vague encouragement rarely changes behavior

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends on family uptake; families under economic or time stress participate less, so the intervention can widen rather than narrow gaps if access to materials is not actively supported [-M]
- Formal, didactic home numeracy activities show weaker and sometimes null relations to math outcomes compared with informal play [Formal home numeracy instruction shows weaker, less consistent relations to children's math skills than informal activities.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014532) [~M]
- One-off information sessions without materials or follow-up produce little sustained change in home practice [-W]
- Effects on arithmetic fact retrieval are modest; non-intensive programs cannot substitute for structured [practice](../elements/practice.md) at school [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Take-home kits**: rotating bags of games, dice, and manipulatives with simple instruction cards
- **Math-lending libraries**: classroom materials checked out weekly, mirroring book-bag programs
- **Digital prompts**: short text/app messages suggesting a numeracy moment for that day (e.g., "count the stairs together")
- **Family math nights**: in-person events where parents practice the games before taking them home
- **Embedded curricula**: programs like *Bedtime Math* (Carnegie Mellon's [Bedtime Math app](https://bedtimemath.org)) deliver a daily story with a math question, requiring almost no parent preparation

### Target Learners
- Preschool and early-elementary children (ages ~3–8), for whom informal number play most strongly predicts later arithmetic [Home numeracy activities predict children's early number and arithmetic skills.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2013.12.003) [+M]
- Families with limited confidence in math, who benefit most from scripted, low-preparation activities
- Children with limited access to math materials at home, when kits and lending libraries remove cost barriers

### Target Learning Goals
- Early number sense: counting, cardinality, comparison, and one-to-one correspondence
- Arithmetic fact retrieval through repeated, low-stakes, playful exposure
- Positive math attitudes — associating math with enjoyable family interaction rather than anxiety
- Spatial and constructional skills through block and building play [Block play predicts math achievement.](../claims/block-play-predicts-math-achievement.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Audit the current environment.** Survey families briefly about existing math activities and material access to tailor suggestions.
2. **Curate a lending collection.** Select games and manipulatives matched to grade-level goals (dice, dominoes, measuring tapes, blocks); pair each with a one-card "how to play and what to notice" guide.
3. **Model the interaction.** Demonstrate a game or math-talk routine at a family night or via short video, making the numeracy conversation explicit rather than assuming parents will infer it.
4. **Send home materials and routines.** Rotate kits weekly; include suggestions embedded in daily routines (cooking, shopping, bedtime) so practice is frequent and personalized.
5. **Follow up lightly.** Use check-ins, photos, or a simple return log to sustain engagement; refresh activities as children's skills grow.

## Related Strategies
- Take-home reading programs — the literacy analogue; the same lending-library mechanics apply, and dialogic reading research shows guided parent–child interaction outperforms unstructured exposure [Dialogic reading improves children's expressive language.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+S]
- Family math nights — the in-person modeling component of a non-intensive HNE program
- Classroom math games — the same games used at school first, then sent home, create continuity

## Examples
- **[Bedtime Math](https://bedtimemath.org)** — A free app and book series delivering a daily short story with leveled math questions; a randomized study found gains in children's math achievement over the school year, largest for children of math-anxious parents (Berkowitz et al., 2015).
- **Take-home math kit programs** — Many early-childhood programs circulate "math backpacks" containing a game, manipulatives, and an activity card; the lending model removes cost barriers and signals that math play is valued.
- **Family Math (Lawrence Hall of Science, UC Berkeley)** — A published activity curriculum ([https://lawrencehallofscience.org](https://www.lawrencehallofscience.org/do-science/family-math)) providing household-math activities designed for home use.
