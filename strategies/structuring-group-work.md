# Structuring Group Work

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Structuring group work means designing collaborative tasks so that interaction is productive: each member has a defined role or contribution, individual accountability is built into the task, and the dialogue patterns that drive learning are prompted explicitly. Unstructured "get into groups and discuss" reliably produces unequal participation and social loafing; structure — roles, protocols, interdependent tasks, and assessment of individual learning — is what converts grouping into [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md).

## Design Implications

Group work improves outcomes when it creates genuine interdependence and individual accountability, not merely proximity [Cooperative learning approaches with these two features outperform individualistic and competitive structures.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. The mechanism is elaborative dialogue: explaining, questioning, and negotiating meaning forces learners to articulate and reorganize their understanding [Collaborative writing improves text quality when students must coordinate and negotiate content.](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+M]. Without structure, higher-ability students dominate, low-ability students disengage, and the group converges on the first plausible answer.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A task that genuinely requires multiple contributions — one that cannot be completed by the strongest member alone
- Individual accountability: each member's learning or contribution is separately visible (individual quiz, assigned role output, random reporter)
- Explicit roles or interaction protocols ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md), discussion sentence stems, turn-taking rules)
- Group processing time: brief reflection on how the team worked together

#### Constraints
- Groups formed without interdependence invite social loafing and free-riding [-S] — accountability must be individual, not just a shared grade
- Group grades without individual accountability can penalize strong members and mask non-learning [-M]
- Tasks below or far above group capability produce off-task talk or premature convergence; poorly designed tasks add coordination load that crowds out learning [Excessive extraneous demands during collaboration degrade learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]
- Simply placing students in groups without training in collaborative skills yields weak effects, especially for young or novice learners [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- [Jigsaw](../patterns/cooperative-learning.md) variants: each member masters a segment and teaches it, forcing dependence on peers
- Think-pair-share and peer instruction: brief structured pairs rather than long-term teams
- Reciprocal roles: rotating facilitator, recorder, skeptic, reporter across sessions
- Online adaptations: shared documents with named sections, discussion protocols with posting quotas, breakout rooms with assigned reporter roles

### Target Learners
- Learners across achievement levels benefit when tasks are structured; low-achieving students gain from peer explanation, high-achieving students gain from the rehearsal effects of explaining [Cooperative learning with individual accountability benefits both high and low achievers.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Students at risk of marginalization benefit from structures that guarantee voice and membership [Structured belonging supports improve outcomes for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Novices need more task structure and role scaffolding than experienced collaborators, who can self-organize [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding through elaboration and peer explanation
- Argumentation and reasoning: defending, critiquing, and revising ideas ([Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
- Collaborative and communication skills as outcomes in their own right
- Transfer of verbal knowledge into usable, articulated understanding ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))

### Instructions
1. Define the task so it requires interdependent contributions; avoid tasks a single strong student could complete alone.
2. Assign roles or positions ([Assigned Positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md)) and teach the interaction protocol explicitly before starting.
3. Set an individual accountability mechanism — individual exit questions, random reporter selection, or role-specific deliverables ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
4. Monitor groups, listening for explanation and questioning rather than answer-sharing; intervene with questions, not answers.
5. Close with individual synthesis and brief group processing ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)) on collaboration quality.

## Examples
- **Jigsaw classroom (Aronson)** — students become experts on one segment in "expert groups," then teach it in mixed "jigsaw groups"; the structure makes each member's contribution indispensable. [https://www.jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org)
- **Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard physics)** — individual clicker vote, structured peer debate, revote; individual accountability via the initial and final votes.
- **PALS (Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies, Vanderbilt)** — structured reciprocal peer tutoring pairs with scripted roles and point systems in reading and math. [https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu](https://iris.peabody.vanderbilt.edu)
