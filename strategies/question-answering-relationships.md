# Question Answering Relationships

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Question Answering Relationships (QAR), developed by Taffy Raphael, teaches learners to categorize questions into types based on the source of the answer: **Right There** (answer stated in one place in the text), **Think and Search** (answer assembled from multiple places in the text), **Author and Me** (answer requires combining text information with prior knowledge), and **On My Own** (answerable from prior knowledge alone, without the text). Learners first learn to label question types, then to use the classification to decide how to search for and construct answers. The strategy makes the otherwise tacit relationship between questions, texts, and the reader's own knowledge explicit and teachable.

## Design Implications

QAR improves comprehension by shifting learners' attention from "finding the answer" to reasoning about *where* the answer lives — a metacognitive move that supports strategic reading [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]. Explicit strategy instruction of this kind is most effective when the strategy is named, modeled, and practiced across varied texts [Teaching reading strategies improves comprehension outcomes.](../claims/reading-strategy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+S]. Because QAR makes the text–reader relationship visible, it directly addresses the common novice error of treating all questions as literal "search and copy" tasks, even inferential ones.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Texts with questions already attached, or a question set the teacher can classify in advance
- Explicit instruction on the four QAR categories with labeled examples of each
- [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling of how an expert decides which category a question belongs to
- Guided practice classifying questions before learners answer them independently

#### Constraints
- Over-reliance on the four labels can become a mechanical sorting exercise; benefits diminish when classification is drilled without discussion of *why* a question maps to a category [Strategy instruction effects weaken when taught as routines without flexible application.](../claims/reading-strategy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [~M]
- Less effective with learners who lack the prior knowledge needed for "Author and Me" and "On My Own" questions [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [~M]
- The two-level version (In the Book / In My Head) is more manageable for younger learners; the four-category version can overload them [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Effects fade if instruction stops at classification; learners must go on to answer questions using the appropriate strategy

#### Implementation Variability
- **Two-level QAR** ("In the Book" vs. "In My Head") for elementary learners; four-level for upper elementary and beyond
- **Student-generated questions**: learners write questions of each type, deepening understanding of the text–question relationship
- **Cross-curricular adaptation**: applied to mathematics word problems (what does the problem give me vs. what must I know?) and to source evaluation in history

### Target Learners
- Upper elementary through secondary readers who over-rely on literal retrieval [+S]
- Struggling readers who benefit from explicit, named strategies rather than implicit expectations [Teaching reading strategies improves comprehension outcomes.](../claims/reading-strategy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Multilingual learners, when paired with [Activation](../elements/activation.md) of prior knowledge needed for "In My Head" questions

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: distinguishing literal from inferential understanding
- Metacognition: monitoring whether one is answering from the text or from one's own knowledge
- Question-asking: learning to generate and evaluate questions of different cognitive types

### Instructions
1. Introduce the four QAR categories with a short text and one example question per category, labeling each aloud.
2. Model the classification decision with a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md): "The answer to this one is stated right here, so it's Right There."
3. Have learners classify a mixed question set before answering, discussing disagreements — some questions legitimately straddle categories.
4. Answer the questions, using the category to guide the approach: search the text for Right There and Think and Search; activate prior knowledge and combine it with text for Author and Me and On My Own.
5. Fade the labels: move from classifying provided questions to [student-generated questions](question-generation.md) across new texts.

## Examples
- **Raphael's original program** (1986) — middle-grade students taught QAR showed improved ability to answer inferential questions and better awareness of answer sources.
- **Question-Answer Relationships in basal reading programs** — many published ELA curricula (e.g., Houghton Mifflin's reading series) embed QAR labels on comprehension questions.
- **Mathematics adaptation** — teachers classify word problems by whether needed information is given in the problem (Right There / Think and Search) or must be supplied from known facts (Author and Me / On My Own).
