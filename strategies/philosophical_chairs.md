# Philosophical Chairs

## Description
A statement that has two possible responses—agree or disagree—is read out loud. Depending on whether they agree or disagree with this statement, learners move to one side of the room or the other. From that spot, learners take turns defending their positions. A Philosophical Chairs debate is often based around a text or group of texts learners have read ahead of time; learners are required to cite textual evidence to support their claims and usually hold the texts in their hands during the discussion. Some teachers set up one hot seat to represent each side, and learners must take turns in the seat. Teachers may also opt to offer a continuum of choices, ranging from “Strongly Agree” on one side of the room, all the way to “Strongly Disagree” on the other, and have learners place themselves along that continuum based on the strength of their convictions.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Requires a physical space where learners can move freely, a provocative statement or question, and clear instructions for learners. Texts or resources for learners to cite as evidence.
#### Constraints
- May be challenging for learners who are shy or reluctant to express their opinions. Requires careful facilitation to ensure respectful communication and avoid conflict. Can reinforce binary thinking if not carefully structured.
