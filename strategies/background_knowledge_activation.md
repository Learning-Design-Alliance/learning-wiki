# Background Knowledge Activation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Before engaging in text-based discussions or new content, instructors deliberately surface and activate learners' background knowledge related to the text's setting, themes, or concepts. This is typically done through open-ended questions, brief free-writes, or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) that prompt learners to connect prior experiences to the new material. Activation makes relevant schemas available in working memory, so incoming information can be integrated rather than encoded in isolation.

## Design Implications

Activation works because comprehension depends on linking new information to existing knowledge structures; without retrieval cues, relevant schemas remain inert and learners process text word-by-word rather than meaningfully [Ausubel's advance organizer research shows pre-organizing relevant prior knowledge improves retention of meaningful material.](../elements/advance-organizers.md) [+M]. The quality of activation matters more than its presence: questions must target knowledge that is genuinely relevant to the task, and instructors must surface *and correct* inaccurate prior knowledge, since activating a misconception strengthens it [~S]. Activation is a preparation step, not a substitute for the learning activity that follows — it should be brief and feed directly into discussion or reading.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Knowledge of learners' backgrounds, gathered through pre-assessment or informal check-ins
- Open-ended questions that target knowledge actually relevant to the upcoming content, not general warm-ups
- A mechanism for addressing misconceptions once surfaced ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), instructor correction, or [Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md))
- A follow-on task where the activated knowledge is immediately used

#### Constraints
- Activating inaccurate or irrelevant background knowledge can impair comprehension; learners with high-confidence misconceptions may integrate new information into faulty schemas [~S]
- Prior knowledge alone does not guarantee performance — activation without instructional support shows weak or null effects when the knowledge is not directly relevant to the task [Prior knowledge is not reliably related to performance unless it is task-relevant and activated.](../claims/prior-knowledge-not-related-to-performance.md) [~M]
- Questions that are culturally distant from some learners' experiences can exclude rather than include, widening participation gaps [-M]
- Over-long activation routines consume time and working memory resources needed for the main task [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) — individual recall, then peer elaboration, then whole-class pooling
- Anticipation guides — learners commit to predictions about the text before reading, then revisit them
- [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) — a structured overview presented before the material, rather than elicited from learners
- Analogy-based activation — mapping a familiar domain onto the new one ([Analogies and Prior Knowledge Activation](../elements/analogies-and-prior-knowledge-activation.md))

### Target Learners
- Learners encountering a topic outside their experience, who lack spontaneous retrieval of relevant schemas [+M]
- Struggling readers, who rarely activate relevant knowledge spontaneously while reading [~S]
- Less effective for learners with strong domain knowledge, who activate relevant schemas automatically and may find elicitation redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading comprehension: connecting text content to existing mental models
- Conceptual understanding: integrating new concepts with prior conceptions [Self-explanation of connections improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]
- Engagement and relevance: making content personally meaningful [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Identify the background knowledge the target text or topic presupposes, and check which learners hold it.
2. Pose 1–3 open-ended questions eliciting relevant experience or knowledge ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
3. Surface misconceptions explicitly and address them before reading or discussion ([Cognitive Conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md)).
4. Bridge to the new content with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) or [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) linking elicited knowledge to the task.
5. Require learners to use the activated knowledge immediately in discussion or writing ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).

## Examples
- **Anticipation guides** — Before reading a history text, learners agree/disagree with statements about its themes, then revise answers after reading; widely used in adolescent literacy programs.
- **Reciprocal Teaching** ([https://www.readingrockets.org/strategies/reciprocal_teaching](https://www.readingrockets.org/strategies/reciprocal_teaching)) — opens each session by prompting students to recall what they already know about the topic before predicting and summarizing.
- **Fountas & Pinnell guided reading** — pre-reading book introductions that connect the text's setting and vocabulary to children's lived experience before reading begins.
