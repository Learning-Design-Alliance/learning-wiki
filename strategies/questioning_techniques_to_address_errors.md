# Questioning Techniques to Address Errors

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Strategic questioning is used to guide students to recognize their errors and correct their thinking, emphasizing conceptual understanding over rote memorization. Rather than directly correcting an error, the teacher asks questions that direct attention to the student's own reasoning — the reasonableness of the answer, the underlying concepts, and the steps that produced the mistake. The teacher acts as a facilitator, guiding the student through self-discovery and correction.

## Design Implications

Questioning that targets the *reasoning behind* an error converts a mistake into a site for conceptual change: when learners detect an inconsistency in their own thinking, they are more likely to revise their underlying conceptions than when they are simply told the right answer [Cognitive conflict motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]. The approach works only when questions are calibrated to the learner's current understanding — questions pitched too far beyond it produce guessing rather than reflection [Scaffolding that is contingent on learner understanding improves outcomes.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Skilled teachers who can diagnose the source of an error and formulate questions that expose it without giving the answer away
- A classroom climate in which students feel safe to respond honestly and treat errors as information, not failure
- Concrete materials where relevant (e.g., base-10 blocks or play money in math interventions) so questions can direct attention to manipulable representations
- Time for genuine dialogue; the strategy is inherently interactive ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness depends heavily on teacher questioning skill; poorly framed questions ("Are you sure?") prompt answer-swapping rather than reasoning review [~M]
- Time-intensive, especially in large classes — difficult to sustain as a whole-class technique
- Students with very low prior knowledge often cannot self-correct through questioning alone and may need more direct correction [~S]
- If students fear embarrassment, they will give socially safe answers instead of revealing their reasoning, defeating the diagnostic purpose [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Socratic questioning**: open-ended probes ("Why does that make sense?") suited to conceptual misunderstandings
- **Funneling vs. focusing**: funneling questions narrow toward the correct procedure; focusing questions keep attention on the student's own reasoning — focusing produces more durable learning [~M]
- **Error-analysis routines**: structured protocols (e.g., "My Favorite No") where the class analyzes an anonymous error collectively
- **Self-questioning prompts**: teaching students to ask the checking questions themselves ("Does this answer make sense?"), shifting the strategy toward [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) and self-regulation

### Target Learners
- Learners who have partial but flawed understanding — enough knowledge to reason with, but misconceptions to surface [Cognitive conflict motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]
- Students who benefit from guided inquiry and self-discovery rather than direct correction
- Less effective for complete novices, who lack the knowledge to evaluate their own reasoning [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Deepen conceptual understanding by connecting errors to underlying principles
- Develop metacognitive monitoring — the habit of checking one's own work and reasoning
- Build mathematical or disciplinary discourse skills through explaining and justifying

### Instructions
1. **Diagnose the error** — determine whether the mistake is procedural, conceptual, or careless before choosing questions ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) as diagnosis)
2. **Ask a question that targets the reasoning, not the answer** — e.g., "Do we have enough ones to take 7 ones from 3 ones?" rather than "That's wrong, try again"
3. **Have the student articulate their thinking** — require them to explain what they did and why ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
4. **Guide re-evaluation** — use follow-up questions to help the student detect the inconsistency themselves, stepping in with more direct guidance only if they stall ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
5. **Confirm and consolidate** — once corrected, have the student restate the corrected reasoning in their own words and apply it to a new problem ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Application](../elements/application.md))

## Related Strategies
- [Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) — errors often stem from misapplied prior knowledge; questioning surfaces which prior idea is being invoked
- [Action-Oriented Feedback](../strategies/action-oriented_feedback.md) — questioning is a form of feedback that tells learners *where their thinking went wrong* rather than what the answer is

## Examples
- Instead of telling a student they cannot subtract a larger number from a smaller number, ask: "Do we have enough ones to take 7 ones from 3 ones?" Instead of directly correcting an incorrectly renamed minuend, ask: "Does 8 tens and 13 tens equal 83?"
- **Cognitively Guided Instruction** ([CGI for Math](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md)) — teachers elicit children's informal solution strategies through questioning before introducing formal procedures, treating incorrect strategies as windows into thinking
- **"My Favorite No"** — a widely used classroom routine in which an anonymous incorrect answer is displayed and the class is questioned to identify what is right about it and where the reasoning breaks down
