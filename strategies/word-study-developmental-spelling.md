# Word Study Developmental Spelling

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Word study is a developmental approach to spelling instruction grounded in the research tradition of Henderson, Templeton, and colleagues. Learners are assessed to determine their stage of orthographic knowledge — typically emergent, letter-name, within-word pattern, syllables and affixes, or derivational relations — and instruction targets the features at the edge of that stage. The core activity is the [word sort](word-sort.md): learners categorize contrastive word sets (e.g., short vs. long vowel patterns) by visual pattern, sound, and meaning, comparing and contrasting rather than memorizing.

## Design Implications

Word study treats spelling as a problem-solving activity in which learners discover generalizations about how English orthography maps sound, pattern, and meaning, rather than as rote memorization of word lists [~M]. Because learners examine multiple exemplars of a pattern side by side, the approach depends on structured comparison — the same mechanism that makes contrasting cases effective for pattern discovery [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S]. Instruction is deliberately sequenced so that each feature is taught when learners are developmentally "ready," avoiding features that are too far beyond current knowledge.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A valid spelling inventory (e.g., the [Words Their Way](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/words-their-way-word-study-for-phonics-vocabulary-and-spelling-instruction/P200000003485) Elementary Spelling Inventory) to place learners in a developmental stage
- Curated word sets that contrast target features with known features, so sorting requires discrimination rather than guessing
- Regular re-assessment and regrouping, since stages shift unevenly across learners
- Time for hands-on manipulation of words (cards, notebooks) followed by writing application

#### Constraints
- Whole-class, one-list instruction fails the approach's core premise: a single list will be too advanced for some learners and redundant for others, producing either overload or disengagement [-M]
- Sorting without follow-up writing application yields weak transfer to actual spelling in composition [-M]; sorts must connect to connected text
- Learners with weak phonological awareness cannot benefit from pattern-level sorts until sound-level knowledge is secure [~S]
- English learners may sort by sound accurately while missing meaning-based features (e.g., homophone pairs), requiring explicit attention to vocabulary

#### Implementation Variability
- Small-group rotation models (as in Words Their Way) vs. whole-class feature-of-the-week with differentiated sort lists
- Open sorts (learner-generated categories) for advanced stages vs. closed sorts (teacher-defined) for introducing new features
- Digital adaptations (e.g., [Words Their Way Online](https://www.pearson.com), [Spelling City](https://www.spellingcity.com) custom lists) that preserve sorting mechanics

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers in the letter-name and within-word pattern stages, who are consolidating [automatic word recognition](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Struggling spellers in upper grades, provided instruction is placed at their actual developmental stage rather than grade level
- Less appropriate for learners already in the derivational-relations stage, who benefit more from morphology and etymology than from phonetic sorts [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Orthographic pattern knowledge: generalizations about vowel patterns, consonant blends, syllable juncture
- Word recognition automaticity, which frees working memory for comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension.](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]
- Vocabulary and morphology: meaning-based features (prefixes, suffixes, Latin/Greek roots) in later stages

### Instructions
1. Administer a developmental spelling inventory and score it feature-by-feature to identify each learner's stage.
2. Form flexible groups and select a feature at each group's instructional edge — one the learner uses but confuses.
3. Design a closed sort contrasting the target feature with a known feature (e.g., CVC short *a* vs. CVCe long *a*), drawing on [chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of words into pattern families.
4. Model sorting 2–3 words aloud, then have learners sort, read, and check their sorts in small groups.
5. Follow with writing application: dictation sentences and use of target words in [authentic writing](authentic-writing.md), so patterns transfer to composition.
6. Re-assess every 2–4 weeks and regroup; move to the next feature when learners spell the current one correctly in writing, not just in sorts.
