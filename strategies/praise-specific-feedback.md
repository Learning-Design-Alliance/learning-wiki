# Praise Specific Feedback

## Description
Praise specific feedback is praise that identifies *what* the learner did — the strategy, effort, process, or feature of the work — rather than delivering a global judgment ("good job," "you're so smart"). It functions as informational feedback: it tells learners which actions to repeat and connects outcomes to controllable causes. It is carried out by pairing an evaluative statement with a concrete description of the behavior or work that earned it.

## Design Implications

Generic praise is processed as social evaluation; specific praise functions as information about the task, which is what feedback research identifies as the mechanism of learning gains [~S]. Praise directed at process and strategy supports persistence and adaptive attributions, while person-directed praise ("you're talented") encourages fixed-ability attributions and avoidance of challenge [~S]. Because praise is also a social reward, overuse or use as control can undermine intrinsic motivation for tasks learners already find interesting [~S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Observation of the actual work or behavior — specific praise cannot be faked from a distance
- A link between the praise and a criterion the learner understands (what "good" looks like)
- Immediacy for behavior shaping; specificity for learning from products

#### Constraints
- Praise for easy, already-mastered tasks signals low expectations and can reduce effort [~S]
- Person-praise ("you're brilliant") after success sets up failure as evidence of inability, reducing persistence after setbacks [~S]
- Controlling praise ("I like the way *you're supposed to* do it") can crowd out intrinsic motivation, especially for interesting tasks [~S]
- In public settings, praise can trigger embarrassment or peer backlash, particularly in adolescence

#### Implementation Variability
- **Process praise** ("you tried a second strategy when the first failed") — best for building persistence
- **Strategy praise** ("your topic sentence sets up the paragraph") — best for product quality
- **Private vs. public** — private for shy or adolescent learners; public for establishing norms in younger groups
- **Written vs. verbal** — written margin comments allow specificity at scale in large classes

### Target Learners
- Younger learners and novices, for whom praise helps identify which behaviors to repeat [~S]
- Learners with low self-efficacy, who benefit from hearing exactly which actions produced success
- Less effective — and sometimes counterproductive — for high-achievers praised for ability rather than strategy [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral and procedural goals: reinforcing specific actions and study strategies
- Motivational goals: building growth-oriented attributions and persistence after difficulty
- Quality of products: directing attention to the features that make work effective

### Instructions
1. Observe the work or behavior closely enough to name a concrete detail ([Check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) and [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) provide the observation points).
2. Name the specific action, strategy, or feature — not the person's traits.
3. Where useful, link it to the criterion or to the learner's controllable effort ("you revised your thesis after the draft feedback — that's why the argument holds together now").
4. Keep praise proportionate; reserve it for genuine accomplishment to preserve credibility.
5. Follow with a next step or question to keep the focus on the work, not the evaluation ([Action-oriented Feedback](../strategies/action-oriented-feedback.md)).

## Examples
- A writing teacher's margin comment: "Your second paragraph anticipates the counterargument — that's what makes this persuasive," rather than "Great essay!"
- [ClassDojo](https://www.classdojo.com) and similar behavior-tracking tools award points for named behaviors ("helped a classmate"), though effectiveness depends on the specificity of the behavior labels, not the points themselves.
- Responsive Classroom's teacher language training distinguishes "reinforcing language" (naming the specific behavior) from generic "good job" praise: [https://www.responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)
