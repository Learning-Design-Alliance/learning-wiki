# Gallery Quote Walk

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A Gallery Quote Walk is a social-emotional check-in routine. Quotes — about learning, effort, identity, or the day's topic — are posted around the room (or on a shared digital board). Students circulate, read the quotes, stand beside the one that best matches their current state of mind, and briefly explain or discuss their choice with others who gathered there. The physical act of choosing and the public placement of the body make internal states visible without requiring extended disclosure.

## Design Implications

The strategy combines embodied choice with low-stakes [activation](../elements/activation.md): it surfaces affective and dispositional information the instructor can use to calibrate the lesson, while giving students autonomy over how they represent themselves [Autonomy support improves intrinsic motivation and engagement.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]. Because participation is structured around a concrete, self-selected artifact rather than direct questioning, it lowers the social cost of sharing and supports belonging, particularly at the start of a course or a difficult topic.

### Context
#### Requirements
- 5–8 curated quotes that are genuinely distinct in tone (not all variations of "I'm excited"), so the choice is diagnostic rather than performative
- Space to move, or an equivalent digital analog (e.g., a Jamboard/Slido quadrant board) for remote or hybrid settings
- A brief sharing or debrief structure so choices are heard, not just made
- Instructor follow-through: the data gathered must visibly shape the session, or students learn the check-in is theater

#### Constraints
- Students who feel socially exposed may choose performatively rather than honestly, especially if standing near peers they want to impress [~M]
- Repeated use with the same quote set produces ritualized, unreflective responses; quotes must rotate and connect to current content
- In large lecture halls without movement space, the embodied component collapses into a poll, losing much of the affective signal [-W]
- Groups with low relational trust may treat the sharing step as a compliance exercise rather than genuine disclosure

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-lesson diagnostic:** quotes about readiness and prior experience with the topic ("I've done this before" / "This is new to me") to inform grouping
- **Mid-unit reflection:** quotes about struggle and persistence, positioned as a [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) before high-stakes work
- **Text-based variant:** quotes drawn from the assigned reading; students stand by the line that struck them, merging the check-in with [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)
- **Silent variant:** students stand by a quote without speaking, then write a one-line rationale — appropriate for low-trust or early-semester groups

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults; works across K-12, higher education, and professional development
- Students in courses where affect strongly shapes performance — e.g., math anxiety, writing apprehension — because naming a state is a first step to regulating it [~W]
- Multilingual learners, when quotes are short and linguistically accessible [Accessible vocabulary and syntax reduce comprehension barriers.](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional: self-awareness and affect labeling
- Metacognitive: surfacing beliefs about one's own learning and readiness
- Community-building: establishing shared norms that struggle and uncertainty are speakable
- Content activation: when quotes come from course material, priming engagement with the topic

### Instructions
1. Post 5–8 quotes around the room before students arrive; keep them visible and legible from a distance.
2. Give a one-sentence prompt ("Stand by the quote that matches how you're feeling about today's topic") and 2–3 minutes to circulate and choose.
3. Have students share their choice with a neighbor or their quote-group; optionally ask one volunteer per quote to say why.
4. Debrief briefly: note patterns aloud ("Several of us are near 'confused but curious' — good, that's where learning happens") and adjust the lesson plan visibly in response.
5. Close the loop later in the session or at the next meeting by returning to the quotes, so the check-in connects to [assessment for learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) rather than standing alone.

## Examples
- **Opening a high-anxiety course:** A statistics instructor posts quotes from famous statisticians about error and uncertainty; students' clustering near "I break things until they work" signals a productive framing to build on in the first lecture.
- **Text-based seminar launch:** An English class posts six lines from the night's reading; students stand by the line that most provoked them, and the resulting groups become the first discussion pods.
- **Remote adaptation:** On Zoom, the instructor displays a numbered quote slide and students type their number in chat, then breakout rooms group by number — preserving choice and sharing while losing the embodied element.
