# Dual Coding

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Dual coding pairs verbal information (words, narration, text) with a complementary visual representation (diagram, animation, image, graph) so that content is processed through both the verbal and visual channels of working memory. The two representations are not redundant — each carries part of the meaning — and learners benefit most when they actively integrate the two into a single coherent mental model. The strategy draws on Paivio's dual coding theory and Mayer's multimedia learning research.

## Design Implications

Combining words and pictures consistently outperforms words alone for transfer and retention, provided the two are integrated rather than split [Words and pictures presented together produce better learning than words alone.](../claims/words-and-pictures-better-than-words-alone.md) [+S]. The visual must be *relevant* to the verbal message: decorative images that do not carry meaning add load without adding learning [Irrelevant graphics and text reduce learning by consuming working memory.](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [+S]. Integration is not automatic — learners need cues, proximity, and sometimes explicit prompts to connect the verbal and visual channels, and overloaded visuals can degrade learning just as overloaded text can [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A visual that genuinely represents the content structure (process diagram, concept map, annotated image), not decoration
- Spatial and temporal contiguity: labels near the relevant visual parts; narration synchronized with animation ([Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md))
- Learner activity that forces integration — labeling, self-explaining, or drawing — rather than passive viewing
- Text and narration managed so they do not compete for the same channel (avoid simultaneous on-screen text plus identical narration)

#### Constraints
- Redundant presentation — identical text spoken aloud and shown on screen — impairs learning compared with narration plus image [Redundant on-screen text with narration impairs learning.](../claims/redundancy-hurts-learning.md) [-S]
- Decorative or seductive visuals unrelated to the learning goal reduce retention and transfer [Irrelevant graphics and text reduce learning by consuming working memory.](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [-S]
- Splitting related information across space or time (e.g., a legend far from a chart) forces costly visual search and reduces learning [Integrated presentation beats split-attention presentation.](../claims/split-attention-effect-integration-improves-learning.md) [+S]
- Complex, high-element-interactivity content can overload the visual channel; [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) and segmenting the visual into steps is required [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]

#### Implementation Variability
- Static diagrams vs. animation: animation is not uniformly better; simple statics with learner pacing often match or beat animation [~M]
- Learner-generated visuals: having students draw or complete diagrams (drawing effect, [Concept Mapping](../elements/concept-mapping.md)) produces strong gains but requires scaffolding for novices
- Text-first vs. image-first sequencing: for unfamiliar learners, a brief advance visual organizer before the verbal explanation helps ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))

### Target Learners
- Novices benefit most, because pictures supply a missing mental model that words alone cannot build quickly [Words and pictures presented together produce better learning than words alone.](../claims/words-and-pictures-better-than-words-alone.md) [+S]
- Low prior-knowledge learners gain more from well-designed multimedia; high-knowledge learners may not need the picture and can find it redundant [~M]
- Learners with strong spatial ability extract more from visuals; those with weak spatial ability need simpler, segmented graphics

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: building structural mental models of systems, processes, and relationships
- Retention: two memory traces (verbal + visual) give two retrieval routes
- Transfer: integrated verbal-visual models support applying knowledge to new situations

### Instructions
1. Identify the core structure of the content (sequence, hierarchy, causality, parts-whole) and choose a visual type that matches it.
2. Write or script the verbal explanation, then strip any words the visual already conveys ([Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md)).
3. Integrate words and picture: place labels adjacent to the relevant visual elements; narrate in sync with any animation.
4. Add integration prompts — ask learners to label a diagram, complete a partially drawn one, or self-explain the connection between image and text ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
5. Remove all decorative imagery and background music that does not serve the objective.
6. Check for redundancy: if narrating, do not display the full narration text on screen.

## Examples
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — narrated problem solving on a shared visual workspace; the writing appears as it is spoken, maintaining temporal contiguity.
- **PhET Interactive Simulations ([https://phet.colorado.edu](https://phet.colorado.edu))** — science simulations pairing dynamic visuals with minimal on-screen text; students manipulate the visual model while the verbal explanation is delivered separately.
- **Anatomy and physiology instruction** — labeled anatomical diagrams with narration replacing paragraph-length text descriptions, a standard application in medical education multimedia.
