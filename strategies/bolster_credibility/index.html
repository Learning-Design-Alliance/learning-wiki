Bolster Credibility¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 2 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Bolster Credibility is a media-literacy strategy centered on a single diagnostic question: did the writer or speaker engage with anyone who disagrees? A credible source calls the senator whose legislation it criticizes, presents the strongest version of the opposing argument, and lets a challenged guest actually answer. Learners use this criterion both to evaluate sources they consume and to strengthen their own arguments — wrestling with opposing views is a service to the reader and typically improves the argument itself.

Design Implications¶

The strategy exploits the contrast between genuine engagement and strawman representation: learners who compare how a source treats its critics develop a transferable heuristic for source evaluation that goes beyond surface cues like design or tone. Presenting contrasting positions side by side supports abstraction of what "fair treatment of disagreement" looks like Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction. [+M]. However, exposure to opposing views alone does not guarantee open-mindedness — people assimilate counterattitudinal information in biased ways, interpreting ambiguous evidence as supporting their prior position [~S]. Structured prompts to articulate the opposing case, rather than merely reading it, are what convert exposure into understanding Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to news sources and opinion pieces that vary in how they treat opposing viewpoints, so learners can contrast engagement with avoidance
  • A protocol or rubric for the diagnostic question (e.g., "Does the source name and represent its critics' strongest argument, or a weakened version?")
  • Structured discussion or writing tasks that require learners to state the opposing case in a form its proponents would accept (Argumentation, Class Discussion)

Constraints¶

  • Simply exposing learners to opposing views can backfire: biased assimilation leads partisans to rate disagreeing evidence as weaker and to polarize further [~S]
  • Corrections of politically charged misperceptions sometimes fail or strengthen false beliefs when they threaten identity [-M]
  • Requiring engagement with opposing views without scaffolding produces shallow, ritualized "on the other hand" gestures rather than genuine steelmanning [-W]
  • Learners with low topic knowledge cannot distinguish a strong opposing argument from a weak one, so the heuristic misfires [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Source comparison: learners analyze two articles on the same event — one that quotes critics, one that does not — and rate credibility
  • Steelmanning exercise: learners write the best possible version of the argument they find least persuasive, then have a proponent assess it
  • Interview analysis: learners evaluate a broadcast interview on whether the guest faced genuine opposing views and was required to answer
  • Authorial practice: in writing instruction, learners apply the criterion to their own drafts — who did I not consult? what would my strongest critic say?

Target Learners¶

  • Secondary and post-secondary students in civics, journalism, or media literacy courses
  • Learners developing argumentation and research writing skills, who must anticipate counterarguments
  • General adult audiences building news-consumption habits; the heuristic is simple enough for novices but requires topic knowledge to apply well [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Critical evaluation of sources: distinguishing genuine engagement with disagreement from strawmanning
  • Argument construction: strengthening one's own claims by addressing the strongest opposing case
  • Epistemic humility: recognizing that wrestling with opposing arguments improves the argument

Instructions¶

  1. Introduce the diagnostic question with a worked contrast: two sources on the same topic, one that engages critics and one that does not (Case Studies).
  2. Have learners annotate each source, marking where opposing views appear, how they are characterized, and whether the critic's strongest or weakest version is presented (Annotating).
  3. Prompt learners to articulate the opposing case in their own words and check it against the source's representation (Self-Explanation [+M]).
  4. Apply the criterion to learners' own writing: identify the strongest objection to their claim and revise to address it (Argumentation).
  5. Discuss in class how the criterion changed their credibility judgments, surfacing cases where engagement with disagreement changed their own view (Class Discussion).

Related Strategies¶

  • Lateral Reading — verifying source credibility by leaving the page and checking what others say; complements the internal-evidence focus of this strategy
  • Steelmanning — the constructive counterpart: building the strongest version of an opposing argument
  • Fact-Checking — addresses factual accuracy; Bolster Credibility addresses argumentative fairness

Examples¶

  • Journalism training: students evaluate a broadcast interview by asking whether the guest was presented with genuine opposing views and challenged to answer, then compare outlets such as NPR's interview format with advocacy programming.
  • Composition courses: a peer-review protocol asks "who did the writer engage with who disagrees?" before any feedback on style, making counterargument treatment the first revision priority.
  • Media literacy units: learners compare coverage of the same policy debate across outlets, rating each on whether critics are quoted, characterized accurately, and allowed to respond.
