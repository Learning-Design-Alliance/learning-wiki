# EQ Feeling Chart Check-In

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students use a provided EQ Feeling Chart — a visual grid of emotion words and faces organized by valence and intensity — to identify and communicate their current emotions. In pairs, they discuss what they are feeling, where those feelings are coming from, and any additional emotions they may be experiencing. The class then reconvenes to share what they noticed during their discussions, without requiring anyone to disclose beyond their comfort level.

## Design Implications

The strategy operationalizes **affect labeling**: putting feelings into words reduces amygdala activation and dampens the intensity of negative emotion, supporting self-regulation before academic work begins [Lieberman et al. (2007) found affect labeling reduced amygdala response.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) [+M]. The chart functions as an external scaffold for emotional vocabulary, which is precisely what novice emotion-identifiers lack; structured emotion-vocabulary instruction improves students' ability to recognize and regulate emotions [RULER's emotion-word instruction improved classroom climate and emotional skills.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2019.1614447) [+M]. The paired discussion adds a social-recognition component that normalizes emotional talk and builds relational trust.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An EQ Feeling Chart (visual emotion vocabulary display) visible to all students
- 5–10 minutes for paired discussion plus a brief whole-class debrief
- A classroom climate of psychological safety; voluntary sharing norms established in advance
- Teacher modeling of the routine the first several times, including the teacher's own check-in

#### Constraints
- Students hesitant to disclose emotions will not benefit from the discussion component; forced sharing can increase social threat rather than reduce it [-M] — participation in the pair discussion must remain voluntary in content even if the check-in itself is routine
- One-off use produces little benefit; the labeling skill develops only through repeated, distributed practice [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- In groups with low trust or recent conflict, whole-class sharing can expose students to ridicule; the debrief should focus on "what did you notice" rather than "who felt what"
- Students with limited emotion vocabulary may point at faces without internalizing labels; the chart must be paired with explicit vocabulary teaching, not used as a substitute for it
- If the routine becomes perfunctory (same answer every day), the labeling practice loses its regulatory effect; teachers should vary prompts and push for granular labels [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Silent entry variant:** students mark their feeling on a posted chart or personal slip on arrival — lower social cost, suitable for adolescents
- **Mood-meter variant:** a two-axis (energy × pleasantness) grid such as Yale's RULER Mood Meter supports more differentiated labeling than a face chart
- **Writing variant:** students journal the label, source, and one coping strategy — adds a self-monitoring record
- **Escalation variant:** the same chart is reused during conflict moments as a de-escalation tool, not just at class start

### Target Learners
- All ages, adaptable from pre-K through college; younger learners need face-based charts, older learners benefit from granular vocabulary
- Students with weak interoceptive awareness or alexithymia-like presentation, for whom the external chart supplies labels they cannot generate [Affect labeling reduces distress even when the label is supplied externally.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) [+M]
- Students whose self-regulation interferes with academic persistence — regular check-ins build the monitoring habit [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Emotional literacy: building differentiated emotion vocabulary
- Self-awareness and self-regulation: recognizing and naming internal states before they drive behavior
- Relationship skills: practicing empathic listening and normalizing emotional conversation
- Classroom climate: establishing that emotions are discussable, which supports engagement and task value [+M]

### Instructions
1. Post the EQ Feeling Chart where all students can see it; briefly review the emotion words, adding new vocabulary as students request it.
2. Model the routine: the teacher locates their own current feeling on the chart, names its source, and names a secondary feeling.
3. Students individually identify their feeling on the chart (pointing, marking, or noting privately).
4. In pairs, students discuss: what am I feeling, where is it coming from, and what else am I feeling? (paired peer conversation within [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
5. Reconvene and ask pairs to share *observations* — patterns, surprises, similarities — rather than individual disclosures ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)).
6. Close by connecting to the day's work: "Given how we're arriving, what do we need from each other today?"

## Examples
- A pre-K classroom using a faces-based feeling chart at morning circle; children move a marker to their feeling and the teacher names it aloud, building vocabulary before children can read.
- A secondary advisory period using the [RULER Mood Meter](https://ycei.org/ruler) (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence) as a daily digital check-in, with aggregated class data displayed anonymously.
- A college social-emotional learning course opening each session with the EQ Feeling Chart check-in, then connecting students' reported states to course content on emotion regulation.
