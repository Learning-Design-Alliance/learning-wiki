# Exit Tickets

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 9 studies (6 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 1 associational), `q2`–`q4` · 4 of 9 report an effect size

## Description
An exit ticket is a brief prompt — typically one to three questions — completed by learners in the final minutes of a lesson and submitted before leaving. It serves two functions simultaneously: learners consolidate and self-assess their understanding, and the instructor gains rapid formative data about what the class did and did not learn, informing the next lesson's starting point.

## Design Implications

Exit tickets enact [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) by creating a low-stakes feedback loop between learner performance and instructional adjustment. Their formative value depends on the instructor actually responding: data collected but never acted on does not improve learning [Formative feedback loops improve achievement when they inform subsequent teaching.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]. Prompts that ask learners to explain reasoning rather than restate facts add retrieval and [Self-Explanation](../elements/articulation.md) benefits on top of the assessment function [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A prompt tightly aligned to the lesson's intended learning goal, not general "how did it go?" questions
- A fast response format (index card, sticky note, digital form) so collection takes under five minutes
- A committed instructor routine for reviewing responses before the next session and adjusting instruction accordingly
- A low-stakes or ungraded framing so responses reflect actual understanding rather than performance anxiety
- Prompts aligned to the lesson's actual learning goal, not generic "did you like this?" questions
- A fast response channel (index cards, sticky notes, digital forms such as Google Forms or Socrative) so the routine costs under five minutes
- Instructor review before the next session, with a visible plan for acting on what the tickets reveal

#### Constraints
- Only captures what learners can report at the moment of instruction; misconceptions that feel resolved may resurface later without spaced review [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [~M]
- End-of-class timing produces rushed, shallow responses if the lesson runs over — the ticket is the first thing sacrificed
- Self-report data is unreliable when learners lack the metacognitive skill to judge their own understanding; confidence and competence frequently diverge for novices
- Daily use without visible instructional response trains learners to treat tickets as compliance rituals, eroding honest self-assessment
- Tickets collected but never reviewed or acted upon yield no formative benefit and erode learner trust in the routine [-M]
- Yes/no or completion-style prompts measure perceived understanding, not actual understanding; learners frequently report confidence where they have none [-M]
- Daily grading of tickets converts a formative tool into summative pressure, suppressing honest "I don't get it" responses [~M]
- Very short prompts cannot assess extended reasoning or transfer; they sample only the lesson's most retrievable content [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **3-2-1 format**: three things learned, two questions, one connection — blends summary with metacognitive reflection
- **Muddiest point**: learners name the single most confusing element (Mosteller, 1989) — maximally efficient for diagnosing whole-class gaps
- **Predict-then-check**: learners answer a target question before instruction and revisit it at exit, making learning visible
- **Digital variants** (Google Forms, Poll Everywhere, Socrative) aggregate responses automatically for large classes
- **Entry tickets**: the same mechanism at lesson start, activating prior knowledge instead of consolidating it
- **Muddiest point** (Mosteller, 1989): learners name the single most confusing idea — fastest diagnostic variant
- **Confidence ratings** paired with answers: supports [self-monitoring](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M] by training learners to calibrate certainty against performance
- **Prediction tickets** (start-of-next-class variant): learners predict what a task will require, priming [activation](../principles/activation.md)
- **Digital variants** (Socrative, Formative, Poll Everywhere) aggregate responses instantly for whole-class display

### Target Learners
- All ages; the format scales from elementary classrooms to university lectures by adjusting prompt complexity
- Novices benefit from the forced retrieval and self-assessment moment, though their self-ratings need instructor correction [Formative feedback loops improve achievement when they inform subsequent teaching.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M]
- Reluctant writers benefit from short, structured formats (sticky notes, rating scales with one justification line)
- K–12 and higher-education learners in content-heavy courses where misconceptions compound across lessons
- Less useful for learners who already self-monitor accurately and for very young learners who cannot yet write fluently — oral or pictorial variants work better there [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Consolidation and retrieval of the day's core content
- Metacognitive monitoring: judging one's own understanding against the learning goal
- Surface data for the instructor: misconception diagnosis and pacing decisions
- Factual and conceptual recall: checking whether core ideas from the lesson were retained
- Misconception diagnosis: surfacing specific errors before they are practiced and entrenched
- Metacognitive calibration: aligning confidence with actual performance
- Not well suited to skill demonstration, extended argumentation, or transfer to novel contexts [~M]

### Instructions
1. Identify the single most important idea of the lesson and write a prompt that requires learners to apply or explain it — not merely recall it.
2. Reserve the final 3–5 minutes; announce the ticket at the start of class so learners monitor for it throughout ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
3. Distribute cards or open the digital form; keep responses anonymous or named depending on the diagnostic purpose.
4. Collect tickets at the door ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).
5. Sort responses into "got it / partial / missing" before the next session; open the next lesson by addressing the most common gap — this closing of the loop is where the learning benefit lives.
6. Periodically return feedback to learners on the class's collective responses so the routine stays meaningful.

## Examples
- **Muddiest Point (Harvard, Mosteller 1989)** — students in a large statistics lecture name the least clear point on a card; the instructor opens the next lecture by addressing the top two confusions.
- **3-2-1 in science classrooms** — after a lab on chemical reactions, students record three observations, two patterns, and one question; the teacher groups questions to launch the next investigation.
- **Socrative / Google Forms in university courses** — a two-question digital ticket auto-plots the class distribution, letting the instructor decide within minutes whether to reteach or advance.
- **Muddiest Point in college lectures** — Mosteller (1989) reported using a one-question end-of-class card in a Harvard statistics course, reviewing responses before the next session and opening class by addressing the confusion.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — end-of-unit mastery checks function as digital exit tickets, feeding dashboards that tell teachers which concepts to reteach.
- **Elementary math classrooms using daily quick-checks** — teachers sort sticky-note responses into piles on a board to decide whether to advance or reteach, a routine consistent with [Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md) practices.

## Description
An exit ticket is a brief written prompt — typically one to three questions — that learners complete in the final minutes of a lesson. Responses serve two functions simultaneously: they prompt learners to retrieve and consolidate what was just taught, and they generate rapid formative data the instructor uses to adjust the next lesson. Formats range from a single conceptual question ("Explain the difference between weathering and erosion in your own words") to confidence self-ratings to "muddiest point" prompts.

## Design Implications

Exit tickets enact [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md): they create frequent, low-stakes checkpoints that let instruction respond to evidence rather than assumption. Their formative value depends on the instructor actually using the data — systematic formative evaluation produces measurable gains, while collection without feedback or adjustment does not [Fuchs & Fuchs meta-analysis of systematic formative evaluation.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Because retrieval at the end of a lesson also functions as a spaced first review, well-designed tickets can modestly support retention [Spaced retrieval improves long-term retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+M].

### Instructions
1. **Set the goal.** Identify the one or two ideas the lesson must land; write the ticket against those, not against what was merely covered.
2. **Design 1–3 short prompts.** Prefer explain-in-your-words, apply-to-a-new-example, or muddiest-point formats over yes/no items. A [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) element can supply the self-assessment component.
3. **Reserve the final 3–5 minutes.** Make the routine predictable so it does not compete with lesson closure.
4. **Collect anonymously where honesty matters.** Anonymity increases disclosure of confusion; named tickets enable individual follow-up — choose deliberately.
5. **Sort responses before the next session** into "got it / partial / misconception" piles and plan the opening of the next lesson around the misconception pile, using [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) data to drive [direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) where needed.
6. **Close the loop.** Open the next class by naming what the tickets showed and how the lesson responds — this is what converts data collection into [formative assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md).
