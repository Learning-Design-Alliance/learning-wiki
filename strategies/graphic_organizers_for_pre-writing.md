# Graphic Organizers for Pre-Writing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 4 claims (4 for) · 10 studies (4 causal, 4 review, 2 quant-synthesis), `q2`–`q4` · 2 of 10 report an effect size

## Description
Students use a visual planning tool — a mind map, outline, story map, T-chart, or argument diagram — to generate and arrange ideas before drafting. Notes are brief phrases rather than full sentences, so working memory is spent on *what to say* and *in what order*, not on sentence construction. The organizer externalizes the planning stage of the writing process, making text structure visible before prose begins.

## Design Implications

Planning is the writing subprocess that most strongly distinguishes skilled from unskilled writers, and explicit planning instruction is among the best-supported writing interventions [A meta-analysis of writing instruction for adolescent students.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543074004487) [+S]. Graphic organizers reduce extraneous load during composition by holding the emerging plan outside working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M], and their spatial, relational format adds a second representation of the content [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Effects on text quality are strongest when the organizer matches the rhetorical structure of the target genre and when students are taught how to use it — an unexplained template is just an empty box.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An organizer whose structure maps onto the target genre (e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning boxes for argument; setting–problem–resolution for narrative)
- Brief teacher modeling of how to populate the organizer, ideally with a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) showing decisions about what belongs where
- A clear handoff into drafting: the organizer should function as the skeleton of the draft, not a separate artifact
- A writing task with enough complexity that planning adds value
- An organizer whose structure matches the rhetorical task (compare/contrast grid, argument frame, narrative story map) — a generic template invites empty boxes
- A clear path from organizer to draft: each box should map onto a paragraph or section
- Time limits on planning so most instructional time goes to drafting and revising

#### Constraints
- Ineffective when students fill in organizers mechanically without understanding the underlying structure; benefits depend on explicit instruction in their use [-M]
- Poorly matched organizers (a story map for a persuasive essay) can impose a misleading structure and degrade text quality [~M]
- Over-reliance can prevent students from internalizing planning routines; organizers should fade as planning skill develops [-W]
- For short or highly familiar writing tasks, the organizer adds steps without adding value [~W]
- Filling in a teacher-provided organizer is low-yield; learners learn from *generating* the structure and links, not from transcribing into boxes [Learner-generated organizers outperform author-provided organizers.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.4.808) [-S]
- Over-scaffolding can produce formulaic writing that follows the template rather than the ideas; fade the organizer as writers develop [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
- Organizers consume time that competes with drafting and revision; for fluent writers the planning aid may add little [Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.2.276) [~M]
- A mismatch between organizer structure and genre (e.g., a five-paragraph frame for an open narrative) constrains thinking rather than supporting it

#### Implementation Variability
- **Completed vs. partial vs. blank**: begin with a teacher-completed model, move to partially filled organizers, then blank templates ([Fading](../elements/fading.md))
- **Digital vs. paper**: tools like [Kidspiration/Inspiration](https://www.inspiration.com) or [Popplet](https://popplet.com) allow rearranging ideas after placement, which paper does not
- **Collaborative filling**: pairs co-construct an organizer before drafting individually, combining planning support with [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)
- **Genre-specific families**: SRSD instruction teaches students to select among organizers by genre rather than always using the same one
- **Learner-generated concept maps** for exploratory or expository tasks, where the writer discovers the structure [Nesbit & Adesope's concept-map meta-analysis.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543076003413) [+M]
- **Genre-specific frames** (story maps, argument/counterargument charts) for novice writers who need the rhetorical skeleton supplied
- **Oral pre-writing** (talking ideas into a partner or recorder before mapping) for learners whose ideas outrun their transcription speed
- **Digital organizers** (Kidspiration, Popplet, Google Jamboard) that allow easy rearrangement — a key advantage over paper for revising plans

### Target Learners
- Novice writers and students with weak planning skills, who otherwise begin drafting with no plan [Explicit planning instruction improves writing quality.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.94.4.687) [+S]
- Students with learning disabilities or ADHD, for whom external structure compensates for executive-function demands [+M]
- English learners, who benefit from the reduced language load of phrase-level planning [~M]
- Proficient writers may find mandatory organizers constraining; allow them to plan in their own format [~W]
- Novice and struggling writers, who benefit most from externalizing planning [Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.2.276) [+S]
- English learners, who can draft ideas in the organizer before tackling sentence-level language
- Less necessary for fluent writers with well-developed internal planning routines [Graham & Perin's meta-analysis of adolescent writing instruction.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.2.276) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Text structure knowledge: internalizing the organizational schema of a genre
- Idea generation and elaboration: producing and connecting content before drafting
- Self-regulated writing: building a planning routine students eventually apply independently
- Planning and organization: selecting, sequencing, and connecting ideas for a purpose and audience
- Genre knowledge: internalizing the structural conventions of narrative, argument, and expository forms
- Self-regulated writing: making planning an explicit, deliberate phase rather than skipping straight to drafting

### Instructions
1. [Activate prior knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) about the topic and the genre — what does finished writing in this form look like?
2. [Model](../elements/think-aloud.md) filling in the organizer for a sample prompt, verbalizing decisions ("I need two reasons here, and each needs evidence").
3. Provide students a genre-matched organizer template and have them generate brief notes — phrases, not sentences — possibly with [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of the task into sections.
4. Have students use the organizer as a drafting map, checking off sections as they write ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
5. Fade the organizer over successive tasks, moving toward independent planning ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

### Instructions
1. **Set the task and activate knowledge.** Briefly [activate prior knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) about the topic and the genre's purpose.
2. **Model the organizer.** Demonstrate filling in a partially completed organizer for a *different* topic, thinking aloud about why ideas go where they do.
3. **Students generate their own.** Learners brainstorm and map ideas, drawing connections themselves rather than copying prompts [Learner-generated organizers outperform author-provided organizers.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.4.808) [+S]. Support with [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) for the genre structure.
4. **Talk through the plan.** Partners or groups explain their maps, prompting [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) and exposing gaps before drafting.
5. **Draft from the organizer.** Each section of the map becomes a paragraph or section; the organizer stays visible as a checklist, not a script.
6. **Fade.** Across successive writing tasks, shift from full frames to blank paper with a prompt to "plan first," building independent planning routines [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]
