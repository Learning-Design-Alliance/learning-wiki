# Graphic Organizers for Pre-Writing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Students use a visual planning tool — a mind map, outline, story map, T-chart, or argument diagram — to generate and arrange ideas before drafting. Notes are brief phrases rather than full sentences, so working memory is spent on *what to say* and *in what order*, not on sentence construction. The organizer externalizes the planning stage of the writing process, making text structure visible before prose begins.

## Design Implications

Planning is the writing subprocess that most strongly distinguishes skilled from unskilled writers, and explicit planning instruction is among the best-supported writing interventions [A meta-analysis of writing instruction for adolescent students.](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543074004487) [+S]. Graphic organizers reduce extraneous load during composition by holding the emerging plan outside working memory [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M], and their spatial, relational format adds a second representation of the content [Dual coding improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M]. Effects on text quality are strongest when the organizer matches the rhetorical structure of the target genre and when students are taught how to use it — an unexplained template is just an empty box.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An organizer whose structure maps onto the target genre (e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning boxes for argument; setting–problem–resolution for narrative)
- Brief teacher modeling of how to populate the organizer, ideally with a [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) showing decisions about what belongs where
- A clear handoff into drafting: the organizer should function as the skeleton of the draft, not a separate artifact
- A writing task with enough complexity that planning adds value

#### Constraints
- Ineffective when students fill in organizers mechanically without understanding the underlying structure; benefits depend on explicit instruction in their use [-M]
- Poorly matched organizers (a story map for a persuasive essay) can impose a misleading structure and degrade text quality [~M]
- Over-reliance can prevent students from internalizing planning routines; organizers should fade as planning skill develops [-W]
- For short or highly familiar writing tasks, the organizer adds steps without adding value [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Completed vs. partial vs. blank**: begin with a teacher-completed model, move to partially filled organizers, then blank templates ([Fading](../elements/fading.md))
- **Digital vs. paper**: tools like [Kidspiration/Inspiration](https://www.inspiration.com) or [Popplet](https://popplet.com) allow rearranging ideas after placement, which paper does not
- **Collaborative filling**: pairs co-construct an organizer before drafting individually, combining planning support with [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)
- **Genre-specific families**: SRSD instruction teaches students to select among organizers by genre rather than always using the same one

### Target Learners
- Novice writers and students with weak planning skills, who otherwise begin drafting with no plan [Explicit planning instruction improves writing quality.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.94.4.687) [+S]
- Students with learning disabilities or ADHD, for whom external structure compensates for executive-function demands [+M]
- English learners, who benefit from the reduced language load of phrase-level planning [~M]
- Proficient writers may find mandatory organizers constraining; allow them to plan in their own format [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Text structure knowledge: internalizing the organizational schema of a genre
- Idea generation and elaboration: producing and connecting content before drafting
- Self-regulated writing: building a planning routine students eventually apply independently

### Instructions
1. [Activate prior knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) about the topic and the genre — what does finished writing in this form look like?
2. [Model](../elements/think-aloud.md) filling in the organizer for a sample prompt, verbalizing decisions ("I need two reasons here, and each needs evidence").
3. Provide students a genre-matched organizer template and have them generate brief notes — phrases, not sentences — possibly with [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of the task into sections.
4. Have students use the organizer as a drafting map, checking off sections as they write ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
5. Fade the organizer over successive tasks, moving toward independent planning ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Examples
- **SRSD (Self-Regulated Strategy Development)** — Graham & Harris's widely researched framework uses genre-specific planning sheets (e.g., POW+TREE for persuasive writing: Topic, Reasons, Explain, Ending) as a core planning tool. Overview: [https://srsd.uncg.edu](https://srsd.uncg.edu)
- **Inspiration / Kidspiration** — [https://www.inspiration.com](https://www.inspiration.com) — diagramming software for webs, concept maps, and outlines that converts directly to outline view for drafting
- **Read Write Think (NCTE/IRA)** — free interactive organizers such as the Persuasion Map and Essay Map: [https://www.readwritethink.org](https://www.readwritethink.org)
