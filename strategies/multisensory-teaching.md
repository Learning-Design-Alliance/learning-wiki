# Multisensory Teaching

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Multisensory teaching engages multiple sensory channels — typically visual, auditory, kinesthetic (movement), and tactile (touch) — in the same lesson, so that learners see, say, hear, and physically manipulate content rather than receiving it through one channel. The approach originated in structured literacy for dyslexia (Orton-Gillingham) and is now applied across subjects, from tracing sandpaper letters to manipulating physical models in science.

## Design Implications

Multisensory instruction is best understood through dual coding: pairing verbal and visual representations creates two retrieval routes and richer encoding than either channel alone [Pairing verbal and visual input improves recall and retention over single-channel input.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+S]. However, the benefit comes from *complementary* representations of the same content, not from adding arbitrary sensory variety — redundant or decorative input can split attention and add extraneous load [~M]. The strongest evidence base is for structured, sequential literacy programs (e.g., Orton-Gillingham–derived approaches) with struggling readers [+W]; broader claims that "teaching to each learner's preferred modality" improves outcomes are not supported [X].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Each modality must carry the *same* conceptual content, not competing information ([Dual Coding Theory](../theories/dual-coding-theory.md))
- Manipulatives, gestures, or movement must be integral to the concept, not decorative
- Explicit, systematic sequencing — multisensory delivery does not substitute for clear instructional structure ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md))

#### Constraints
- Adding a second channel with redundant or irrelevant material increases extraneous cognitive load rather than supporting encoding [~M] — see [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md)
- Tailoring instruction to diagnosed "learning styles" (visual vs. auditory learners) does not improve outcomes; matching hypotheses have repeatedly failed experimental tests [X]
- Benefits shrink or reverse for learners with high prior knowledge, for whom concrete manipulatives can be redundant [~M] — consistent with the [Expertise Reversal Effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md)
- Evidence for generalization beyond early literacy and specific domains (e.g., math facts, letter formation) is thin [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Structured literacy:** simultaneous visual–auditory–kinesthetic–tactile (VAKT) letter-sound work, as in Orton-Gillingham and its derivatives
- **Embodied cognition variants:** gesture and physical action tied to meaning ([Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md)), e.g., acting out story events or physical simulations of science phenomena
- **Manipulative-based:** concrete objects ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) of physical material), transitioning to pictorial and abstract representations (concrete–representational–abstract sequence in math)
- **Multimedia variants:** narration plus animation rather than narration plus on-screen text, per multimedia learning principles

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers and students with dyslexia or word-recognition difficulties, where VAKT letter-sound work is a core component of structured literacy interventions [+W]
- Novices learning symbolic systems (letters, numerals, notation) who benefit from dual verbal–visual encoding [Pairing verbal and visual input improves recall and retention over single-channel input.](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+S]
- Young learners for whom concrete manipulation supports abstraction before symbolic fluency develops
- Less beneficial for advanced learners, who can often skip the concrete/multisensory stage [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Foundational symbol–sound and symbol–meaning mappings (phonics, numeracy)
- Vocabulary and concept acquisition through dual verbal–visual encoding
- Procedural fluency in early math via concrete–representational–abstract progression

### Instructions
1. Identify the core concept and select a visual and a physical/kinesthetic representation that each express the *same* structure — not merely an attention-grabbing add-on.
2. Present the concept with simultaneous channels: learners see the symbol, hear the sound or explanation, and trace, build, or gesture the content ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) and tracing are low-cost kinesthetic options).
3. Have learners produce the multisensory response themselves (say it while writing it, build it while explaining it) rather than only observing.
4. Fade the concrete and kinesthetic supports as fluency develops, moving toward symbolic-only work ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)) to avoid over-reliance on manipulatives.
5. Assess through the target modality of eventual use (e.g., reading text, not tracing letters) to confirm transfer.

## Examples
- **Orton-Gillingham approach** (and derivatives like [Wilson Reading System](https://www.wilsonlanguage.com) and [SPIRE](https://www.schoolspecialty.com/spire)) — learners see a letter, say its sound, and trace it in sand or on a textured surface simultaneously; the approach underpins most structured literacy interventions for dyslexia.
- **Montessori sandpaper letters and movable alphabets** (https://amiusa.org) — tactile letter forms paired with sound and name, a classic VAKT design.
- **Concrete–representational–abstract instruction in mathematics** — students solve problems with physical manipulatives, then drawings, then symbols, with each stage representing the same problem structure.
