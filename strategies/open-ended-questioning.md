# Open Ended Questioning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Open ended questioning poses questions that cannot be answered with a single word or a memorized fact — questions such as "Why might this solution fail?" or "What would happen if…?" — and treats learner responses as objects for further inquiry. The instructor withholds immediate evaluation, uses follow-up probes, and allows sufficient [wait time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wait_time) so learners construct extended, reasoned answers.

## Design Implications

Open questions push learners beyond retrieval into elaboration, explanation, and justification, which strengthens understanding by forcing connections to prior knowledge [Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Their effectiveness depends on the instructor's follow-through: questions that are genuinely open but then funneled to one "right" answer teach learners that the openness was rhetorical. Extended wait time (3+ seconds) after both question and answer increases response length and quality of reasoning [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions anchored to a shared object of thought (a text, dataset, case, or artifact) so responses are grounded, not speculative
- Genuine uncertainty on the instructor's part about the answer, or at least willingness to accept multiple defensible answers
- Norms and structures for responding — [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) prompts, pair-share, or written pre-drafting — so all learners formulate answers, not only volunteers
- Follow-up probes ("What's your evidence?", "How does that compare with…?") that press for reasoning rather than closure

#### Constraints
- With novices, unguided open questioning can produce unproductive search and floundering; some prior knowledge or structure is needed before questions pay off [~M]
- High wait time is difficult to sustain in large classes or fast-paced curricula; silence is often cut short, collapsing the question into a closed one [-M]
- Vague or overly broad questions ("What do you think about X?") elicit opinion rather than reasoning and add little learning value [-W]
- Cultural and affective factors matter: learners low in [belonging](../elements/belonging.md) or unfamiliar with discussion norms may experience public open questioning as risk rather than invitation [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Socratic questioning** — a scripted sequence of probes that systematically challenges assumptions, evidence, and implications
- **Question stems / reciprocal teaching** — learners generate the open questions themselves, shifting the cognitive demand to question-asking
- **Written or digital variants** — backchannels, polls with free-text, or discussion boards lower the social risk of public response
- **Funneling vs. focusing** — focusing patterns keep the learner doing the cognitive work; funneling toward the teacher's answer reduces the benefit

### Target Learners
- Learners with some prior knowledge of the topic, who can use open questions to reorganize and elaborate it [~M]
- Reluctant contributors benefit from written or paired variants before whole-group questioning [~W]
- Less effective as a first exposure for complete novices, who lack the knowledge base to generate substantive answers [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and [cognitive disequilibrium](../theories/constructivism.md) that motivates conceptual change [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]
- Reasoning, justification, and [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) skills [Argumentation improves reasoning.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Formative insight for the teacher into learner thinking [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. Anchor the question to a concrete artifact or case (see [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)) so all learners share an object of reasoning.
2. Pose the question, then wait at least 3–5 seconds; resist filling silence.
3. Collect responses via pair-share or quick writing so every learner drafts an answer before public discussion.
4. Probe with follow-ups that demand evidence, comparison, or implication rather than evaluating the first answer as right or wrong.
5. Close by making the reasoning public — have learners compare positions or summarize the class's emerging answer, connecting to [Discussion-Based Learning](../patterns/discussion-based-learning.md).

## Examples
- **Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown)** — students take turns generating open questions about a text segment, alongside summarizing and predicting; see [Reciprocal Teaching](../elements/reciprocal-teaching.md).
- **[Ambitious Science Teaching](https://ambitiousscienceteaching.org)** — "eliciting ideas" routines use open questioning to surface students' initial models before instruction.
- **[Socratic seminars](https://www.paideia.org)** in Paideia schools — open questions about a shared text drive student-led discussion.
