Safe and Healthy Elementary School Recess¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 4 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 review), q3 · 1 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Safe and healthy recess treats recess as an instructional setting rather than unstructured downtime. Recess is planned like a lesson — with game structures, role assignments, and trained adult facilitation — so that play deliberately builds cooperation, conflict resolution, and physical activity. Programs such as Playworks TeamUp organize recess around inclusive games, junior coach roles, and explicit conflict-resolution routines (e.g., rock-paper-scissors as a dispute protocol), aiming to reduce bullying and disciplinary referrals while increasing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity.

Design Implications¶

Structured recess leverages the fact that social skills and self-regulation are learned through guided practice in authentic peer settings, not through instruction alone. Physical activity during the school day also supports attention and on-task behavior in subsequent lessons Classroom physical activity improves attention and on-task behavior. [+M]. Adult facilitation matters: simply adding structure without coaching and modeling tends to produce compliance rather than transferable social skills.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A recess plan developed like a lesson plan: game rotations, equipment inventory, and space zoning
  • Trained recess staff (coaches, paraprofessionals, or teachers) who can model game rules and conflict-resolution language
  • Student leadership roles (e.g., junior coaches) that distribute ownership of game facilitation
  • A consistent, low-friction conflict-resolution protocol all students know (e.g., rock-paper-scissors for disputed calls)
  • School leadership commitment to protecting recess time and sustaining staffing

Constraints¶

  • Over-structuring recess can eliminate the autonomy and free play that make recess restorative; benefits depend on balancing organized games with student choice [~M]
  • Effects fade without sustained staffing and coaching; one-off training or short-term programs show weaker impacts on climate and behavior [~M]
  • Requires space, equipment, and supervision ratios that under-resourced schools may struggle to maintain
  • Gains in recess behavior do not automatically transfer to classroom behavior without explicit connection (shared language, check-ins, debriefs)

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full-service models (Playworks TeamUp: full-time coach embedded in the school) versus training-only models (existing staff trained in recess facilitation)
  • Peer-led variants where older students run games as junior coaches, deepening their own leadership and coaching skills
  • Climate-integrated variants that connect recess routines to classroom norms, morning meetings, and whole-class sharing debriefs

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary students (K–5/6), with the strongest documented effects in high-poverty urban schools where unstructured recess frequently produces conflict
  • Students who struggle with unstructured social settings benefit from explicit game structures and assigned roles [~M]
  • Older elementary students gain additional value from leadership roles that build responsibility and belonging Belonging interventions improve student outcomes. [+M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Social-emotional: cooperation, inclusion, conflict resolution, self-regulation
  • Behavioral climate: reduced bullying and disciplinary referrals
  • Physical health: increased moderate-to-vigorous physical activity
  • Leadership: facilitation and peer-coaching skills for student coaches

Instructions¶

  1. Audit current recess: observe conflict hotspots, inactive students, and exclusion patterns; inventory equipment and space.
  2. Co-develop a recess plan with staff and students — game rotations, zone maps, and role assignments (assigned positions such as junior coaches and game leaders).
  3. Model each core game and the conflict-resolution protocol explicitly before launching, using act-it-out demonstrations of common disputes and their resolution.
  4. Facilitate actively: coaches coach in the moment — prompting inclusion, reinforcing positive interactions, and redirecting conflict to the shared protocol (application of the routine in authentic situations).
  5. Structure peer interaction deliberately through small-sided, inclusive games that require cooperation (peer interaction).
  6. Debrief: use brief whole-class sharing or morning-meeting check-ins to name what worked, resolve lingering disputes, and connect recess norms to classroom expectations.
  7. Monitor and iterate: track activity levels, incident reports, and student feedback; adjust game offerings and roles.

Related Strategies¶

  • Active Recess — the overlapping strategy of raising physical activity levels during recess; safe-and-healthy recess adds the social-climate and conflict-resolution layer
  • Cooperative Learning — the same group-reward and individual-accountability logic applied to playground games rather than academic tasks
  • Social-Emotional Learning Integration — recess as a practice field for SEL competencies taught in the classroom
  • Guide students in organized activities during recess and improve the recess play yard to raise physical activity, especially for girls

Related Elements¶

  • Modeling — coaches demonstrate games and dispute resolution before students run them independently
  • Coaching — in-the-moment facilitation during play, the primary adult role at structured recess
  • Peer Interaction — the core mechanism through which social skills are practiced
  • Check-In — debrief structures that consolidate recess learning into classroom climate

Tools¶

  • Playworks TeamUp — full-service recess program with embedded coaches, game libraries, and junior coach leadership program
  • CDC recess framework — Recess in Schools: A Practice Guide (CDC & SHAPE America, 2017) for planning and policy guidance
  • Peaceful Playgrounds — low-cost painted-game and rule-poster alternative for schools without dedicated coaches

Examples¶

  • Playworks TeamUp — an independent evaluation across multiple urban districts found improvements in recess organization, student cooperation and conflict resolution, and reductions in bullying and disciplinary referrals relative to comparison schools [~M].
  • Junior Coach programs — upper-elementary students lead games for younger students, applying social learning theory: younger children imitate peer models, while junior coaches deepen their own leadership through teaching.
