Have participants write, draw, and physically enact their own imagined situations to deepen imagery¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 mixed) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 review), q2 · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

In the recommendations section, the author suggests three refinements to scripted imagery activities: having participants write their own imagery situations, draw what they imagine, and use gestures and body language while imagining. The author reports that participants "who did this in a previous study found their own situations more motivating than the ones that I had written since they could identify with them more closely."

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Teacher feedback or correction of participant-written imagery situations, which the author notes can also improve writing

Constraints¶

  • Recommendations draw on the author's prior studies (Magid, 2011; Magid & Chan, 2012) and participant suggestions rather than on this study's own comparisons

Target Learners¶

  • Elementary and secondary language learners

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Enhancing the vividness and motivational force of learners' vision of their Ideal L2 self

Claims¶

  • A scripted-imagery and goal-setting intervention increased motivation, confidence, and positive attitudes toward English in 90% of the experimental group versus 50%, 75%, and 90% respectively in the control group [+W]
  • Imagining the Ideal L2 self evoked pride and other positive emotions that participants reported as motivating their study of English [+W]
  • Imagination training improved the ability to use imagination in 63% of participants in both groups [+W]
  • Imagining worked examples benefits learners with adequate prior knowledge but harms performance for low-knowledge learners [~W]
