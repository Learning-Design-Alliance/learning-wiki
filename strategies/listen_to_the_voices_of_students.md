# Listen to the Voices of Students

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Providing students authorship and allowing them to share their stories empowers them, fostering agency and leadership. This approach disrupts traditional classroom power dynamics by treating student perspectives as legitimate instructional content — students shape discussion topics, project directions, and classroom norms rather than merely responding to teacher-initiated prompts. It is carried out through structured sharing formats, student-led discussion, and authentic authorship opportunities in which learners' lived experiences become part of the curriculum.

## Design Implications

Elevating student voice builds on self-determination research: experiences of autonomy and relatedness support intrinsic motivation, and voice-giving practices are a direct way to supply both [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Voice practices also raise task value — students engage more deeply when they see their own identities and experiences reflected in the work [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]. For students of color and other learners whose stories are often pre-written by dominant narratives, authorship over their own accounts is an equity practice, not merely an engagement tactic.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A classroom environment that is psychologically safe and inclusive; students must trust that their contributions will be treated with respect before they will speak authentically
- A deliberate shift from teacher-centered to student-centered facilitation — the teacher's role moves from evaluator to listener and co-constructor ([Active Listening](../strategies/active_listening.md))
- Structured formats that distribute talk, so voice is not captured by a confident few ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [Peer Interaction](../elements/peer-interaction.md))
- Authentic audiences and purposes for student stories, so sharing has consequence beyond the grade ([Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md))

#### Constraints
- Requires additional time to facilitate student-led discussion and activities; coverage-driven pacing crowds it out
- Can be challenging to manage if students are not accustomed to taking initiative — voice practices introduced into a compliance-oriented classroom culture often produce superficial participation rather than genuine agency [~M]
- Without intentional facilitation, "student voice" can become tokenism: students are consulted but decisions remain unchanged, which can erode trust rather than build it
- Students with strong communication apprehension may experience forced public sharing as threatening; multiple modes of contribution (writing, small group, digital) mitigate this

#### Implementation Variability
- **Consultation level** ranges from students advising on existing plans, to co-designing curriculum, to full student-led inquiry — deeper levels yield stronger agency effects but demand more scaffolding
- **Story-based variants** emphasize personal narrative and cultural identity (e.g., students designing projects reflecting their backgrounds); **governance variants** emphasize shared decision-making about classroom norms and activities
- Digital tools (discussion boards, podcasts, video) extend voice to students less comfortable speaking in whole-class settings

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners broadly, with particular benefit for students of color and other students whose perspectives are marginalized in traditional settings, for whom authorship counters pre-written narratives [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Disengaged or low-efficacy students, for whom visible ownership of learning can rebuild a sense of competence and belonging [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Less effective as a standalone approach for novices learning highly structured procedural content, where explicit guidance remains primary

### Target Learning Goals
- Agency and leadership: students taking ownership of their learning and classroom community
- Communication and self-advocacy: articulating one's perspective and listening to others' ([Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md))
- Equity and empathy: valuing diverse perspectives and disrupting traditional power dynamics ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Metacognition and self-regulation: reflecting on one's own learning experiences [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Establish safety and norms for respectful sharing before asking for authentic voice ([Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md))
2. Open structured sharing opportunities where students tell their own stories and pose their own questions ([Peer Interaction](../elements/peer-interaction.md), [Whole-Class Sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md))
3. Model receptive listening — paraphrase, ask follow-up questions, and visibly act on what students say ([Active Listening](../strategies/active_listening.md))
4. Hand over authorship: students design projects, select topics, and lead discussions ([Academic Choice (Planning, Working, Reflecting)](../strategies/academic_choice_(planning,_working,_reflecting).md))
5. Close the loop by reflecting with students on how their input shaped the class, making agency visible

## Examples
- **Youth participatory action research** (e.g., the [Public Science Project](https://www.publicscienceproject.org) at CUNY Graduate Center) — students design and conduct research on problems in their own communities and present findings to authentic audiences.
- **Student-led conferences** — replacing teacher-led parent conferences with students presenting their own learning narratives and goals.
- **Story-centered projects** — learners designing projects that reflect their cultural backgrounds and experiences, then leading class discussion on topics of their choosing.
