# Providing Multiple Means of Representation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Providing multiple means of representation is the first principle of Universal Design for Learning (UDL): content is offered in more than one format — text, audio, video, diagrams, worked models — so that perceptual, linguistic, and prior-knowledge differences do not become barriers to access. It goes beyond redundancy: each representation should be pedagogically functional, not merely a duplicate, and should include options that adjust vocabulary, syntax, and background demands.

## Design Implications

Multiple representations work because they distribute information across verbal and visual channels and reduce the chance that a single format's demands (decoding, listening comprehension, unfamiliar vocabulary) block access to the content itself [Presenting material across visual and verbal channels improves learning over single-channel presentation.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+S]. But added representations are not free: each channel consumes working memory, so formats must be integrated and coordinated rather than scattered [Cognitive overload degrades learning when total demands exceed working memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. Irrelevant or decorative media actively harms learning [Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning.](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- At least two genuinely functional representations of core content (e.g., text plus annotated diagram, not text plus decorative image)
- Accessible formats: captions, alt text, screen-reader-compatible structure, transcripts
- Vocabulary support — glossaries, definitions, plain-language alternatives ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- Activation of relevant background knowledge, since a representation only helps if learners can connect it to what they know ([Activation](../principles/activation.md))

#### Constraints
- Redundant on-screen text read aloud verbatim impairs learning by overloading the visual and auditory channels simultaneously [-S] — narration plus identical text is worse than narration alone for fluent readers
- Adding formats without integration (e.g., a diagram far from its explaining text) forces costly visual search and splits attention [-S]
- Multiple representations increase production and maintenance cost; applying them to low-stakes or easily-accessed content wastes effort better spent elsewhere [~M]
- For learners with strong prior knowledge, extra scaffolding representations can slow processing [Guidance that helps novices can hinder experts (expertise reversal).](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full UDL implementation:** all materials designed from the start with format options ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) built in rather than retrofitted)
- **On-demand alternatives:** a primary representation with optional supports — glossaries, translations, [Audiobooks](../elements/audiobooks.md), [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)
- **Learner-selected format:** [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md) letting students pick text, audio, or video versions of the same content
- **Segmenting and chunking:** breaking dense media into learner-paced segments ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md))

### Target Learners
- Learners with sensory, language, or processing differences who are blocked by a single default format [+M]
- Novices, who benefit from concrete visual models paired with verbal explanation [Presenting material across visual and verbal channels improves learning over single-channel presentation.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-learning.md) [+S]
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from vocabulary scaffolds and visual support alongside text
- Less beneficial to over-scaffold experts, who may find redundant formats inefficient [Guidance that helps novices can hinder experts (expertise reversal).](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Foundational knowledge acquisition: making content accessible regardless of entry format
- Conceptual understanding: multiple representations support building flexible mental models [Analogical and multi-case comparison improves transfer.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Transfer: seeing the same idea in multiple forms supports recognizing it in novel contexts [Analogical reasoning improves transfer.](../claims/analogical-reasoning-improves-transfer.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Identify the core content and the barriers embedded in the default representation (reading level, sensory modality, assumed background).
2. Design a second, functionally different representation — e.g., an annotated diagram, a narrated model, or an [Analogy](../elements/analogies.md) — and integrate it spatially and temporally with the first.
3. Add vocabulary and background supports: glossaries, plain-language summaries, [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), or [Activation](../principles/activation.md) prompts.
4. Ensure accessibility: captions, transcripts, alt text, logical heading structure.
5. Manage load: segment media, apply [Chunking](../principles/chunking.md), and cut decorative media [Coherence principle: irrelevant material hurts learning.](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [+S].
6. Offer format choice where feasible ([Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md)) and check that each option leads to the same learning goal.

- **[Newsela](https://newsela.com)** — publishes the same article at multiple reading levels, varying linguistic representation while holding content constant.
- **Textbook with integrated figures** — a well-designed anatomy text pairs every verbal description with a labeled diagram on the same spread, enacting integrated dual-channel presentation.
