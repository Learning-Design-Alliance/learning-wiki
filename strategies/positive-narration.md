# Positive Narration

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 mixed) · 7 studies (3 review, 2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis), `q3`–`q4` · 1 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Positive narration is a classroom management technique in which the teacher describes appropriate student behavior aloud as it happens ("I see tables 2 and 3 already have their materials out"), rather than calling out or correcting misbehavior. It works by making behavioral expectations salient through live models, redirecting collective attention toward what students *should* be doing. It is a core component of techniques such as Teach Like a Champion's "Positive Framing" and Responsive Classroom's teacher language.

## Design Implications

Positive narration leverages social learning: peers who hear a described behavior are more likely to imitate it than to imitate the misbehavior that would have drawn attention [Modeling increases the likelihood of observed behaviors being reproduced.](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) [+M]. It also functions as differential reinforcement — attending to desired behavior while withholding attention from undesired behavior — which is more effective than punishment-oriented correction for maintaining on-task conduct [Reinforcing desired behavior is more effective than punishing undesired behavior for establishing classroom conduct.](../theories/behaviorism.md) [+M]. Narration must be genuinely descriptive and behavior-specific; vague praise ("good job, everyone") does not produce the same effect [Feedback that specifies the behavior or strategy outperforms generic praise.](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Clear, previously taught behavioral expectations that narration can reference
- Descriptive, non-evaluative language tied to observable actions
- Genuine behaviors to narrate — narration of nonexistent behavior erodes credibility
- Consistency: intermittent narration sharply reduces its signal value
- Clearly established expectations students can recognize when narrated
- Specific, behavior-level language ("Marcus started on problem 1") rather than evaluative praise
- Genuine, accurate observation — narrating behavior that is not happening erodes credibility
- Broad coverage: narrate different students and behaviors so narration does not become favoritism

#### Constraints
- Overuse or singling out individual students can feel manipulative or performative, undermining [autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md) and intrinsic motivation [-M] — controlling teacher language reduces self-determined motivation
- Ineffective when expectations were never explicitly taught; narration reminds but does not establish norms
- Can escalate rather than reduce disruption when students perceive narration as attention-seeking bait or sarcasm [~W]
- Less effective with adolescents, who are more sensitive to public evaluation and may resist overt behavioral framing [-W]
- Overuse produces habituation; students stop attending to narrated praise [Praise loses effectiveness when delivered indiscriminately or too frequently.](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [~M]
- Narration perceived as manipulative or sarcastic by older students can backfire and damage teacher credibility [-M]
- If expectations are unclear, narration of "good behavior" is ambiguous and does not guide behavior [~M]
- Rewards-and-recognition framing (public praise as a de facto reward) can undermine intrinsic motivation for the task itself [Rewards perceived as controlling undermine intrinsic motivation.](../claims/rewards-undermine-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-group narration** ("Most people have started the do-now") versus **anonymous group framing** that avoids naming individuals
- **Proactive narration** at transition points versus **reactive narration** when off-task behavior begins to spread
- **Silent alternatives** — proximity and nonverbal cues — for contexts where narration itself is disruptive
- **Student self-narration**, where learners describe their own process, bridging toward [self-regulated learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md)
- **Narrate-to-the-room**: describe behavior without names ("I see pencils moving on the do-now") — lower risk of singling out
- **Narrate-to-nearby**: quietly narrate near off-task students, using proximity plus description as a redirect
- **Narrate-then-prompt**: pair narration with a next-step cue ("Tables 1 and 2 are ready; everyone needs materials out by the timer")
- **Nonverbal narration**: pointing to posted expectations or student work instead of speaking, reducing interruption

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-school students, who respond strongly to public behavioral modeling [+M]
- Classes during transitions, independent work, and group work — the highest-risk moments for off-task contagion
- Less appropriate for adult learners, where the same function is better served by clear norms and [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md)
- Most effective in elementary and early middle grades, where public teacher attention carries strong social weight [+M]
- Useful for students with attention or self-regulation difficulties, who benefit from frequent, concrete restatements of expectations [~M]
- Less effective with adolescents, who may resist public teacher attention; private or nonverbal variants preferred [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Classroom conduct and procedural routines: establishing and maintaining behavioral expectations
- Time-on-task: protecting instructional time during transitions
- Classroom climate: signaling that effort and cooperation are noticed and valued
- Procedural and behavioral routines: transitions, materials management, group-work norms
- Self-regulation: making expected strategies visible so students can model them
- Classroom climate: building a shared, positively framed sense of "what we do here"

### Instructions
1. Teach and rehearse the expected behavior explicitly before narrating it ([Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) for routines).
2. Scan the room continuously; identify students performing the desired behavior.
3. Narrate descriptively and promptly: name the behavior, not the student, where possible ("I see notebooks open with today's date").
4. Withhold attention from minor misbehavior; address it privately if it persists rather than narrating the negative.
5. Fade narration as routines become automatic, shifting responsibility to students ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Examples
- **Teach Like a Champion (Lemov)** — the "Positive Framing" and "100%" techniques train teachers to narrate what students are doing right ("Your eyes should be on your copy of the text — I see almost all of them are") rather than what they are doing wrong. https://teachlikeachampion.com
- **Responsive Classroom** — "Reinforcing language" teacher scripts describe student actions specifically to consolidate routines. https://www.responsiveclassroom.org
- **KIPP charter network** — narration during transitions ("Scholars in row 2 are already in silent sync") as a standard whole-school culture practice.
- **PBIS (Positive Behavioral Interventions & Supports)** — [pbis.org](https://www.pbis.org): school-wide frameworks train teachers in specific behavior-specific praise and narration of expectations; a large RCT of PBIS showed reduced office discipline referrals [Bradshaw, C. - **Teach Like a Champion (Lemov, 2015)** — the "Positive Framing" and "Narrate the Positive" techniques codify narrate-to-the-room narration in scripted teacher practice
- **Responsive Classroom** — [responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org): teacher language curriculum distinguishes reinforcing language (narration) from reminding and redirecting language

## Description
Positive narration is a classroom management strategy in which the teacher verbally describes students' desired behavior as it happens ("I see tables 2 and 3 have already started their lab write-ups"), rather than calling out off-task behavior. It works through social learning and public reinforcement: naming compliant behavior makes expectations concrete and salient for every learner who hears it, without singling out or shaming anyone.

Vague praise ("good job, everyone") loses its informational value quickly [Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1080/00405840709536524) [+S].

4. Rotate narration across students, behaviors, and locations so all learners are periodically represented.
5. Follow narration with the next instructional move; do not pause and wait for compliance, which converts narration into a veiled threat.
6. Address persistent off-task behavior privately or via [Proximity](../elements/proximity.md) rather than escalating to public correction.
