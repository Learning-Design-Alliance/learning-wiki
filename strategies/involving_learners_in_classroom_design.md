# Involving Learners in Classroom Design

## Description
Involving learners in classroom design means actively soliciting their input when creating or modifying the physical learning environment — seating arrangements, use of space, displays, and furniture. It is carried out through structured consultation (surveys, design charrettes, co-design workshops) followed by visible incorporation of learner decisions. The pedagogical value lies less in the resulting layout than in the experience of meaningful choice and ownership it provides.

## Design Implications

Learner involvement in environmental design is a form of autonomy support: providing meaningful choice within structure, which is a core condition for intrinsic motivation [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Choice effects on motivation and effort are well documented, though the choices must be substantive and consequential rather than trivial [Choice improves intrinsic motivation and perceived competence.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.270) [+M]. Physical classroom factors (light, air quality, ownership and flexibility of space) account for a measurable portion of variance in learning outcomes, with "ownership" and "flexibility" features among the significant factors [Barrett et al. classroom design study.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2015.02.013) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine decision space: learners must have real authority over some aspect of the environment, not merely advisory voice
- Structured input mechanisms — surveys, [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), design workshops — rather than open-ended suggestion boxes
- Visible follow-through: incorporating (or explaining why not) learner suggestions, so the choice is consequential
- Willingness on the instructor's part to relinquish some control and trust learners with responsibility

#### Constraints
- Trivial or token choices (e.g., choosing between two nearly identical options) do not produce motivation benefits and can feel manipulative [Choice improves intrinsic motivation and perceived competence.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.270) [~M]
- Unbounded choice without structure can overwhelm learners and consume instructional time [-W]
- Diverse preferences can conflict; majority voting on design decisions can marginalize minority preferences and learners with sensory or accessibility needs [-W]
- Budget, safety regulations, and fixed architecture limit what learner input can actually change; unfulfillable suggestions erode trust [-W]
- Evidence for direct achievement gains from learner-designed environments is thin; most research concerns physical environment factors generally, not learner participation in design specifically [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Consultative:** instructor surveys preferences and designs accordingly (lowest cost, least ownership)
- **Co-design:** learners participate in design charrettes or redesign projects, treating the classroom itself as an authentic problem [Authentic audiences and purposes increase engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- **Ongoing negotiation:** rotating responsibility for zones of the room (reading corner, display walls) so design becomes a recurring practice rather than a one-off event
- **Digital extension:** learners co-design online course spaces, seating charts in flexible classrooms, or layout of collaborative work areas

### Target Learners
- K–12 learners, who benefit from visible ownership of shared space and the responsibility it confers [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- Learners with low [self-efficacy](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md), for whom visible authorship of the environment signals belonging and competence [+W]
- Adult and higher-education learners benefit more from co-design of course structures and schedules than of physical space, where they typically have less contact time [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Motivation and engagement: increasing task value through personal investment in the learning space [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]
- Self-regulation: practicing collective decision-making, negotiation, and responsibility for a shared resource
- Community building: establishing shared norms and belonging through collaborative design work

### Instructions
1. Identify the decision space: which aspects of the environment learners can genuinely control (seating, displays, furniture arrangement, noise zones) and which are fixed.
2. Gather structured input using [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md), surveys, or a design workshop; ask learners to justify proposals in terms of how the space supports learning, not just preference.
3. Facilitate [Collaborative Decision-Making](../elements/collaborative-decision-making.md) to converge on a design, using criteria (accessibility, sightlines, collaboration needs) as shared constraints.
4. Implement the design with learners where feasible, treating setup as an [Application](../elements/application.md) of their plan.
5. Review periodically: schedule [Check-Ins](../principles/check-ins.md) to evaluate whether the design is working and revise — making clear that the design is provisional and learner-governed.

## Examples
- A primary teacher runs a design charrette in which small groups propose layouts for the classroom library, present them against criteria (browsing access, comfort, noise separation), and vote; the winning group manages the space thereafter.
- A secondary school involves student panels in the redesign of a learning commons, a practice documented in participatory school-design research ([Woolner et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/03054980701457689)).
- A university instructor asks students to propose and test room configurations for seminar vs. group-work sessions, revising the default layout each term based on end-of-unit feedback.
