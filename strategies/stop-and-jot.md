# Stop and Jot

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Stop and Jot is a structured processing activity in which learners pause at designated points during a lesson and write brief responses — summaries, answers, predictions, or connections — to instructor-posed questions, often in a "stop box" on their notes. The pause gives learners time to consolidate and make sense of material before new content arrives, and gives the instructor a fast formative read on understanding. It functions simultaneously as engagement technique, note-taking scaffold, and comprehension check.

## Design Implications

Stop and Jot converts passive listening into intermittent [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md), which improves exam performance relative to lecture alone [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Its effectiveness depends on prompt quality: questions that require summarizing, connecting, or applying produce deeper processing than questions answerable by copying what was just said. The written record also creates an artifact instructors can scan to surface misconceptions and target feedback at the task or process level [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Prepared prompts tied to the key ideas of the segment, not generic ("any thoughts?")
- A visible, consistent location for jots (a stop box in notes, a slide cue, a shared document)
- Sufficient processing time — typically 1–3 minutes; rushing defeats the purpose
- A follow-up move: sharing, cold-calling on jots, or a quick instructor scan ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))

#### Constraints
- Less effective for learners who struggle with writing fluency; the transcription burden can crowd out thinking [-M] — offer drawing, oral, or dictation alternatives
- Prompts answerable by verbatim copying produce shallow processing and false fluency [~M]
- Overuse fragments a lesson and disrupts narrative flow; 2–4 well-placed stops per session outperform stopping every few minutes [~W]
- Without instructor follow-up, learners learn that jots are not read and engagement decays [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Jot-Pair-Share** — jots feed a brief pair discussion before whole-class sharing, adding [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)
- **Quick Jot** — a single 30-second response used as a mid-lesson comprehension pulse
- **Stop and Fill** — learners complete a partially structured frame or [Graphic Organizer](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) rather than free writing
- **Digital jots** — collaborative documents or tools (e.g., Google Docs, Padlet) allow real-time instructor scanning of all responses at once

### Target Learners
- Applicable across all levels and subject areas
- Particularly useful for learners who benefit from structured processing and note-taking support, including novices who cannot yet discriminate key ideas on their own [Graphic organizers support novice comprehension.](../claims/graphic-organizers-support-novice-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Learners with writing difficulties need alternative response modes or extended time [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Retention and comprehension of expository or lecture content
- Summarization and identification of key ideas
- Personal relevance: connecting new material to prior knowledge and experience
- Formative assessment data for the instructor

### Instructions
1. Identify 2–4 key transition points in the lesson where consolidation is needed.
2. Write a specific prompt for each stop that requires summarizing, predicting, or connecting — not copying.
3. Teach the routine: signal the stop, point to the stop box, state the prompt, and set the time.
4. Give adequate silent writing time; resist filling the silence.
5. Follow up: have learners share jots with a partner, select responses to discuss, or scan digital jots to decide whether to reteach ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) and [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md)).

## Examples
- **Elementary literacy blocks** — teachers place a stop box in guided-reading notes; students jot a prediction or inference at chapter breaks, then discuss with a partner (Jot-Pair-Share).
- **Large lecture courses** — instructor pauses twice per lecture with a slide prompt; students respond in a shared Google Doc the instructor scans live to decide whether to reteach.
- **Professional development** — facilitators use Quick Jots between segments so participants record one implication of each concept for their own context before moving on.
