"Document Outline" Tool¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Students use the "Document outline" tool in Google Docs to extract evidence supporting the main ideas they've found in a text. They write headings for paragraphs or groups of paragraphs and select "Heading 1" from the drop-down menu; sentences or phrases representing supporting evidence are summarized and labeled "Heading 2." The tool automatically populates a navigable outline that can be exported to the top of the document, giving students a ready-made scaffold for review and writing initiation.

Design Implications¶

The strategy combines structural text analysis with external organization: assigning headings forces students to discriminate main ideas from supporting detail, and the auto-generated outline makes that structure visible and reusable. Summarizing evidence into heading-level phrases is a form of generative processing that supports comprehension better than rereading or highlighting alone Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]. Because the outline is produced as a byproduct of the work rather than composed separately, it lowers the startup cost of writing from sources.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to Google Docs and the source text(s) in digital form
  • Explicit criteria for what counts as a main idea versus supporting evidence, so heading labels are meaningful rather than arbitrary
  • A follow-on task that uses the outline (e.g., drafting an argument or summary), otherwise the artifact is abandoned
  • A genre-appropriate outline template or exemplar (e.g., argument essay, lab report, design doc) so learners know what sections are expected
  • A low-friction tool or surface for drafting and rearranging the outline
  • An instructor or peer checkpoint that reviews the outline before drafting begins
  • Explicit instruction in what belongs at each level (section vs. point vs. evidence) — see Clear Structure Presentation

Constraints¶

  • Requires familiarity with Google Docs formatting; the mechanics of applying heading styles can consume working memory that should go to comprehension [~W]
  • The outline is only as good as the extracted evidence — students who misidentify main ideas produce confidently organized but inaccurate structures, and the tool's polish can mask weak analysis [-W]
  • Works only for texts with a discernible hierarchical structure; flat or highly narrative texts yield degenerate outlines [~W]
  • Time-consuming for long texts; benefits shrink if the outlining becomes mechanical copy-and-summarize without judgment [-W]
  • Outlining imposed as mandatory compliance without instruction in why produces formulaic, mechanical structures [-M] — learners fill slots without reasoning about the argument
  • Overly detailed outlines can consume the planning effort that should go into content; rigid templates suppress voice and authentic organization [-W]
  • For experienced writers, forced outlining can disrupt productive recursive drafting processes [~W]
  • Weak writers may produce outlines that merely list topics without logical relations; without feedback on the outline itself, the tool adds little [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Teacher provides partial headings (a "skeleton outline") that students complete — a fading approach for novices
  • Students outline the same text in pairs and compare outlines, surfacing disagreements about what the main ideas are
  • Outline used as a pre-writing scaffold for Argument Construction or as a comprehension check on its own
  • Adapted to other tools with outline or heading-navigation features (Word's Navigation Pane, Notion's outline) when Google Docs is unavailable
  • Instructor-provided skeleton — sections pre-named; learners fill in points (strongest for novices)
  • Learner-generated outline with exemplar comparison — learners draft their own structure, then compare against a model outline
  • Collaborative outlining — groups negotiate structure before dividing writing tasks; supports Collaborative Writing
  • Reverse outlining — learners extract an outline from an existing draft to diagnose structural problems, used at revision rather than planning stage

Target Learners¶

  • Middle school, high school, and college students reading expository or argumentative texts
  • Struggling writers who stall at the blank page; the exported outline provides an immediate starting structure [+W]
  • Novices benefit most from the imposed main-idea/evidence distinction; advanced students may find the two-level constraint limiting [~W]
  • Novice writers and students new to a genre, who lack an internalized schema for document structure Advance organizers improve learning of new material. [+M]
  • Less necessary for experienced writers with automatized genre knowledge [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reading comprehension: identifying text structure and main ideas
  • Evidence selection: distinguishing claims from support
  • Organization for writing: converting reading notes into a drafting scaffold
  • Chunking practice: grouping paragraphs under headings reduces the volume students must hold in mind when writing Chunking reduces working memory load. [+S]
  • Writing quality: coherent organization and logical flow of extended text
  • Genre knowledge: understanding the conventional structure of reports, arguments, and explanations
  • Self-regulated learning: planning and monitoring as explicit, teachable phases of composition

Instructions¶

  1. Assign the source text (Assigned Readings) and model the heading-labeling decision once on a shared document.
  2. Students read and, for each paragraph or paragraph group, write a heading naming the main idea and apply "Heading 1."
  3. Students summarize supporting evidence into short phrases, apply "Heading 2," and place them under the relevant main-idea heading — a digital form of Annotating.
  4. Students open the auto-generated outline, review it for gaps or mislabeled ideas, and revise headings — a self-explanation pass over their own structure Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M].
  5. Students use the exported outline to begin drafting (Application of Knowledge), turning each Heading 1 into a section and each Heading 2 into a point with evidence.

Related Strategies¶

  • 5-Minute Writing Conferences — a natural checkpoint for reviewing outlines with individual learners

Related Elements¶

  • Annotating — the heading labels are structured annotations; both make thinking about text visible
  • Advance Organizers — the finished outline functions as an organizer for the writing that follows
  • Argument Construction — the claim/evidence structure of the outline maps directly onto argument structure
  • Application of Knowledge — drafting from the outline is the application step that prevents shallow encoding

Tools¶

  • Google Docs "Document outline" — automatically builds a navigable outline from applied heading styles
  • Microsoft Word Navigation Pane — equivalent heading-based outline feature
  • Notion / Obsidian — outline panes for students working in knowledge-base tools

Examples¶

  • A middle school science class outlines a news article on climate change: Heading 1 labels each claim ("Glaciers are retreating"), Heading 2 labels the evidence beneath it ("Satellite mass measurements since 2002"), then students draft an evidence-based paragraph from the exported outline.
  • A college writing course uses paired outlining: two students outline the same journal article independently, then compare Heading 1 labels to negotiate what the author's main argument actually is.
  • Google Docs outline view / Notion outliner — learners build a collapsible heading hierarchy before drafting; headings double as a navigation aid during revision (docs.google.com).
  • Purdue OWL essay outline guides (owl.purdue.edu) — published templates for argumentative and expository outlines used as instructor-provided skeletons.
  • Workflowy — drag-and-drop outliner used in composition courses for rearranging sections before drafting, making structural revision cheap.
