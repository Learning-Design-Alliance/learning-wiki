# Math Cards

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Math Cards is a matching activity in which learners pair cards that represent the same quantity or product in different forms — a number sentence (9 × 4), an area model, a set of dominoes, an array of dots, or a word problem. Learners must judge equivalence across representations and explain *why* the cards match, shifting the goal from speeded recall of facts to flexible understanding of what multiplication means. The activity is typically played in pairs or small groups, with discussion of equivalences as the central learning event.

## Design Implications

The strategy exploits the research base on multiple representations: connecting symbolic, pictorial, and concrete forms of the same mathematical idea builds more flexible and durable number sense than symbol-only drill [Connecting multiple representations improves conceptual and procedural outcomes.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+M]. Removing time pressure matters as much as the representations themselves — speeded fact practice produces math anxiety and does not yield durable fluency, whereas strategy-based practice does [Speeded practice increases anxiety without improving retention relative to untimed strategy-based practice.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]. The explanation step converts a matching game into conceptual discussion; without it, learners may match by surface features.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A card set with 3–5 representation types per product (number sentence, area model, dot/domino sets, equal-groups story, answer card)
- Enough cards per product that matching is non-trivial (distractor cards with near-miss products, e.g., 9 × 4 vs. 8 × 4)
- A discussion prompt: "How do you know these show the same amount?"
- Time for learners to explain equivalences aloud, not just complete matches

#### Constraints
- Effectiveness drops sharply if learners match by visual pattern rather than quantity — near-miss distractors and required explanations counteract this [-M]
- Preparation cost is real: a full class set of multi-representation cards takes significant teacher time to produce; commercial or printable sets reduce this burden
- If used as a speeded competition, the activity reproduces exactly the timed-recall conditions it is designed to avoid [-M]
- Learners who have not yet encountered area models or arrays need an introduction to those representations first, or the cards add load rather than meaning [Working-memory overload from unfamiliar representations degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Concentration/memory game format (cards face down) adds retrieval demand but can shift focus to luck; open matching better serves discussion
- "Which one doesn't belong?" variant: present four cards where three share a product and one does not
- Learners can author their own card for a given product — a stronger generation task than matching
- Extend to other operations (addition combinations, fractions, negative numbers) by changing representation types

### Target Learners
- Primary students (roughly ages 7–10) building multiplication fact fluency beyond counting strategies
- Students who know some facts by rote but cannot explain or represent them — the matching-and-explaining format targets exactly this gap [+M]
- Older students relearning foundational facts benefit from the low-stakes, untimed format

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of multiplication as area, arrays, and equal groups
- Fact fluency grounded in strategies (e.g., 9 × 4 = 10 × 4 − 4) rather than isolated recall
- Representational flexibility: moving between symbolic, pictorial, and concrete forms [+M]

### Instructions
1. Introduce or review each representation type with a whole-class example so no card format is unfamiliar.
2. Deal or distribute mixed card sets to pairs or small groups.
3. Learners find cards showing the same product and lay them out as a matched set ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. For each match, learners explain how the cards are equivalent; the teacher circulates and probes ("Where is the 4 in the domino picture?") ([Provide Feedback](../elements/provide-feedback.md)).
5. Close with a whole-class share of one interesting equivalence or a strategy someone used, connecting representations back to the number sentence.

## Examples
- A Year 3 class matches a card showing 9 × 4 with an area model card (a 9-by-4 rectangle), a domino card (two rows of dots), and a story card ("4 tables with 9 chairs each"), then explains where each factor appears in each picture.
- [Youcubed](https://www.youcubed.org/task/math-cards/) — Jo Boaler's published Math Cards task, which popularized this format as part of the "fluency without fear" approach.
- A "Which one doesn't belong?" warm-up: cards for 6 × 5, 5 × 6, 30, and 6 + 5 — the last card is the odd one out, prompting discussion of the difference between adding and multiplying.
