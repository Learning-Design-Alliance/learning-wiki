# Collaborative Critique and Redesign of Flawed Studies

## Description
Requiring students to redesign a flawed study, not just critique it, forces them to grapple with the epistemic ideals that underlie design choices (validity, objectivity, adequate sampling) rather than mechanically applying a checklist. The strategy pairs three ingredients: an authentic popular-media source reporting a psychological finding (so students encounter evidence as it actually circulates, not as a sanitized textbook example), a phenomenon that is intentionally difficult to operationalize (e.g., emotions, therapeutic "depth"), and a small-group discourse requirement that forces students to justify design choices to peers rather than submit an individual answer.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A popular-media source (newspaper article, talk, podcast) reporting a psychological claim with identifiable methodological weaknesses
- A synchronous small-group format (the source study used groups of five, 90 minutes, producing one collaborative response) so that design choices must be argued for, not just individually assumed
- A phenomenon or construct that lacks an obvious, ready-made operational definition, so students must work through construct-validity tensions rather than look one up
#### Constraints
- Without pairing critique with an actual redesign task, students tend to stop at surface-level criticism (e.g., "small sample") without engaging the underlying ideal (why does sample size matter for this claim specifically)
- A single collaborative task per topic does not, by itself, teach students to evaluate the *credibility of the source* itself (author expertise, outlet credibility) — that requires a separate emphasis if it is also a goal

### Target Learners
- Introductory-level students encountering psychological/scientific evidence evaluation for the first time
- [Grasp of Evidence framework](../principles/epistemic-cognition.md) — extends this general framework to evidence evaluation in the social/behavioral sciences specifically

### Target Learning Goals
- Shifting evidence evaluation from ideals grounded in personal experience/intuition toward disciplinary ideals (valid methods, objectivity-as-procedure, replication)
- Distinguishing a phenomenon's conceptual definition from its operational (measurable) definition

### Instructions
1. Present a popular-media report of a psychological finding with real, identifiable design flaws (e.g., an underpowered or poorly controlled study, an ill-defined construct).
2. In small groups (synchronous, ~90 minutes), have students identify the study's methodological problems.
3. Require the group to produce an alternative study design addressing those problems — including an explicit operational definition of the key construct.
4. Require a short written justification of key design choices (why this sample, why this measure) so the group must externalize the epistemic ideal behind each choice.
5. Repeat across 2-3 tasks spanning different phenomena over a course, so students encounter the same ideals (validity, objectivity, sampling) applied to different construct-definition challenges.

## Examples
- Groups evaluated the "Little Albert" experiment's ethical and methodological problems, then designed a replication with a proper sample, consent procedures, and controls.
- Groups evaluated an emotion-detection algorithm's construct validity (was it actually measuring emotion, or self-reported perception of emotion?) and designed an alternative approach combining multiple measurement instruments.
- Groups operationalized "depth" in psychotherapy — initially treating it as an unmeasurable, purely subjective quality, then converging on a specific, defensible operational definition (e.g., "focuses on a problem's underlying sources") sufficient to design a comparative study.
