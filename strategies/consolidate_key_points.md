# Consolidate Key Points

## Description
Consolidate Key Points is the step in a discussion sequence — typically following [pair talk](../patterns/think-pair-share.md) or small-group work — in which the teacher elicits, synthesizes, and explicitly states the ideas students should retain. The teacher draws together student contributions, corrects or sharpens them, and names the takeaway so that every learner leaves the activity with a clear, accurate summary rather than a fragmentary memory of the conversation.

## Design Implications

Consolidation closes the loop opened by student talk: discussion generates ideas, but without an explicit synthesis many learners — especially novices — fail to extract the general principle from the exchange [~M]. Teacher-led summarization reduces the working-memory burden of holding multiple conversational threads and supports schema formation by chunking the discussion into a coherent structure [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S]. Rosenshine's research on effective teaching identifies this as one of the most consistent practices of successful instructors: reviewing and summarizing key points at the end of each segment of instruction [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear prior activity (e.g., [Think-Pair-Share](../patterns/think-pair-share.md), [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)) whose ideas need synthesis
- Teacher knowledge of the target takeaway, so the synthesis is purposeful rather than a mere recap of who said what
- A mechanism for collecting student ideas (cold call, share-out, whiteboards) before the teacher states the consolidated version

#### Constraints
- Consolidation delivered *before* students have attempted to articulate ideas themselves removes the generative benefit of the discussion [~M] — the sequence matters: student talk first, teacher synthesis second
- If the teacher merely repeats student answers verbatim without correcting misconceptions or adding structure, consolidation adds little beyond the discussion itself [-W]
- Overly long or dense summaries can overload working memory; the takeaway should be brief and chunked [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Teacher-stated summary**: the teacher names the key point(s) explicitly after hearing student ideas — strongest for novices
- **Student-generated summary with teacher refinement**: students draft the takeaway; the teacher edits and confirms — stronger for learners with some prior knowledge, since self-generated summaries outperform received ones when learners are capable of producing them [+M]
- **Written anchor**: the consolidated point is recorded on the board, an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md), or a shared document so it persists beyond the lesson
- **Exit-ticket consolidation**: students write the takeaway themselves as a formative check ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))

### Target Learners
- Novices, who are least able to extract the "moral of the story" from unstructured discussion on their own [~M]
- Learners with limited academic language, for whom an explicit, named takeaway clarifies what the discussion was *about*
- More capable learners benefit more from generating the consolidation themselves than from receiving it [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: naming the principle the discussion surfaced
- Knowledge retention: a clear summary improves later recall, especially when revisited across sessions [Spaced repetition improves retention.](../claims/spaced-repetition-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Metacognition: modeling how to distill a conversation into its essential point

### Instructions
1. Run the generative activity first — pair talk, group work, or [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) — so students have ideas to consolidate.
2. Collect two or three student contributions, selecting ones that move toward the target idea.
3. Synthesize aloud: connect student ideas to each other and to the key point, correcting misconceptions as they surface.
4. State the takeaway explicitly and briefly — one or two chunked sentences, not a recap of the whole conversation [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S].
5. Record the takeaway visibly (board, organizer, shared notes) and, where possible, ask students to restate or write it in their own words [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

## Examples
- **Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction** — "review and summarize key points" appears as a core practice of teachers whose students made the highest gains, applied at the end of each teaching segment rather than only at lesson's end.
- **Gagné's 9 Events** — consolidation enacts the "enhance retention and transfer" event ([Gagné's 9 Events of Instruction](../patterns/gagnés-9-events-of-instruction.md)), typically via a summary and a transfer prompt.
- **Discussion-based secondary classrooms** — after small-group analysis of a text, the teacher names the interpretive move the class made ("we supported claims with textual evidence") and records it as an anchor chart.
