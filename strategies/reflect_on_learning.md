# Reflect on Learning

## Description
Reflection asks learners to deliberately revisit what they learned, how they learned it, and what remains unclear — through journaling, structured discussion, or self-assessment prompts. It converts experience into articulated knowledge: learners must retrieve, organize, and evaluate recent learning rather than simply moving on. When done collaboratively, learners also encounter peers' reasoning, exposing gaps and alternative strategies.

## Design Implications

Reflection works because it forces retrieval and elaboration — both robust consolidation mechanisms — and because articulating one's understanding surfaces misconceptions that would otherwise stay hidden [Retrieval practice strengthens long-term retention more than restudying.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. Unstructured "think about your learning" prompts produce shallow responses; effective reflection is scaffolded with specific prompts tied to lesson goals and success criteria [Assessment for learning improves achievement by making criteria and progress visible to learners.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Reflection is most powerful when it feeds forward — informing what the learner or teacher does next — rather than serving as a closing ritual.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Protected time at the end of a learning episode, not leftover time
- Specific prompts tied to the learning goal ("What strategy did you use and why?" rather than "How did it go?")
- A mechanism for reflection to influence subsequent instruction (exit tickets, revised plans, [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md))
- Psychological safety for honest self-assessment, especially in group settings

#### Constraints
- Unprompted reflection is rare; without structure, learners default to vague or purely affective responses [-M]
- Reflection on poorly encoded or superficially learned content yields little — it cannot substitute for the learning itself [-M]
- Repetitive, identical reflection routines become compliance rituals with declining value [~M]
- Novices often lack the knowledge to evaluate their own understanding accurately, producing confident but miscalibrated self-assessments [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Written journaling** — private, supports depth; slow to review
- **Exit tickets** — brief, reviewable by the teacher before the next session
- **Collaborative reflection** — [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or pair debriefs expose learners to alternative strategies and reasoning
- **Structured protocols** — sentence starters ("I used to think… now I think…"), concept-map updates, or error analyses
- **Portfolio reflection** — cumulative self-assessment against criteria over time

### Target Learners
- Adolescents and adults, who have the metacognitive capacity to evaluate strategy use; younger learners need heavily scaffolded, concrete prompts [~M]
- Learners building self-regulated learning habits — reflection is a core phase of the self-regulation cycle ([Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md))
- Struggling learners benefit from prompts that surface strategy choices, not just content recall

### Target Learning Goals
- Consolidation of content into long-term memory through retrieval and elaboration [Retrieval practice strengthens long-term retention more than restudying.](../claims/distributed-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Metacognition: awareness of what one knows, what strategies work, and where gaps remain
- Transfer: articulating underlying principles makes them available for use in new contexts
- Self-assessment accuracy against explicit criteria [Assessment for learning improves achievement by making criteria and progress visible to learners.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. **Signal the episode's end and pose a specific prompt** tied to the goal (e.g., "What was the hardest step, and what made it hard?") — use [Check-Ins](../elements/check-in.md) to establish the routine
2. **Individual reflection first** — brief writing or [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) of notes, so each learner generates their own account before social influence
3. **Peer or small-group debrief** — [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or structured pair sharing where learners compare strategies and explanations
4. **Whole-class synthesis** — collect patterns, misconceptions, and effective strategies; the teacher adjusts the next session accordingly
5. **Feed forward** — open the next session by revisiting reflections, closing the loop

## Examples
- **"I used to think… now I think…"** routine (Project Zero, Harvard Graduate School of Education, [visiblethinkingrz](https://pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines)) — a sentence-starter protocol that makes conceptual change explicit
- **Exit tickets** — students answer one content question and one strategy question on a slip or digital form; the teacher sorts responses to plan the next lesson's review
- **Collaborative debrief in project-based learning** — teams analyze what worked in their process, not just their product, before the next iteration
- **Learning journals in mathematics** — students explain their solution strategy in words, exposing procedural vs. conceptual understanding
