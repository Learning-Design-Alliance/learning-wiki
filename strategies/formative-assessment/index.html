Formative Assessment¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 7 studies (5 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 review), q2–q4 · 5 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Formative assessment is the process of gathering evidence about learner understanding while learning is underway and using that evidence to adjust instruction and learner activity. It differs from summative assessment in purpose rather than instrument: the same quiz is formative if its results change what happens next, summative if it only certifies attainment. The core loop is: elicit evidence → interpret it against learning goals → act on it (by teacher, learner, or peers) Assessment used formatively improves achievement. [+S].

Design Implications¶

Formative assessment operationalizes Assessment for Learning: assessment tasks become instructional events rather than measurement events. Its effect sizes are among the largest in education, but only when the information produced actually feeds forward into modified teaching or study behavior — assessment without consequential adjustment produces little gain Assessment used formatively improves achievement. [+S]. Feedback is the primary mechanism, and it works best when it answers "Where am I going? How am I doing? Where to next?" rather than grading the person Feedback improves learning when it addresses the task, not the self. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Clear, shared learning targets — learners cannot close a gap they cannot see (Constructive Alignment)
  • Low-stakes elicitation tasks that surface thinking, not just answers (Check-In, 3-2-1 Reflection)
  • A decision rule: the instructor must know in advance what they will do with each likely result (reteach, regroup, advance)
  • Feedback that specifies the gap between current and target performance and gives actionable next steps

Constraints¶

  • Feedback that is only evaluative (grades, praise) yields near-zero gains and can reduce performance Feedback improves learning when it addresses the task, not the self. [-M]
  • Frequent low-stakes quizzing without instructional response adds workload and anxiety without benefit Cognitive overload degrades learning. [~M]
  • Public elicitation can expose struggling learners; anonymous response systems mitigate this
  • Over-reliance on self-assessment without training produces inaccurate self-ratings, especially in novices [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Elicitation format: exit tickets, hinge-point multiple-choice questions, whiteboards, clickers, think-pair-share
  • Response locus: teacher-adjusted (reteaching), learner-adjusted (revision, self-regulation), or peer-adjusted (structured peer feedback)
  • Technology-mediated: adaptive platforms that adjust item difficulty from response data (Adaptive Learning)

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling and lower-attaining learners benefit most; ungraded frequent assessment disproportionately raises their achievement Assessment used formatively improves achievement. [+S]
  • Learners developing self-regulation, who need external monitoring cues before internalizing them
  • Less effective when learners lack the prerequisite knowledge to interpret feedback meaningfully [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: diagnosing and correcting misconceptions mid-instruction
  • Skill development: identifying which step of a procedure breaks down
  • Self-regulated learning: building learners' capacity to judge their own work against criteria

Instructions¶

  1. Define the learning target and a "hinge question" whose answers discriminate between key understandings and misconceptions.
  2. Elicit responses from all learners simultaneously (whiteboards, clickers, Check-In routines) — not just volunteers.
  3. Interpret the distribution against your decision rule; resist explaining before learners have attempted the task.
  4. Act: reteach, restructure groups, or move on — visibly connecting the adjustment to the evidence.
  5. Provide task-level feedback with a next step, then require learners to act on it (Assessment).
  6. Repeat at spaced intervals; formative assessment is a cycle, not an event.

Related Strategies¶

  • 3-2-1 Reflection — a lightweight elicitation routine for surfacing prior conceptions and confusion
  • Exit Tickets — end-of-session evidence capture that shapes the next session
  • Retrieval Practice — low-stakes quizzing that doubles as formative evidence and memory strengthening

Examples¶

  • Dylan Wiliam's "hinge questions" — multiple-choice items with distractors mapped to specific misconceptions; the whole class answers simultaneously and the teacher chooses one of several pre-planned instructional responses.
  • Kahoot! / Poll Everywhere — anonymous whole-class response systems that make the misconception distribution visible to instructor and learners alike.
  • ASSISTments — online math homework platform that gives immediate task-level feedback to students and error-pattern reports to teachers, who adjust the next lesson.
