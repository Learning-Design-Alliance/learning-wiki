# Check In Activities

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Check in activities are short, low-stakes prompts or routines — a one-word mood poll, a "rose and thorn" share, a quick exit-to-entry question — that open a session by inviting learners to report how they are arriving. They serve two functions simultaneously: giving the instructor actionable information about readiness and affect, and signaling that each learner is seen as a person, not only a performer of tasks.

## Design Implications

Check ins work primarily through social-emotional and motivational channels rather than direct content instruction: belonging and teacher-student relationships are reliable predictors of engagement and persistence [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Their formative value depends on the instructor actually using what is learned — adjusting pacing, grouping, or framing in response — since information gathered but ignored teaches learners that the ritual is empty [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A consistent, predictable routine so the ritual itself lowers social risk
- Prompts that are genuinely low-stakes and optional in depth — learners should be able to participate without forced self-disclosure
- A visible instructor response loop: naming patterns ("several of you flagged confusion about X") and acting on them
- Time discipline — 2–5 minutes, or the activity crowds out instruction

#### Constraints
- Forced or repetitive emotional disclosure can feel surveilling and reduce trust, especially with adolescents [~M]
- In large classes, individual check ins become logistically impossible; aggregate formats (polls, sticky notes) preserve function but weaken relational signaling [-W]
- If responses never influence instruction, learners disengage from the ritual and it consumes time [-M]
- Poorly framed prompts ("how is everyone?") elicit social-desirability answers rather than usable information [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Affective check ins** — mood scales, emoji polls, one-word weather metaphors; fastest, best for pacing decisions
- **Cognitive check ins** — "one thing you're still unsure about from last class"; connects to [Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md)
- **Relational check ins** — brief personal shares in small groups; strongest belonging effect, highest time cost
- **Asynchronous check ins** — LMS polls or entry tickets in online and flipped settings; see [Flipped Classroom](../patterns/flipped-classroom.md)

### Target Learners
- Learners in new or transitional environments (start of term, post-break, new cohorts), where belonging uncertainty is highest [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Online and hybrid learners, for whom check ins substitute for ambient social cues available in physical classrooms [+W]
- Less valuable as a standing daily ritual for mature, stable cohorts, where it can feel performative [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional: building classroom community and psychological safety
- Metacognitive: prompting learners to notice and name their own readiness and confusion
- Self-regulation: supporting learners to connect their state to their choices [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Choose a consistent format and time slot; predictability lowers social risk.
2. Pose a low-stakes prompt (affective, cognitive, or relational) — see [Check In](../elements/check-in.md) for the element-level design.
3. Collect responses quickly (poll, round-robin, sticky notes, chat).
4. Acknowledge what you heard and make one visible instructional adjustment — pacing, grouping, or re-teaching — so learners see the loop close.
5. Vary prompts periodically to prevent ritual fatigue; a game format such as ['What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In](../strategies/whats_my_emotion_game_check-in.md) can renew engagement.

## Examples
- **Responsive Classroom Morning Meeting** ([https://www.responsiveclassroom.org](https://www.responsiveclassroom.org)) — a structured daily greeting, share, and activity routine used in K-8 classrooms; the greeting/share phases are canonical check ins.
- **Zoom/Teams emoji or one-word polls** at the start of synchronous online sessions — aggregate affect data in under a minute for large sections.
- **"Muddiest point" entry tickets** in university courses — students submit one lingering confusion from the previous session, which the instructor addresses in the first ten minutes.
