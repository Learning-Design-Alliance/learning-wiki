# After Action Review

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
An After Action Review (AAR) is a structured reflection conducted immediately after a task, simulation, or performance episode. Learners answer four canonical questions — what was supposed to happen, what actually happened, why the differences occurred, and what will be done differently next time — converting experience into explicit, transferable lessons. The format originated in the U.S. Army and has been adapted widely in medical simulation, aviation, and project-based education.

## Design Implications

AARs work because they force comparison between expected and observed outcomes, which surfaces faulty mental models that unstructured experience leaves implicit [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. The review must be blame-free and learner-led; when facilitators dominate or attribute error to individuals, psychological safety collapses and participants stop surfacing genuine causes. Timing matters: conducting the AAR while memory of the event is fresh preserves diagnostic detail.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A concrete, recently completed performance event with observable outcomes
- A facilitator (or trained peer) who enforces the four-question structure and a no-blame norm
- Time to articulate causes and commit to specific changes ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md))
- A record of lessons learned that feeds into the next attempt or [Practice](../elements/practice.md) cycle

#### Constraints
- In low-trust or hierarchically charged environments, learners underreport errors and the review produces sanitized lessons [-M]
- Reviews of rare or ambiguous events can entrench wrong causal explanations when groups settle on a plausible-but-incorrect "why" without evidence [~M]
- AARs conducted long after the event lose accuracy as memory reconstructs; immediate or same-day review is substantially more diagnostic [~M]
- Overuse on routine tasks breeds ritual compliance — the format adds value only when outcomes were uncertain or performance fell short

#### Implementation Variability
- **Facilitator-led** (medical simulation debriefs): an instructor probes causal reasoning with advocacy-inquiry questioning
- **Peer-led team AAR** (project-based learning): students rotate the facilitator role to build evaluative capability
- **Micro-AAR**: a two-minute self-questioning routine after individual problem sets or writing drafts
- **Written AAR logs**: asynchronous versions in online courses, ideally paired with instructor [Feedback](../elements/feedback.md) on the causal analysis itself

### Target Learners
- Learners in simulation, clinical, or performance-based settings where errors carry diagnostic information [+M]
- Intermediate learners who have enough experience to generate causal hypotheses; pure novices often lack the knowledge to explain *why* outcomes diverged and need expert modeling of the causal analysis [~M]
- Teams, where shared review builds a common model of what "good" looks like

### Target Learning Goals
- Transfer: extracting generalizable principles from specific episodes
- Self-regulation: building the habit of monitoring and adjusting one's own performance
- Diagnostic reasoning: linking outcomes to underlying causes rather than surface features

### Instructions
1. Complete the task, simulation, or performance episode and capture observable outcomes.
2. Ask: *What was supposed to happen?* — restate the intent and success criteria.
3. Ask: *What actually happened?* — establish facts before interpretations.
4. Ask: *Why was there a difference?* — probe causal explanations, using [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) to make reasoning explicit; the facilitator models expert causal analysis for novices.
5. Ask: *What will we do differently?* — commit to specific, testable adjustments for the next cycle.
6. Record lessons and revisit them at the start of the next attempt, closing the loop with [Practice](../elements/practice.md).

## Related Strategies
- Structured debriefing in simulation-based education — the clinical adaptation of the same four-question protocol
- Error analysis — AAR is the team-level, event-driven form of the same diagnostic move

## Examples
- **U.S. Army training doctrine** — the original AAR format, run at soldier, team, and unit levels after every training exercise, with lessons rolled into the next iteration ([Army field guidance](https://armypubs.army.mil)).
- **Medical simulation debriefing** — programs such as those following the [Center for Medical Simulation](https://harvardmedsim.org) debriefing model use advocacy-inquiry versions of the AAR after mannequin-based scenarios.
- **Agile retrospectives** — software teams run sprint retrospectives that adapt the AAR's intent/actual/cause/change structure to development work.
