# Source Evaluation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Source evaluation is the practice of deliberately assessing where information comes from, who produced it, and whether it can be trusted — before relying on it in research, argument, or decision-making. As a strategy, it means teaching learners explicit evaluative moves (checking provenance, corroboration, and evidence quality) rather than assuming these judgments develop incidentally.

## Design Implications

Explicit instruction in evaluation techniques substantially improves learners' ability to judge online and print sources [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' source evaluation.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. The most effective approaches teach *lateral* moves — leaving a source to check what others say about it — rather than relying on internal features like design quality or tone, which novices over-weight and manipulators exploit. Evaluation should be embedded in authentic tasks where the judgment matters (building an argument, answering a research question), not taught as an isolated checklist [Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective in online contexts.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic information tasks with genuinely variable source quality (web research, current events, literature review)
- Explicit modeling of expert evaluative moves, ideally via [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) demonstration of how a skilled reader investigates a source
- Criteria that go beyond surface features: authorship, funding, corroboration across independent sources, evidence quality
- Opportunities to compare sources making competing claims ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md))

#### Constraints
- Checklist approaches (CRAAP test and similar) transfer poorly to real web contexts, where professional-looking sites routinely masquerade as credible [Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective in online contexts.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-S]
- Novices judge credibility by surface features (design polish, .org domains, confident tone), so instruction that does not directly confront these heuristics leaves them unchanged
- Evaluation skills are domain-sensitive; training in one topic area does not automatically generalize without varied practice contexts
- High working-memory demand when learners must evaluate while also comprehending; scaffold by separating the two phases [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Lateral reading**: professional fact-checkers leave a source immediately to search for outside information about it; teach this as the default first move
- **Corroboration protocols**: require a minimum of independent sources before a claim enters student work ([3-Source Rule](3-source_rule.md))
- **Provenance tracing**: follow claims, images, or quotes back to their original context
- **Structured comparison**: give learners two sources on the same event and ask which is more reliable and why

### Target Learners
- Secondary and post-secondary students conducting independent research [Explicit civic online reasoning instruction improves students' source evaluation.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Learners in civic education contexts who will encounter persuasive and partisan content
- Adult learners in professional programs (health, journalism, policy) where information quality has real consequences
- Younger learners benefit from simplified versions (who made this? how do they know?) but need concrete modeling

### Target Learning Goals
- Information literacy: judging credibility and reliability of sources
- Critical thinking: distinguishing evidence quality from rhetorical confidence
- Argumentation: selecting defensible evidence for claims ([Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md))
- Civic reasoning: evaluating news, media, and political claims

### Instructions
1. **Model expert evaluation** — demonstrate lateral reading and provenance checking on a live, real-world source using [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md); include a source that *looks* credible but fails scrutiny
2. **Contrast cases** — present paired sources on the same topic with differing reliability and have learners diagnose the differences ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md)) [Comparing and contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
3. **Guided practice** — learners evaluate new sources using the modeled moves, with prompts fading over time
4. **Embed in authentic tasks** — require evaluated sources as the evidence base for an [Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md) task or research product, so evaluation has consequences
5. **Corroboration requirement** — enforce a minimum independent-source standard ([3-Source Rule](3-source_rule.md)) in finished work

## Examples
- **Stanford History Education Group (SHEG) civic online reasoning curriculum** ([https://cor.stanford.edu](https://cor.stanford.edu)) — classroom lessons built on fact-checker strategies (lateral reading, click restraint, provenance), validated in large-scale assessments
- **News Literacy Project — Checkology** ([https://checkology.org](https://checkology.org)) — interactive modules on source credibility, misinformation, and evidence standards for secondary students
- **First-year composition courses** requiring annotated bibliographies in which students justify each source's authority and evidence quality before drafting an argument
