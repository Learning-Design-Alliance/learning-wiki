# Multisensory Phonics Instruction

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Multisensory phonics instruction teaches grapheme–phoneme correspondences by engaging several channels at once: the learner looks at the letter, says its sound, and simultaneously traces it in sand, forms it with tiles, taps the phonemes on their fingers, or writes it in the air. The approach is most associated with the Orton-Gillingham tradition and its derivatives, where "simultaneous multisensory" is one component of a package that is also explicit, systematic, cumulative, and diagnostic.

An honest account of this strategy has to separate those components, because they are not equally well supported. Explicit, systematic phonics is one of the best-established findings in reading research. The specifically *multisensory* addition is not, and the two are routinely credited together.

## Design Implications

The systematic-phonics core is strongly supported: explicit, sequenced instruction in letter–sound correspondences improves word reading substantially, and more so than approaches that leave the code to be inferred [Systematic Phonics Improves Word Reading](../claims/systematic-phonics-improves-word-reading.md) [+S] [Phonics Instruction Outperforms Whole Word For Generalization](../claims/phonics-instruction-outperforms-whole-word-for-generalization.md) [+S]. The gains are largest for beginning readers and for those at risk of difficulty, and they run through decoding to comprehension, because accurate automatic word recognition frees capacity for meaning [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]. Phonemic awareness training is the companion piece [Phonemic Awareness Training Improves Reading](../claims/phonemic-awareness-training-improves-reading.md) [+S].

What has not been isolated is the contribution of the multisensory element itself. Reviews of Orton-Gillingham-based programmes find that the studies almost never separate the multisensory component from the explicit, systematic, cumulative structure it travels with, so the effects attributed to "multisensory" are as consistent with the structure doing the work [~W]. This matters practically: a school choosing between a multisensory programme and a systematic non-multisensory one is choosing between a strongly supported thing and the same strongly supported thing with an unevaluated addition — not between an enhanced and a basic version.

The addition is also frequently justified by an appeal to visual, auditory, and kinaesthetic learner types, which is not a defensible rationale — matching instruction to a diagnosed learner style yields no benefit [Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning](../claims/learning-styles-matching-does-not-improve-learning.md) [-S]. The defensible rationale is different and narrower: tracing and building slow the learner down at the point of grapheme–phoneme mapping and give an additional motor trace of a form that is easily confused visually.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A defined scope and sequence of correspondences, taught in a deliberate order from simple and high-utility to complex — the systematic part is the part that carries the evidence
- Cumulative review, so previously taught correspondences continue to appear
- Materials for the tactile channel that do not themselves demand attention: sand trays, letter tiles, textured cards ([Word Building](word_building.md))
- Immediate corrective feedback at the point of error, before the wrong mapping is rehearsed ([Immediate Feedback](../elements/immediate-feedback.md))
- Decodable text so that new correspondences are practised in reading, not only in isolation ([Decodable Text Reading](decodable-text-reading.md))
- Ongoing diagnostic assessment to place each learner in the sequence rather than teaching the whole class the same lesson

#### Constraints
- The multisensory component is not separately evidenced; the systematic, explicit, cumulative structure it accompanies is where the demonstrated effect lies [~W]
- Justifying the approach by learner "styles" is unsupported and can lead to withholding print-based instruction from learners labelled kinaesthetic [Learning Styles Matching Does Not Improve Learning](../claims/learning-styles-matching-does-not-improve-learning.md) [-S]
- Tactile materials can capture attention in their own right; sand, tiles, and manipulatives add extraneous processing when the activity becomes about the material [Cognitive Overload Degrades Learning](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Slower per-correspondence than a purely visual-auditory routine, which matters when instructional time is the binding constraint [-M]
- Phonics gains are strongest in the early years and for struggling readers; extending intensive decoding instruction to fluent readers displaces work on comprehension and vocabulary [~M]
- Programme fidelity requires substantial teacher training, and the Orton-Gillingham literature is dominated by small studies with weak designs [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Orton-Gillingham and derivatives** — the full package: explicit, systematic, cumulative, diagnostic, simultaneous multisensory
- **Letter tiles and word building** — physical manipulation of graphemes to build and change words ([Word Building](word_building.md))
- **Elkonin boxes and finger tapping** — segmenting phonemes with a motor action, targeting phonemic awareness rather than the grapheme
- **Sand, gel, and air writing** — motor tracing of letter forms while producing the sound ([Air Writing](air_writing.md))
- **Sound walls** — phoneme-organized reference display, replacing the alphabetic word wall ([Building a Sound Wall](building_a_sound_wall.md))
- **Systematic phonics without the multisensory layer** — the same sequence and explicitness delivered visually and orally ([Explicit Systematic Phonics Instruction](explicit-systematic-phonics-instruction.md))

### Target Learners
- Beginning readers acquiring the alphabetic code, where phonics gains are largest [Systematic Phonics Improves Word Reading](../claims/systematic-phonics-improves-word-reading.md) [+S]
- Learners with dyslexia or persistent decoding difficulty, the population the Orton-Gillingham tradition was built for
- Learners who confuse visually similar graphemes, where a motor trace of the form plausibly helps
- Older struggling readers who need the code taught explicitly without primary-school framing
- Poor fit for fluent decoders, for whom continued correspondence drill displaces comprehension and vocabulary work

### Target Learning Goals
- Accurate decoding of unfamiliar words through grapheme–phoneme mapping
- Phonemic awareness: segmenting, blending, and manipulating sounds [Phonological Awareness Training Improves Reading](../claims/phonological-awareness-training-improves-reading.md) [+S]
- Encoding — spelling, which the same correspondences serve in reverse
- Automatic word recognition, and through it, reading comprehension [Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) [+S]

### Instructions
1. **Assess and place.** Determine which correspondences the learner already controls, and start at the first gap rather than at the start of the programme ([Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md)).
2. **Introduce one correspondence explicitly.** Name the grapheme, produce the phoneme, show the mouth position — do not ask learners to induce it from examples ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md)).
3. **Add the motor channel at the point of mapping.** Learner traces or builds the grapheme while saying the sound, so the action and the sound co-occur rather than alternate.
4. **Blend into words immediately.** Move from the isolated correspondence into words containing it in the same session ([Word Building](word_building.md)).
5. **Read it in connected text.** Practise the new correspondence in decodable text where it actually appears, so decoding transfers to reading ([Decodable Text Reading](decodable-text-reading.md)).
6. **Spell it back.** Dictate words using the correspondence, since encoding checks the mapping in the other direction.
7. **Correct at the point of error.** Interrupt and re-run the mapping immediately rather than letting the error be rehearsed ([Immediate Feedback](../elements/immediate-feedback.md)).
8. **Review cumulatively.** Include previously taught correspondences in every session's word lists ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).

## Examples

**Orton-Gillingham lessons in a dyslexia clinic:** A structured session moves through phonogram review with tracing, new correspondence introduction, word building with tiles, dictation, and decodable text — the multisensory element woven through a strictly cumulative sequence.

**Sand-tray letter formation in reception:** Children trace a grapheme in a sand tray while producing its phoneme, then find the same grapheme in a word — motor formation tied directly to the sound rather than to handwriting practice.

**Finger tapping for phoneme segmentation:** Learners tap one finger per phoneme before spelling, giving a physical count that makes an omitted sound visible.

**Systematic phonics without manipulatives:** A whole-class programme delivering the same scope, sequence, explicitness, and cumulative review through choral response and written practice — the comparison that shows how much of the effect the structure alone carries.
