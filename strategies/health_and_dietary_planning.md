# Health and Dietary Planning

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners study dietary guidance such as the food pyramid or food-plate models, learn about daily nutritional requirements, and then plan food for one day that satisfies those requirements. The strategy combines content learning (what the body needs) with authentic decision-making (constructing a plan under constraints), so knowledge of nutrition is applied rather than merely recalled.

## Design Implications

Planning a day's meals is an application task: learners must translate a general guideline ("balance the food groups") into a concrete, constraint-satisfying artifact. This mirrors research on implementation intentions, where forming a concrete if-then plan is more effective than forming a general intention [Gollwitzer, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493) [+M]. The task also benefits from [Activation](../principles/activation.md) — eliciting learners' own eating habits first makes the nutritional content relevant and gives them a baseline plan to revise [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- An authoritative dietary reference (food pyramid, MyPlate, or national guidelines) presented at an appropriate reading level
- Nutritional data for common foods, in a form learners can look up without excessive effort ([Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md)) [+M]
- A planning template (meal grid or worksheet) that structures the day into meals and snacks
- A way to check the plan against requirements — self-check, peer check, or teacher feedback ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)) [+S]

#### Constraints
- If learners must compute nutritional values from raw data tables, working memory load can crowd out the conceptual goal [Overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-S] — provide pre-summarized food cards or a simplified lookup tool
- Dietary guidance is a simplification; learners may overgeneralize (e.g., treating all fats as bad) unless exceptions and individual variation are discussed [~M]
- Cultural and socioeconomic variation in food access means a "correct" plan may be unrealistic for some learners; plans should be judged on nutritional reasoning, not on specific foods chosen
- For learners with disordered eating or food insecurity, the task requires sensitivity and should never involve tracking or restricting actual intake

#### Implementation Variability
- Younger learners (K–4): sort picture cards of foods into groups, then assemble a day from the sorted cards
- Older learners: use real nutrition labels or a database (e.g., MyPlate tools) and justify trade-offs in writing
- Extension: plan for a specific persona (an athlete, a toddler, a person with diabetes) to add constraint complexity and perspective-taking
- Cross-curricular: combine with mathematics (servings, fractions, budgeting) or social studies (food systems, access)

### Target Learners
- Elementary and middle-grade learners encountering nutrition science for the first time
- Learners who benefit from concrete, personally relevant application rather than abstract content [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Family/consumer science and health education students at secondary level, with added complexity

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual: understanding food groups, balanced diets, and daily requirements
- Procedural: planning and decision-making under multiple constraints
- Attitudinal: building durable motivation for healthy eating through personal ownership [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Activate prior knowledge: have learners record or discuss what they ate yesterday ([Activating Prior Knowledge](activating-prior-knowledge.md)).
2. Present the dietary reference (food pyramid/MyPlate) with worked modeling of one already-planned meal ([Application](../elements/application.md)).
3. Learners determine daily requirements for a target person (themselves or a persona).
4. Learners plan a full day of food using food cards, labels, or a database, checking each meal against the requirements ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
5. Peer or teacher review: does the plan meet the requirements? What trade-offs were made? Revise and share.
