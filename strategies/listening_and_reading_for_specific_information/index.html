Listening and reading for specific information¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 unmarked) · 10 studies (3 causal, 3 review, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

This strategy directs learners to extract particular details — names, dates, figures, key phrases, or specific ideas — from spoken or written input, rather than attempting exhaustive comprehension. Learners receive a purpose or task before engaging with the material, listen or read selectively, monitor their comprehension, and infer missing meaning from linguistic and contextual clues. It is a core comprehension strategy in second-language pedagogy and in academic study skills, where learners must process lectures, reports, and examinations efficiently.

Design Implications¶

Selective attention to a stated purpose reduces the processing burden of full comprehension and directs working memory to task-relevant input Relevancy of emphasis directs attention. [+M]. Effectiveness depends on giving learners the purpose before exposure: tasks assigned after input begins produce unfocused processing and weaker extraction. Pre-teaching key vocabulary and activating relevant background knowledge further reduces the lexical and conceptual load of real-time comprehension Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Authentic or level-appropriate texts and audio with identifiable, extractable target information
  • A pre-listening/pre-reading task that states the specific purpose (e.g., a table to complete, questions to answer)
  • Explicit instruction and Think-Aloud modeling of how skilled listeners and readers locate and verify target information
  • Guided practice with graduated task difficulty, followed by feedback on accuracy of extraction

Constraints¶

  • Learners with limited vocabulary or decoding fluency cannot allocate attention to selective search; automatic word recognition is a prerequisite for higher-level comprehension Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+S]
  • Over-reliance on scanning for details can displace global comprehension — learners may extract facts without building a coherent mental model of the whole text [~M]
  • Fast, unsegmented authentic audio can exceed processing capacity for lower-proficiency learners, causing them to lose the thread entirely [-M]
  • Tasks that name the target too precisely (e.g., "listen for the word tariff") encourage word-matching rather than meaning-based comprehension [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Pre-task vs. while-task framing: purpose given before exposure (stronger) vs. questions answered during or after (weaker but useful for assessment)
  • Modality: listening tasks benefit from replay and segmentation; reading tasks allow self-paced re-reading and Annotating
  • Scaffolding intensity: from word lists and partial transcripts down to unaided extraction as proficiency grows
  • Task format: gap-fills, tables, schedules, mind maps, cause–effect charts, note-taking frames

Target Learners¶

  • Adult second-language learners transitioning to academic study or workplace training, where lecture and document comprehension are high-stakes [+M]
  • Intermediate learners who have basic decoding fluency but lack strategic processing habits; beginners need vocabulary and fluency support first Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+S]
  • Exam candidates (e.g., IELTS, TOEFL) who must locate answers under time pressure

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension of specific factual information in extended input
  • Strategic processing: setting purposes, monitoring comprehension, inferring from context
  • Note-taking and information-transfer skills that support later study tasks

Instructions¶

  1. Activate relevant background knowledge and pre-teach essential vocabulary (Activation; Activating Prior Knowledge)
  2. Present the purpose and the extraction task (a table, questions, or graphic organizer) before exposure (Advance Organizers)
  3. Model the strategy on a short excerpt, verbalizing how to locate and verify target information (Think-Aloud)
  4. Have learners engage with the full material, extracting the targeted information (Assigned Readings or Lectures)
  5. Debrief: compare answers, discuss where information occurred and how it was identified, and have learners explain their reasoning (Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.) [+M]
  6. Repeat with progressively longer, faster, or less scaffolded material

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — pre-reading knowledge activation makes selective search more efficient
  • Skimming and Scanning — the complementary rapid-processing techniques for gist and location
  • Note-Taking — the common downstream use of extracted information
  • Teach IELTS listening through explicit strategies and tips alongside task-based materials

Related Elements¶

  • Advance Organizers — frame the purpose before exposure
  • Annotating — marks extracted information during reading
  • Chunking — segments long input into processable units
  • Think-Aloud — makes expert selective-processing moves visible

Tools¶

  • BBC Learning English (https://www.bbc.co.uk/learningenglish) — graded audio with comprehension tasks
  • TED-Ed / TED Talks (https://ed.ted.com) — authentic lectures with built-in listening comprehension questions
  • ReadWorks (https://www.readworks.org) — leveled texts with question sets targeting specific details

Examples¶

  • Learners complete a train schedule while listening to station announcements, extracting times and platform numbers.
  • Students fill in a cause–effect chart while listening to a recorded university lecture on climate policy.
  • ESL learners read a short news article to answer "who, what, when, where" questions, then justify their answers by pointing to the relevant sentences.
  • IELTS preparation courses train candidates to read questions first, predict the type of answer (number, name, date), and listen for paraphrase rather than exact wording.
