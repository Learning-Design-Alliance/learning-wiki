# Retrieval Practice Warm Ups

## Description
Retrieval practice warm ups are brief activities at the start of a lesson in which learners recall previously learned material from memory — answering questions, solving a few problems, or summarizing prior content — before new instruction begins. They function simultaneously as [retrieval practice](../principles/retrieval-practice.md), spaced review, and a formative check that surfaces gaps before new material builds on old.

## Design Implications

Warm ups exploit the testing effect: recalling information strengthens its memory trace more than rereading or restudying it [Retrieval practice produces durable, transferable learning.](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. Because warm ups recur at the start of each lesson, they naturally implement spaced retrieval, which amplifies the retention benefit over single-session practice [Spaced retrieval outperforms massed retrieval for long-term retention.](../claims/spaced-retrieval-improves-retention.md) [+S]. They also serve an activation function, bringing prior knowledge into working memory so new instruction can connect to it [Activation of prior knowledge improves learning from new instruction.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Questions or problems targeting material from *previous* lessons, not the current one
- Low stakes: ungraded or lightly graded, so errors are safe and informative
- A brief feedback or review step so learners correct errors immediately [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Short duration (3–8 minutes) so it does not crowd out new instruction

#### Constraints
- Retrieving material learned only minutes ago yields little benefit; the warm up must target earlier content for spacing to operate [Spaced retrieval outperforms massed retrieval for long-term retention.](../claims/spaced-retrieval-improves-retention.md) [-M]
- If warm ups become graded high-stakes quizzes, they shift from retrieval to assessment and can raise anxiety without adding learning benefit [~M]
- Purely factual recall questions build retention of facts but transfer little to application; at least some questions should require inference or application [~M]
- Errors retrieved with high confidence but without corrective feedback can entrench misconceptions [High-confidence errors improve retention only when corrected.](../claims/high-confidence-errors-improve-retention.md) [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Brain dump**: learners write everything they remember about a topic; works when content is broad and questions would cue too narrowly
- **Two-question quiz**: 2–4 short answer or multiple-choice items, often delivered via clickers or tools like [Kahoot!](https://kahoot.com) or [Quizizz](https://quizizz.com)
- **Cumulative problem warm up**: one or two problems mixing this week's and last month's material, common in mathematics (e.g., "Do Now" routines in [Illustrative Mathematics](https://illustrativemathematics.org) curricula)
- **Retrieval + elaboration**: after recall, learners explain *why* an answer is correct, adding a generative step

### Target Learners
- All learners benefit from retrieval, but warm ups are especially valuable for learners who do not spontaneously self-test while studying [Retrieval practice produces durable, transferable learning.](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]
- Struggling learners benefit from the low-stakes error surfacing, provided feedback follows promptly [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Learners with high prior knowledge still gain from retrieval, though they may need harder, application-level questions to avoid trivial recall [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Long-term retention of declarative and procedural knowledge
- Fluency building: fast, accurate recall of foundational facts and procedures
- Cumulative course coherence: keeping earlier units accessible while later ones build on them

### Instructions
1. Select 2–5 questions covering material from at least one week prior, mixing fact recall with one application item.
2. Present the questions before any review of the material — retrieval must come from memory, not notes.
3. Set a short timer (3–5 minutes) and keep stakes low; frame errors as useful information.
4. Reveal answers and give brief corrective feedback, addressing common errors aloud [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].
5. Transition into new instruction by linking the warm up content to today's topic ([Activation](../elements/activation.md)).
6. Rotate question sources across the term so all major content is revisited on a spaced schedule.

## Examples
- **"Do Now" routines** in US mathematics classrooms: 2–3 cumulative problems on the board as students enter, reviewed together before the lesson
- **[Retrieval Practice](https://www.retrievalpractice.org) (Agarwal & Bain)** — teacher-facing resources documenting starter quizzes and "brain dump" warm ups across K–12 subjects
- **Language classrooms**: opening each session with 5 vocabulary items from previous weeks recalled orally before new vocabulary is introduced
