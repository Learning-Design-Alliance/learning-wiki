# Teach students expert-like evaluation skills and epistemology to work with, not defer to, AI

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 design), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Description
Faced with an AI that can pass an introductory physics course, the article recommends taking this as a wake-up call and teaching the inherently human skill of evaluating correctness of one's own and others' work. It names techniques including "dimensional analysis, order-of-magnitude estimates, checking for coherence, considering implications, and the ability to consider limiting cases". Moving students toward a more expert-like epistemology is framed as increasingly important as AI permeates professional life.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instruction must develop students' ability to evaluate the correctness of their own and others' work rather than accept presented results uncritically.
#### Constraints
- The article warns that retreating to few high-stakes proctored exams would fly in the face of physics education research favoring frequent formative assessment and spaced repetition, and that detector-based defence is an arms race it calls potentially fruitless.

### Target Learners
- undergraduate introductory physics students

### Target Learning Goals
- evaluating correctness of problem solutions
- expert-like epistemology in physics problem solving
