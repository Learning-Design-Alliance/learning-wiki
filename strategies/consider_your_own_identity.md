# Consider Your Own Identity

## Description
Educators systematically examine their own racial identity, social position, biases, and privileges as preparation for facilitating conversations about race. Structured tools support this work: Glen Singleton and Curtis Linton's *Courageous Conversations About Race* framework, Peggy McIntosh's "invisible knapsack" inventory of white privilege, the Intercultural Development Inventory (IDI) as a measure of intercultural sensitivity, and the construction of a personal racial autobiography. The premise is that unexamined identity leaks into facilitation — through avoidance, defensiveness, or microaggressions — and that self-knowledge is a prerequisite for creating an equitable classroom climate.

## Design Implications

Self-reflection on identity functions as a form of [Activation](../principles/activation.md) applied to the facilitator rather than the learner: surfacing prior assumptions before they interfere with new learning [Activation improves learning outcomes.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Research on teacher development shows that structured critical self-reflection produces stronger culturally responsive practice than unstructured sensitivity training [Developing cultural critical consciousness improves preservice teachers' culturally responsive practice.](../claims/building-empathy-improves-intergroup-attitudes.md) [+W]. The work is developmental and iterative, not a one-time exercise; identity reflection must be revisited as educators encounter new populations and contexts.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A commitment to sustained self-reflection, not a single workshop session
- Structured protocols and instruments (e.g., the [Courageous Conversation](https://courageousconversation.com) protocol, the [IDI](https://idiinventory.com)) rather than open-ended introspection alone
- Time for individual reflection plus facilitated peer discussion with agreed norms
- Psychological safety — reflection on privilege and bias triggers defensiveness that must be worked with, not suppressed

#### Constraints
- One-shot diversity training without follow-up produces little durable change in educator practice [-W]
- Confronting privilege can trigger defensive reactions (guilt, denial, tone-policing) that derail reflection if not anticipated and facilitated [~M]
- Self-reported bias awareness is a weak proxy for actual behavior change; reflection must be paired with observation and feedback on practice [-W]
- Educators from marginalized groups bear disproportionate emotional labor when reflection is framed as a shared, equally-costly activity [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Racial autobiography** — a written narrative tracing one's own racial socialization, shared in small groups
- **Instrument-based** — IDI assessment with individual debrief coaching against a developmental profile
- **Protocol-based** — Singleton and Linton's four agreements (stay engaged, speak your truth, experience discomfort, expect/accept non-closure) governing ongoing staff inquiry
- **Embedded** — identity reflection woven into lesson planning and post-teaching debriefs rather than isolated sessions

### Target Learners
- Educators preparing to facilitate discussions about race, particularly those whose racial or cultural position differs from their students'
- Preservice teachers, for whom structured critical self-reflection during training predicts later culturally responsive practice [+W]
- School staff teams doing whole-faculty equity work, where shared vocabulary and norms matter

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive self-awareness: recognizing one's own assumptions, privileges, and blind spots
- Facilitator readiness: managing one's own emotional responses during charged discussions
- Dispositional change: moving from colorblind or deficit framings toward culturally sustaining practice

### Instructions
1. Establish group norms for discomfort and non-closure before any content work, using a structured [Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) to surface current emotional states.
2. Complete a structured self-inventory — McIntosh's privilege knapsack, an IDI assessment, or a racial autobiography prompt — as individual [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) of one's own position.
3. Process the inventory in facilitated peer discussion with [Active Listening](active-listening.md) norms, focusing on patterns across narratives rather than debating individual claims.
4. Connect insights to practice: identify specific facilitation moves (question framing, response to student comments, curriculum choices) that your identity position makes easier or harder.
5. Revisit and reassess periodically; treat identity reflection as ongoing inquiry rather than a completed credential.

## Examples
- **Courageous Conversations About Race** ([courageousconversation.com](https://courageousconversation.com)) — Singleton and Linton's protocol used in district-wide staff equity work; educators examine their own racial position using the "race matters" compass before facilitating student dialogue.
- **Intercultural Development Inventory** ([idiinventory.com](https://idiinventory.com)) — a validated 50-item assessment of intercultural sensitivity, administered with a qualified administrator and individual debrief.
- **McIntosh's "Unpacking the Invisible Knapsack"** — a widely used list-based reflection on unearned privilege, typically followed by small-group sharing of which items resonated.
- **Racial autobiography in teacher education** — preservice programs asking candidates to write and share narratives of their own racial socialization before their first field placements.
