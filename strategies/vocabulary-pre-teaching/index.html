Vocabulary Pre Teaching¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (2 for, 2 against) · 10 studies (4 review, 3 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 2 of 10 report an effect size

Description¶

Vocabulary pre-teaching introduces the meanings of selected words before learners read a text, watch a demonstration, or begin a task. The instructor selects a small set of high-impact words, provides student-friendly definitions and examples, and often engages learners in brief active processing (e.g., generating examples, connecting to known concepts) so the meanings are available when the words appear in context.

Design Implications¶

Pre-teaching removes a common bottleneck in comprehension: when too many words are unknown, working memory is consumed by decoding meaning instead of building a situation model Automatic word recognition frees resources for comprehension. [+M]. Effects are strongest when the taught words are genuinely critical to understanding the passage and when instruction goes beyond dictionary definitions to include multiple encounters and active use [~M]. Because vocabulary is learned incrementally, pre-teaching works best as the first exposure in a cycle of repeated, spaced encounters rather than a one-time event.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Careful word selection: prioritize words that are (a) essential to comprehending the text, (b) likely unknown, and (c) broadly useful (e.g., Beck et al.'s "Tier Two" academic vocabulary)
  • Student-friendly definitions — conversational explanations with examples, not dictionary paraphrases
  • Active engagement: learners say, use, or transform the word (generate examples, contrast with near-synonyms, connect to prior knowledge via Analogies)
  • A follow-up context (the text or task) in which learners actually encounter and use the words
  • Text analysis to identify which words actually block comprehension — typically 5–8 words, not 20
  • Student-friendly, conversational definitions rather than dictionary phrasing (Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax)
  • Multiple encounters: example sentences, nonlinguistic representation, or a quick use-it-yourself prompt

Constraints¶

  • Pre-teaching too many words dilutes instruction and overloads working memory; benefits shrink when word lists exceed roughly 5–10 per lesson Chunking reduces working memory load. [-M]
  • Word meanings taught in isolation, without later reinforcement in context, show weak and fast-decaying effects [-M] — single pre-teaching sessions rarely produce durable word learning
  • Pre-teaching can remove productive struggle: when the goal is inferring meaning from context, supplying definitions beforehand eliminates the practice the activity was designed to provide [~M]
  • Effects on general comprehension are smaller than effects on word-specific knowledge; pre-teaching a handful of words rarely moves whole-text comprehension unless the words are truly pivotal [-W]
  • Pre-teaching too many words dilutes instruction and overloads learners; comprehension benefits shrink as word lists grow Vocabulary instruction improves comprehension most when it is rich and involves active processing. [-M]
  • Ineffective when the pre-taught words are marginal to the passage's main ideas — the time cost yields no comprehension gain
  • Does not substitute for wide reading; vocabulary breadth is built primarily through volume of independent reading over time [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Rich instruction: extended discussion, multiple contexts, word associations (e.g., Beck & McKeown's Text Talk approach) — slower but deeper
  • Brief definitional pre-teaching: quick definitions plus one example — appropriate for words needed only for one reading
  • Multimedia pre-teaching: images, gestures, or video for concrete words, especially for multilingual learners
  • Morphological pre-teaching: teaching root words and affixes that recur across the upcoming text, extending coverage beyond the selected words
  • Full pre-teaching: definition, example, context, and learner-generated sentence before reading — for words central to the text's argument
  • Light-touch preview: brief pronunciation and gloss only — for words needed for surface fluency but inferable in context
  • During-reading substitution: teaching words at the moment they first appear, when the surrounding context supplies meaning
  • Morphological pre-teaching: teaching a root or affix (e.g., bio-, -ology) that unlocks a word family rather than a single word

Target Learners¶

  • Struggling readers and learners with limited background knowledge, for whom unknown words are a primary comprehension barrier [+M]
  • Multilingual learners, when paired with visuals and first-language supports [+M]
  • Less necessary for proficient readers with strong vocabularies, who can infer most meanings from context [~W]
  • Novices in a discipline facing technical terminology (e.g., osmosis, tariff, metaphor) before first exposure to domain texts
  • Less necessary for proficient readers with strong background knowledge, who can infer most word meanings from context [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Comprehension of complex texts where specific words are pivotal
  • Academic language development (Tier Two vocabulary)
  • Background knowledge activation before reading Activation improves learning. [+M]
  • Reading comprehension: ensuring the text itself is understandable on first pass
  • Word knowledge depth: building initial meanings that later encounters consolidate
  • Disciplinary literacy: acquiring the technical lexicon of a field before reading in it

Instructions¶

  1. Select words: choose 5–10 words that are essential to the text, likely unknown, and broadly transferable; cut words learners can infer from context.
  2. Prepare friendly definitions: write conversational explanations with a familiar example for each word.
  3. Open with activation: connect each word to learners' prior knowledge using an Advance Organizer or Analogies.
  4. Teach actively: present the definition, then have learners generate their own examples, judge example/non-example cases, or answer quick "have you ever…" questions.
  5. Reinforce in context: direct attention to the words during the reading or task, and schedule Spaced Retrieval or later review encounters so meanings consolidate.

Related Strategies¶

  • Pre-questioning — a parallel pre-reading activation strategy focused on ideas rather than words
  • Frayer Model — a graphic organizer often used for the active-processing step
  • Morphological Analysis — extends pre-teaching coverage by teaching recurring word parts
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — pre-teaching is vocabulary-specific activation; both prepare the schema the text will require
  • Concrete actions for mathematics educators: focus on ideas over words, engage students in defining early, connect multiple representations, extend to formal definitions, and nurture the genre of definition

Examples¶

  • Text Talk (Beck & McKeown) — kindergarten and primary teachers pre-teach Tier Two words from a read-aloud with rich definitional explanations and child-generated examples, then revisit the words across the week.
  • Word Generation (SERP Institute) — a middle-school academic vocabulary program that introduces a small set of high-utility words before discussion and reading across subject areas. https://serpmedia.org/wg/
  • Newsela / Achieve3000 — digital reading platforms that surface pre-taught key vocabulary with definitions before the article text, then check word knowledge in follow-up quizzes.
  • Beck & McKeown's Text Talk — a published instructional routine in which teachers pre-teach a handful of Tier Two words from a read-aloud, then revisit them in rich discussion afterward.
  • Word Generation (SERP Institute, https://www.wordgeneration.org) — a middle-school program built around a weekly set of high-utility academic words introduced before discussion and writing tasks.
  • Content-area example: before a science passage on ecosystems, a teacher pre-teaches producer, consumer, and decomposer with a quick diagram and a "which one are you at lunch?" prompt, then has students apply the terms while reading.
