# Graphic Organizers for Lecture Support

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Instructors deliver lectures using a graphic organizer — a concept map, matrix, flowchart, or hierarchical diagram — to visually represent the structure of the content as it is presented, rather than relying on bullet-point slides or a linear outline. The organizer is built up during the lecture (or provided partially complete), so students see how each new idea connects to what came before. It functions as both a presentation medium for the instructor and a note-taking scaffold for students.

## Design Implications

Graphic organizers exploit the dual-channel capacity of working memory: presenting relational structure spatially alongside verbal explanation improves recall and inference compared with verbal-only presentation [Presenting information in both verbal and visual form improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]. Meta-analytic evidence shows graphic organizers outperform text-only or outline-based presentation, particularly when they make relationships (hierarchy, causality, comparison) explicit rather than merely decorating the content [Graphic organizers improve learning outcomes compared with text-only instruction.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]. Their benefit depends on the organizer matching the actual structure of the content — a hierarchy for taxonomies, a matrix for comparisons, a flowchart for processes — and on students actively processing it rather than passively receiving it.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Lecture content analyzed in advance to identify its underlying structure (hierarchical, sequential, comparative, causal)
- An organizer type matched to that structure, not chosen for visual appeal
- Progressive reveal or completion during the lecture so the visual grows with the explanation ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) the content into organizer segments)
- Student-facing copies with deliberate blanks, so note-taking requires active processing

#### Constraints
- Instructor preparation cost is high: translating dense content into an accurate visual takes significant time and iteration
- A poorly matched organizer (e.g., a linear flowchart for comparative content) can impose a misleading structure and harm learning [Cognitive overload degrades learning when presentation demands exceed working memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Overly dense organizers with every detail filled in become unreadable and defeat the purpose; students who copy a complete organizer verbatim learn less than those who complete parts of it themselves [Learner-generated graphic organizers produce better learning than provided ones.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [~M]
- Benefits shrink for content that is genuinely linear or procedural, where an outline serves as well

#### Implementation Variability
- **Completed vs. skeletal**: provide the full organizer as a study aid, or a partial version students complete during lecture (the latter produces more engagement)
- **Instructor-built vs. student-built**: students construct their own organizer after or during the lecture, which shifts the strategy toward generative learning
- **Static handout vs. progressive build**: reveal the organizer incrementally (on a whiteboard or animated slide) to control the order of information
- **Organizer as advance organizer**: present the completed structure before the lecture to activate relevant prior knowledge ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))

### Target Learners
- Novices who lack a schema for the content and would otherwise record lecture notes as disconnected facts [Graphic organizers improve learning outcomes compared with text-only instruction.](../claims/advance-organizers-improve-learning.md) [+M]
- Students with weak note-taking skills, who benefit from an external structure for their notes
- Less beneficial for advanced learners who already impose their own structure on content; a provided organizer may be redundant for them [Cognitive overload degrades learning when presentation demands exceed working memory capacity.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Relational knowledge: understanding how concepts connect, not just what they are
- Comprehension and retention of expository lecture content [Presenting information in both verbal and visual form improves recall.](../claims/dual-coding-improves-recall.md) [+S]
- Preparation for later knowledge construction — the organizer becomes a substrate for [Concept Mapping](../elements/concept-mapping.md) or summarization activities

### Instructions
1. Analyze the lecture content and select the organizer type that matches its structure (hierarchy, matrix, flowchart, cycle).
2. Present the skeletal organizer at the start of the lecture as a roadmap ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
3. Build the organizer incrementally as each concept is explained, narrating the relationships aloud ("this branch is a *type of* the one above…").
4. Leave selected nodes or links blank for students to complete, requiring active processing ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
5. Close by having students use the completed organizer to answer an integrative question or predict a related case ([Application](../elements/application.md)).

## Examples
- A biology instructor uses a classification tree on the whiteboard to teach the taxonomy of living organisms, adding each branch as it is discussed and leaving species names blank for students to place.
- A history lecturer uses a comparison matrix (causes, actors, outcomes) filled in across three revolutions, so students see structural parallels rather than three disconnected narratives.
- **Cornell note-taking with a graphic header**: some published course materials (e.g., open-education lecture templates) pair a concept-map header with lined note space, combining relational and detail capture.
