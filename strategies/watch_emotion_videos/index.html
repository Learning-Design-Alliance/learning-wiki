Watch Emotion Videos¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 against) · 2 studies (1 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 1 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

The teacher shares a short video that names and explains "big emotions" (anger, anxiety, frustration, excitement) and models simple regulation strategies. Video serves as a Demonstration of emotional vocabulary and coping steps, followed by a structured debrief in which students connect the content to their own emotional experiences and rehearse the regulation steps.

Design Implications¶

Video is effective here because it combines visual and verbal channels to convey both emotion concepts and modeled coping behavior [~M], consistent with dual coding of emotional content (see Dual Coding Theory). Prosocial and socio-emotional media content reliably increases children's prosocial behavior and emotional understanding when the target skill is clearly depicted [~M]. However, watching alone is insufficient: learning depends on the debrief and on guided application, since passive viewing without prompts produces shallow engagement and illusions of understanding [-M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Access to video playback and time for a debrief discussion
  • A video whose emotion and regulation strategy are explicit and age-appropriate — not merely entertaining
  • A debrief structure that asks students to identify the emotion, name the strategy shown, and connect it to their own experience (Class Discussion)
  • A follow-up application moment, such as a Check-In where students use the strategy during the day

Constraints¶

  • Poorly chosen videos (fast-paced, high sensory intensity, or ambiguous content) can dysregulate rather than teach; some students need alternative sensory activities [-M]
  • Videos that depict emotions without showing regulation steps model arousal without coping [-M]
  • Watching without debrief does not produce transfer to real emotional situations [-M]
  • Cognitive overload from dense narration or multiple simultaneous strategies degrades learning, especially for young children Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Grade-level matching: simpler, single-emotion videos for K–2; videos with more complex social situations for grades 3–5
  • Pause-and-predict: stop the video at emotional peaks and ask students to name the feeling and predict a strategy before it is shown
  • Repeated viewing of the same short video across a unit builds fluency with the vocabulary and steps
  • Students can later create their own emotion videos, shifting from Demonstration to Act It Out

Target Learners¶

  • K–5 learners, who benefit from concrete, visual models of internal states they cannot yet name [~M]
  • Students with limited emotional vocabulary or with autism/ADHD who benefit from explicit, repeatable modeling
  • Less effective for adolescents without adaptation; older students often find cartoon-style emotion videos patronizing [-W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Emotional literacy: identifying and naming emotions in self and others
  • Procedural knowledge of regulation: knowing the steps of a coping strategy (e.g., stop–breathe–name the feeling–choose an action)
  • Attitude and empathy goals: normalizing big emotions and building perspective-taking (see Building Empathy)

Instructions¶

  1. Preview and select a short (2–5 minute) video that names one emotion and demonstrates one clear regulation strategy.
  2. Activate prior experience: ask students to recall a time they felt the target emotion (Activation).
  3. Play the video, pausing at key moments to ask "What is this character feeling? What could they do?"
  4. Debrief with Class Discussion: name the emotion, restate the strategy steps, and connect to students' own lives.
  5. Rehearse the steps as a class (Act It Out), then post the steps as a visual reference.
  6. Embed a Check-In later the same day asking students to try the strategy and report how it went.

Related Strategies¶

  • 'What's My Emotion?' Game Check-In — a follow-up activity that rehearses emotion naming after viewing
  • Acting-Role-Play — live enactment of the regulation steps the video modeled

Related Elements¶

  • Demonstration — the video functions as a model of emotion and coping behavior
  • Check-In — the application moment that converts viewing into practice
  • Class Discussion — the debrief structure that makes viewing active

Examples¶

  • Daniel Tiger's Neighborhood (PBS) — each episode includes an explicit strategy song (e.g., "When you feel so mad that you want to roar, take a deep breath and count to four"); research links active mediation of this content to preschoolers' use of the strategies.
  • Sesame Street emotional well-being segments — short clips modeling naming feelings and calming strategies, with printable debrief guides for teachers.
  • Classroom routine: watch a 3-minute clip on managing frustration, debrief the steps, then use the same steps during a subsequent Check-In before a challenging task.
