# Offering Multiple Options for Family Engagement

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Offering multiple options for family engagement means designing a portfolio of participation pathways — school-based events, home visits, take-home materials that guide learning support, technology-mediated communication, peer-to-peer family networks, and community partnerships — rather than a single canonical mode of involvement. The strategy recognizes that families differ in work schedules, language, transportation, digital access, and cultural norms around school participation, so no single channel reaches everyone. It is carried out by auditing barriers, mapping options against them, and coordinating the options so families can move among them as circumstances change.

## Design Implications

Family engagement functions like a motivational and access problem: participation rises when families perceive invitation, efficacy, and feasible opportunity [~M]. Hoover-Dempsey and Sandler's model locates engagement decisions in families' role construction and sense of efficacy for helping — both of which are shaped by how schools invite and equip families, not just by how often they are asked [Hoover-Dempsey & Sandler model of the parental involvement process.](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220679509594755) [+M]. Multiple options also build social capital: connecting families to one another and to community organizations increases engagement through network effects, not only through direct school outreach [Parents' social networks predict involvement beyond individual characteristics.](https://doi.org/10.1177/000271620204583101) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A barrier audit of the school community (schedules, language, transportation, digital access, caregiving responsibilities) before selecting options
- Multiple genuinely distinct channels: in-person events at varied times, home visits, materials guiding at-home learning support, and technology-based communication
- Bilingual and culturally responsive communication; translated materials alone are insufficient without relationship-building
- Partnerships with local organizations that can host skill-building and leadership development for family members
- A coordination structure (e.g., a family engagement team) so options are managed rather than ad hoc

#### Constraints
- Offering options without removing underlying barriers (work schedules, childcare, language) produces nominal rather than substantive choice; participation gains concentrate among already-engaged families [-M]
- Technology-mediated options exclude families without reliable devices or connectivity, and can widen gaps if treated as the primary channel [-M]
- Home visits require training, time, and trust-building; poorly resourced visit programs can feel intrusive and damage relationships [-W]
- Coordination costs are real: a broad portfolio with weak follow-through signals tokenism and depresses trust [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Tiered models: universal options (newsletters, flexible conferences) plus targeted options (home visits, family workshops) plus intensive options (family leadership development)
- Dual-generation approaches where family members build their own skills (ESL, digital literacy) alongside supporting children's learning
- Community-based engagement, where events happen in trusted neighborhood settings rather than the school building
- Technology as one channel among several: two-way messaging apps (e.g., TalkingPoints, ClassDojo) with SMS fallback for families without smartphones

### Target Learners
- Families facing significant barriers to engagement — shift workers, families with limited English proficiency, families with negative prior school experiences — who are excluded by single-channel approaches [~M]
- Families new to a school community, for whom varied low-stakes entry points lower the cost of first contact
- Family members developing leadership capacity through peer networks and community partnerships [Parents' social networks predict involvement beyond individual characteristics.](https://doi.org/10.1177/000271620204583101) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Increased and more equitable family participation in children's education
- Family efficacy for supporting learning at home
- Family-school partnership quality and mutual trust
- Family leadership development for school and community governance

### Instructions
1. Audit barriers and assets in the school community through surveys, conversations, and existing attendance data; identify which families are currently unreached.
2. Design a portfolio of options spanning school-based, home-based, technology-mediated, and community-based channels, each mapped to a specific barrier.
3. Prepare staff and materials: train teachers for home visits and relationship-based outreach, translate and culturally adapt communications, and set up two-way communication tools.
4. Connect families to one another through peer networks, buddy systems, or family advisory groups so support does not depend solely on school staff.
5. Partner with local organizations to offer family members skill-building and leadership development opportunities.
6. Track participation by option and by family subgroup, and adjust the portfolio annually — retire low-uptake options and expand those reaching previously unreached families.

## Related Strategies
- Home Visits — an intensive, relationship-first engagement option within the portfolio
- Family Workshops on Learning at Home — equips family members with concrete guidance for supporting children's learning
- Community Partnerships for Family Leadership — extends engagement into civic capacity-building

## Examples
- **Epstein's Six Types of Involvement framework (Johns Hopkins NNPS, [National Network of Partnership Schools](https://nnps.jhucsos.com))** — organizes engagement into parenting, communicating, volunteering, learning at home, decision-making, and collaborating with the community; schools use it to build a written partnership plan with multiple option types.
- **Parent Teacher Home Visits ([pthvp.org](https://www.pthvp.org))** — a national home-visit model built on relational, voluntary visits in which educators visit families on neutral ground, used across hundreds of districts.
- **TalkingPoints ([talkingpts.org](https://talkingpts.org))** — a two-way family communication platform with translation into 100+ languages and SMS fallback, allowing engagement without smartphone or English proficiency requirements.
- **Dual-generation family academies** — district-run programs (e.g., family leadership institutes such as the National Parent Leadership Institute, [parentleadership.org](https://www.parentleadership.org)) that combine children's learning support with adult skill-building and civic leadership training.
