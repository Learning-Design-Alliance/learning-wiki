# Mapping Online Reading Behavior

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners start with a research question and document every step and detour they take while reading online — sites visited, links followed, searches issued, and sources abandoned — using drawing, mind-mapping software, or screenshots. They then share their maps and reflect on their decision-making at each node: why they followed a link, why they left a page, and how they judged a source's credibility. The map externalizes an otherwise invisible reading process so it can be examined, compared, and improved.

## Design Implications

Mapping converts the fast, largely automatic decisions of online reading into objects of reflection, which is the mechanism by which metacognitive awareness grows [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]. Because skilled online reading involves evaluating source reliability while navigating, the mapping-and-reflection cycle also builds critical evaluation of information — a core component of multiple-source comprehension [~M]. The strategy works best when reflection is structured (prompts, comparison with peers) rather than left open-ended.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to online reading materials and a mapping tool (mind-mapping software, drawing tools, or paper and pencil)
- A genuine research question with multiple plausible paths and sources
- Structured reflection prompts targeting decision points (Why this link? Why leave this page? How did you judge this source?)
- A sharing or comparison phase so learners see alternative navigation paths for the same question

#### Constraints
- Learners often cannot accurately recall their navigation after the fact [-W]; concurrent documentation (screenshots, browser history review) is more reliable than retrospective reconstruction
- The activity is time-consuming, and learners prone to wandering off-topic may produce maps of drift rather than purposeful search [-W]
- Mapping itself adds extraneous load for novices juggling reading, navigation, and documentation simultaneously [~M]; modeling the mapping first reduces this burden
- If reflection prompts focus only on *what* was visited rather than *why*, maps become logs with little learning value [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Concurrent vs. retrospective mapping:** learners annotate as they read (more accurate) or reconstruct afterward from browser history (less disruptive to reading)
- **Individual vs. comparative:** maps can be private reflections or compared across peers to reveal that different paths yield different source quality
- **Tool variation:** screenshots, concept maps, flowcharts, or annotated browser-history timelines all work; the format matters less than the decision-point prompts

### Target Learners
- Middle school, high school, and adult learners developing online research skills
- Novice online readers who navigate impulsively, following the first plausible link without evaluating it [~M]
- Less beneficial for expert readers, whose navigation is already strategic and for whom mapping adds overhead without new insight [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive awareness of one's own online reading and search processes [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation.](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]
- Critical evaluation of information sources and multiple-document comprehension
- Self-regulated search strategy: planning, monitoring, and revising navigation in digital environments

### Instructions
1. Pose an open research question and have learners plan an initial search approach before going online.
2. Learners read and navigate, documenting each site, link, and search in a running map — screenshots or browser-history review make the record accurate.
3. At each node, learners annotate the decision: why they followed the link, stayed, or left.
4. Learners share maps in [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or small groups, comparing paths to the same question and discussing which decisions led to credible, relevant sources.
5. Learners write a short reflection synthesizing what their map reveals about their habits and what they will change — a form of [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) that consolidates the metacognitive insight.

## Related Strategies
- **Source evaluation checklists** — complements mapping by giving learners explicit criteria to apply at each decision point
- **Search strategy instruction** — mapping reveals the search behaviors that explicit strategy instruction then targets
- **Reading logs** — the print-era analogue; mapping extends logging to navigation decisions, not just content

## Examples
- A middle-school media literacy unit in which students map their path while researching a controversial topic, then compare maps to see how quickly different students landed on low-credibility sites.
- An undergraduate research-skills course where students reconstruct their navigation from browser history and annotate credibility judgments at each source, following the multiple-source evaluation model studied by Brand-Gruwel and colleagues.
- **News Literacy Project** (https://newslit.org) classroom activities that ask students to trace and document how they encountered and verified a claim online.
