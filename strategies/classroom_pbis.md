# Classroom PBIS

## Description
Classroom Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS) is the classroom-level application of a multi-tiered behavior framework: teachers define 3–5 positively stated behavioral expectations, teach them explicitly like academic content, acknowledge them consistently with specific reinforcement, and respond to problem behavior with predictable, instructional consequences. Behavior data (e.g., office discipline referrals) is collected and reviewed to adjust supports.

## Design Implications

Classroom PBIS treats behavior as a learnable skill set rather than a trait, applying the same instructional logic to conduct as to content — modeling, practice, and feedback. Its effectiveness rests on consistency and positive ratio: reinforcement of expected behavior must substantially outnumber corrective responses, and expectations must be operationalized in context-specific routines [~S]. Because it is rooted in operant principles, its design overlaps heavily with [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md), while its emphasis on teaching expectations connects to [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) applied to social behavior.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A small set (3–5) of positively stated, school-aligned expectations (e.g., "Be respectful"), translated into concrete behavioral examples for each classroom routine
- An explicit teaching phase: expectations are modeled, rehearsed, and reviewed like any skill ([Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) of expected behavior, then student practice)
- A low-effort, high-frequency acknowledgment system (specific praise, tokens, or points) with a stated positive-to-corrective ratio (commonly 4:1 or higher)
- A continuum of predictable, instructional responses to problem behavior that preserve instructional time
- Regular data review (referrals, acknowledgment counts) to identify students needing Tier 2/3 supports

#### Constraints
- Token or point systems can undermine intrinsic motivation when rewards feel controlling rather than informational [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation; externally controlled reward contingencies can crowd it out.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [~M] — rewards framed as feedback ("I noticed you helped a classmate") fare better than contingent payment for compliance
- Inconsistent implementation across staff erodes effects; a system reinforced by one teacher but ignored by others teaches learners that expectations are situational [-M]
- Punitive or exclusionary responses (removal, public reprimand) disproportionately harm students with disabilities and students of color and do not teach replacement behavior [-S]
- Effects fade when acknowledgment systems are dropped abruptly once "behavior is good" — maintenance requires planned thinning of reinforcement, not removal

#### Implementation Variability
- Tier 1 (universal): expectations taught to all students, reinforcement for all
- Tier 2: targeted supports such as Check-In/Check-Out (CICO), a daily adult mentor and point card
- Tier 3: individualized function-based behavior support plans
- Classroom adaptations range from paper token economies to digital tools (ClassDojo, LiveSchool); digital points systems add data but risk becoming public leaderboards that shame low earners

### Target Learners
- All students in a classroom benefit from predictable structure and explicitly taught routines [+S]
- Students with externalizing behavior or weak self-regulation benefit most from Tier 2 intensifications such as CICO [+M]
- Relationship-focused components matter for disengaged students: brief empathic discipline conversations reduce suspensions for defiant behavior [Brief empathic discipline interventions reduce suspensions among middle-school students.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]
- Students whose sense of belonging is fragile are harmed by public point displays; private acknowledgment is safer [Belonging interventions improve outcomes for marginalized students.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Procedural fluency in classroom routines (transitions, group work, independent work)
- Social-emotional skills: self-regulation, cooperation, help-seeking
- Academic engaged time — the mechanism by which behavior supports improve achievement

### Instructions
1. Define 3–5 positively stated expectations and translate each into 2–3 observable behaviors per major routine.
2. Teach expectations explicitly: [Demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md) by the teacher, non-examples, then student [Practice](../elements/practice.md) and rehearsal during the first weeks and after breaks.
3. Establish an acknowledgment system with specific, labeled praise ("You started work within 30 seconds") delivered at a high positive ratio.
4. Respond to problem behavior with brief, calm, instructional consequences; reserve relationship-repair conversations for private moments.
5. Collect acknowledgment and referral data; review every 4–6 weeks and intensify supports (CICO, individual plans) for students not responding.

## Related Strategies
- Classroom management systems that share PBIS's structure but lack its teaching and data components tend to be compliance-only; PBIS's distinctive move is treating expectations as curriculum
- Function-based thinking — identifying what problem behavior achieves for the student (escape, attention) before selecting a response

## Examples
- **[PBIS Technical Guide](https://www.pbis.org)** — the national PBIS implementation center; provides classroom matrix templates, CICO materials, and fidelity measures (Self-Assessment Survey, TFI).
- **Check-In/Check-Out (CICO)** — a widely studied Tier 2 package: a morning check-in with a mentor, a daily point card rating expectations each period, and an end-of-day check-out with take-home feedback.
- **ClassDojo** — a digital points platform used in many PBIS classrooms; effective when points are private and paired with specific feedback, counterproductive as a public leaderboard.
