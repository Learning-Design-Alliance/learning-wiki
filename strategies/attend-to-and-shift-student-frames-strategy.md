# Attend to students' epistemic frames during problem solving and deliberately shift or follow them to keep students productive

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 6 claims (5 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (2 qualitative, 1 review, 1 associational), `q2` · 0 of 4 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article recommends that instructors use the four-frame framework to notice how students are solving problems and then either help them shift frames or support their current one. As the authors put it, "helping them shift their frame to a conceptual one might help them become productive" when students grind through tedious algebra, and attending to framing can help stuck students become unstuck. Joining students in their existing frame (follow-the-leader) can check in with them, collaboratively nudge them along, and implicitly support their self-efficacy and agency as learners and problem solvers.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Instructors must be able to determine what frames students are in, which the article says requires examining students' discourse including language, tone, and prosody, so video-based or close observational data is preferred
#### Constraints
- The article does not prescribe an a priori sequence of frames; which frames are productive must be an interaction between the students and the problem at hand, and the reflection stage of prescriptive rubrics is often treated algorithmically (units match, answer on the key) rather than conceptually

### Target Learners
- upper-division physics students working in small groups on math-rich problems

### Target Learning Goals
- productive problem solving
- coordinating math and physics ideas
- self-efficacy and agency as problem solvers
