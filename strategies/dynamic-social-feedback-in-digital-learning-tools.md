# Implement dynamic social feedback in digital learning tools to potentially increase motivation and learning effort

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 2 studies (2 causal), `q3` · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 3 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article proposes that anticipating positive and negative social feedback in digital learning tools could raise task motivation. As the authors write, "digital learning tools are increasingly used and implementing dynamic social feedback could potentially increase the motivation to perform well on a task." They further suggest the "expectation of a pleasurable learning experience via dynamic social feedback could increase learning effort such as frequency or duration of interaction with the learning material," potentially enhancing learning success. The strategy extends the SID paradigm's motivational findings to educational app contexts such as vocabulary study.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Feedback must be arranged so learners can obtain positive feedback (e.g., for fast or correct responses) and avoid negative feedback, as in the SID task.
#### Constraints
- The article's own data showed no additional RT benefit of dynamic video over static photo feedback; it was anticipating valent feedback that sped responses.
- Adult education applications require attention to possible age-related changes in socio-emotional processing.

### Target Learners
- Young adult learners (18–35 years)
- Older adult learners (50–84 years)

### Target Learning Goals
- Increased motivation and learning effort (e.g., frequency or duration of interaction with learning material such as vocabulary study apps)

## Claims

- [Anticipating positive and negative social feedback accelerates response times in young and older adults, with no additional effect of dynamic video over static photo feedback](../claims/social-feedback-anticipation-accelerates-rt-across-age.md) [+W]
- [Cues predicting positive social feedback acquire positive implicit valence (evaluative learning) in both young and older adults, while negative cues do not differ from neutral](../claims/evaluative-learning-positive-social-feedback-cues.md) [+W]
- [Feedback valence had no significant direct effect on willingness to invest effort in four experiments, but had significant indirect effects via threat (all four) and via challenge (Experiments 1 and 2) pointing in opposite directions](../claims/feedback-willingness-indirect-challenge-threat.md) [~W]
