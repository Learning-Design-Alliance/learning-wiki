# Web-Based Dictionary & Thesaurus

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy integrates web-based dictionaries (e.g., Merriam-Webster, Wiktionary) and thesauruses (e.g., Thesaurus.com, WordHippo) into reading, writing, and vocabulary instruction so learners can resolve word meanings and find synonyms on demand. Unlike print references, digital tools offer instant search, audio pronunciation, embedded example sentences, and hyperlinked cross-references that support rapid, low-cost lookup during authentic reading and writing tasks.

## Design Implications

Lookups are most valuable when they occur during meaningful reading or writing rather than as isolated drill, because encountering a word in context while resolving its meaning contributes to incidental vocabulary acquisition [Knight, 1994] [+M]. However, lookup alone produces fragile word knowledge; words need multiple encounters and generative use to be retained [Nation, 2001] [+S]. Design the task so the dictionary serves comprehension and production, then require learners to *use* the word — the "involvement load" of a task (need, search, evaluation) predicts how much vocabulary sticks [Laufer & Hulstijn, 2001] [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Reliable access to a quality reference tool with learner-appropriate definitions (learner dictionaries such as the [Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English](https://www.ldoceonline.com) or [Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org) define words within a controlled defining vocabulary, supporting [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md))
- A reading or writing task in which the target words actually matter to the learner's goal
- Follow-up activities requiring generative use: writing original sentences, choosing among synonyms for register, or explaining the word in one's own words
- Brief instruction in lookup strategy itself — reading the whole entry, checking example sentences, comparing thesaurus options for connotation rather than taking the first synonym

#### Constraints
- Copying the first definition or synonym without evaluating fit produces word choices that are technically synonymous but contextually wrong [-M] — thesaurus use without instruction on connotation and register frequently degrades writing quality
- For beginning readers, decoding the definition can exceed the difficulty of decoding the target word; definitions written outside a controlled vocabulary impose extraneous load [~M]
- Over-reliance on lookup can short-circuit the effortful inference-from-context that itself builds word knowledge; tasks that require guessing before confirming produce stronger retention than immediate lookup [~S]
- Frequent interruptions for lookup disrupt reading flow and comprehension of the passage as a whole, especially for learners with small vocabularies [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Pre-teaching vs. lookup:** pre-teach high-frequency, barrier words; leave lower-frequency words to dictionary resolution
- **Embedded tools:** e-reader and browser extensions (e.g., Kindle's tap-to-lookup, Lingro for web pages) reduce the interruption cost of switching contexts
- **Bilingual vs. monolingual:** bilingual dictionaries speed comprehension for lower-proficiency learners; monolingual learner dictionaries better support depth of word knowledge as proficiency grows
- **Thesaurus as writing tool:** pair synonym search with a register check ("Is *mitigate* right in a text message?") to make the tool teach connotation, not just substitution

### Target Learners
- Intermediate readers and language learners who can decode definitions but lack breadth of vocabulary [+M]
- Writers seeking precision and variety beyond their productive vocabulary
- Less suitable for absolute beginners, who need [Automatic Word Recognition](../claims/automatic-word-recognition-frees-resources-for-comprehension.md) built through direct teaching before lookup becomes efficient [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Receptive vocabulary growth during independent reading
- Productive vocabulary: choosing words with appropriate connotation, register, and collocation
- Word consciousness: noticing morphology, etymology, and relationships among words via hyperlinked entries

### Instructions
1. Select a text or writing task containing a manageable number of unknown words; pre-teach only the words essential to comprehension.
2. Model a lookup aloud: read the whole entry, examine the example sentence, and compare two thesaurus candidates for connotation before choosing.
3. Have learners read, looking up only words that block understanding; require a guess at meaning from context *before* confirming with the dictionary.
4. Require generative use: each learner writes an original sentence or short passage using two looked-up words, choosing synonyms deliberately.
5. Consolidate with spaced review — a personal word list, flashcards, or a class shared glossary — since single encounters rarely produce durable learning [Nation, 2001] [+S].

## Examples
- **[Vocabulary.com](https://www.vocabulary.com)** — dictionary entries with adaptive quizzing; lookups feed a spaced-repetition practice loop, addressing the retention problem of one-off lookups.
- **[Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com)** — audio pronunciation, example sentences, and a learner-friendly thesaurus with usage notes distinguishing near-synonyms.
- **[WordHippo](https://www.wordhippo.com)** — combined dictionary/thesaurus with filters for formality and length, useful for teaching register in writing instruction.
- **E-reader lookup (Kindle, Apple Books)** — tap-to-define during reading with automatic vocabulary lists, minimizing the interruption cost of mid-reading lookup.
