# A Finder's Guide to Facts

## Description
A Finder's Guide to Facts is a short checklist of questions readers apply to a news story to judge its credibility: Is the story outrageous? Does the headline match the article body? Are quotes presented in context? Does the story attack a generic enemy? Who is the source, and what is their track record? The strategy converts expert habits of skepticism into an explicit, teachable routine that novices can apply before trusting or amplifying a story.

## Design Implications

The guide works by interrupting the automatic acceptance response: readers who pause to apply structured questions show better discrimination between accurate and fabricated content than readers relying on intuition alone [~M]. Its questions target known markers of misinformation — emotional provocation, headline–body mismatch, decontextualized quotation, and out-group scapegoating — so learners practice *discrimination* rather than blanket distrust. Because the checklist is procedural, it benefits from [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with contrasting real and fake stories, and from [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) as learners justify their credibility judgments [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to real news stories, including fabricated or manipulated examples for contrast
- A printed or digital version of the question set, applied consistently across stories
- Time for learners to justify judgments aloud or in writing, not just answer yes/no
- Instructor familiarity with the source-evaluation question so they can model it ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))

#### Constraints
- Checklist use without feedback can produce overconfidence: learners who apply surface questions may still miss sophisticated fabrications that pass every heuristic [-M]
- Less effective when learners lack background knowledge about news production (sources, wire services, editorial standards); the "who is the source?" question is inert without that schema [~M]
- Applying the guide only to stories one already dislikes risks motivated reasoning — the checklist becomes a weapon for dismissing inconvenient facts rather than a genuine test [-M]
- Heuristic questions alone underperform expert strategies such as lateral reading (leaving the page to check what other sources say about the source) [~S]

#### Implementation Variability
- As a whole-class routine: teacher projects a story, class applies each question, then compares judgments
- As an individual annotation task: learners mark evidence for each question directly on the article ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- As a game or sorting activity: learners rank a mixed set of stories by credibility and defend the ranking
- Extended into lateral reading: after the checklist, learners open new tabs to investigate the source itself

### Target Learners
- Secondary and post-secondary students encountering news primarily through social media feeds, where headline-only exposure is common
- Adult learners in media literacy and civics courses; the questions are accessible without specialized domain knowledge
- Weakest fit for learners who already possess strong source-evaluation schemas, for whom the checklist adds little beyond what they do spontaneously [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical evaluation: judging the credibility and bias of information sources
- Media literacy: recognizing structural markers of fabricated or manipulative reporting
- Dispositional goals: building a habit of verification before sharing

### Instructions
1. Introduce the question set and model it on one story, thinking aloud through each question ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md)).
2. Present a second story; learners apply the questions individually, annotating the text ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md)).
3. Pair learners to compare judgments and resolve disagreements through [Argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md) grounded in the checklist criteria.
4. Debrief with the full class ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)), surfacing cases where the heuristics were ambiguous or failed.
5. Follow with a contrasting-cases activity: real, satirical, and fabricated stories sorted side by side, so learners abstract the discriminating features [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M].

## Examples
- **NPR** — NPR's education team published the Finder's Guide to Facts as a classroom resource for evaluating news stories ([npr.org](https://www.npr.org)).
- **Stanford History Education Group — Civic Online Reasoning curriculum** — free lessons and assessments in which students evaluate news and social media sources; its "lateral reading" tasks extend the checklist into active source investigation ([cor.stanford.edu](https://cor.stanford.edu)).
- **News Literacy Project — Checkology®** — a platform with interactive lessons on source credibility, misinformation markers, and verification habits ([checkology.org](https://checkology.org)).
