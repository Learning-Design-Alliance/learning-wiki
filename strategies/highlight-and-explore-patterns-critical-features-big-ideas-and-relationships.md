# Highlight and Explore Patterns, Critical Features, Big Ideas, and Relationships

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (3 for, 1 mixed, 1 against) · 10 studies (5 causal, 3 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 2 of 10 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
This strategy directs learner attention to the features, patterns, and organizing ideas that matter most for the learning goal, and makes relationships between concepts explicit. It combines *signaling* (visual or verbal cues that mark critical information) with *guided exploration* (activities in which learners compare cases, trace patterns, and articulate how ideas connect). The aim is coherent schema construction rather than accumulation of isolated facts.

## Design Implications

Attention is the scarcest resource in learning: learners attend to what is salient, not necessarily to what is important. Explicit cues that mark critical features improve selective attention and learning, especially when material is complex or the goal is not obvious [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention to cued information.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]. But highlighting alone is passive; the exploration half of the strategy — comparing multiple cases that vary systematically — is what drives abstraction of the underlying pattern [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of deep features.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]. Signaling also manages extraneous load by reducing the need to search for structure [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear analysis of which features are *critical* for the learning goal — cueing everything cues nothing
- Contrast sets or case sequences in which relevant features vary and irrelevant ones are held constant or systematically varied
- Prompts that require learners to articulate the pattern or relationship ([Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md), [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
- Visual design that distinguishes emphasis from decoration (consistent color-coding, [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md))
- A clear analysis of which features, patterns, and relationships are actually critical to the learning goal — highlighting the wrong things is worse than highlighting nothing
- Visual or verbal signaling devices (headings, color, underlining, advance organizers) applied sparingly and consistently
- Comparison tasks or prompts that require learners to examine *how* cases differ and what stays invariant

#### Constraints
- Learner-generated highlighting is one of the least effective study techniques when used without training or prompts [Experimenter-provided underlining is more effective than student underlining.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [-M] — students highlight indiscriminately and mistake marking for knowing
- Adding interesting but irrelevant "seductive details" diverts attention from critical content and depresses retention [-S]
- Heavy cueing can backfire for learners with high prior knowledge, who process well-structured material fine without it [Guidance becomes less effective as expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Cueing a single exemplar risks anchoring learners to surface features; patterns only emerge from [Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md) or [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) [~M]
- Signaling loses its power when everything is emphasized; the effect depends on genuine selectivity
- Highlighting surface features without connecting them to a big idea can encourage rote pattern-matching rather than transfer
- For learners with strong prior knowledge, heavy signaling can be redundant and may interfere with self-regulated processing [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- Instructor-provided signals (color, arrows, bolding, advance organizers) vs. structured learner discovery (sorting tasks, case comparison, concept mapping)
- Domain-specific forms: marking tense endings in language study, annotating proof structure in mathematics, contrasting worked examples with [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md), concept maps in biology
- Timing: cues before study (as an advance organizer), during (embedded annotation), or after (summary and synthesis)
- **Instructor-provided signaling**: advance organizers, bolded critical features, annotated worked examples
- **Contrastive exploration**: presenting non-examples and near-misses so learners must discriminate the critical feature
- **Learner-guided exploration**: pattern-hunting tasks, sorting/classifying activities, "what changed, what stayed the same" comparisons
- **Relational mapping**: concept maps, analogies, and explicit "this is like X because…" prompts

### Target Learners
- Novices, who cannot yet distinguish critical from incidental features on their own [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention to cued information.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]
- Learners facing high-element-interactivity material where structure is not self-evident [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Less cueing for advanced learners, who may find it redundant or attention-splitting [Guidance becomes less effective as expertise increases.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- Learners in complex, feature-rich domains (science, mathematics, medicine) where surface features mislead
- Less beneficial for experts, who already extract structure efficiently and may find signaling redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Concept formation: discriminating defining features from surface features
- Knowledge organization: building relational schemas and seeing "big ideas" that unify topics
- Transfer: abstracting deep structure that applies across contexts [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of deep features.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Conceptual understanding: identifying defining features and distinguishing them from surface features
- Schema formation and abstraction: seeing the big idea across varied instances
- Transfer: recognizing when a familiar pattern applies in a new context

### Instructions
1. Identify the 2–4 critical features or big ideas the goal depends on; cut everything else from the cueing layer.
2. Open with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) that names the pattern learners should watch for.
3. Present a contrast set — cases that vary the critical feature — rather than a single example ([Comparing Cases](../elements/comparing-cases.md), [Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md)).
4. Signal critical features consistently (color-coding, annotation, [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) that map new structure onto familiar structure).
5. Prompt learners to state the pattern and how cases relate ([Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md)); have them generate their own example or non-example to check that the abstraction stuck.

## Examples
- **[Project Gutenberg-annotated close reading]** — no; a stronger published example: **Facing History and Ourselves** (https://www.facinghistory.org) uses guided annotation and contrast cases to highlight patterns in historical evidence.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — color-codes parts of expressions and graphs to mark the critical feature being discussed, then follows with practice on varied items.
- **Concept-oriented physics curricula such as *Physics by Inquiry* (McDermott)** — students compare contrasting experiments to abstract the invariant relationship, with instructors highlighting the critical variable.
- **Mathematics worked-example pairs** — side-by-side problems differing only in the critical feature, with the differing step highlighted, prompting learners to articulate the rule governing when each method applies.
- **[Contrasting Cases (ThinkerTools)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1003424819564)** — Schwartz & Bransford's "A Time for Telling" studies had students compare paired motion-graph cases before instruction, letting them discover the critical feature (slope) that the subsequent lecture then named.
- **[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — worked-example videos use color and annotation to single out the critical step in each problem, then follow with practice on varied surface features of the same underlying pattern.
- **Cognitive Tutor / [Carnegie Learning MATHia](https://www.carnegielearning.com)** — the tutor highlights the relevant quantities in each problem context, teaching students to attend to the structural features that drive the solution rather than the story details.

# Highlighting and Exploration of Patterns, Critical Features, Big Ideas, and Relationships

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
This strategy directs learner attention to what matters most in a task, text, or domain — critical features, recurring patterns, underlying big ideas, and the relationships among them — rather than leaving learners to discover salience on their own. It is carried out through two complementary moves: *highlighting* (signaling, underlining, advance organizers, contrastive framing) and *exploration* (guided comparison of cases, pattern-finding prompts, self-explanation questions that push learners to articulate the structure themselves).

## Design Implications

Novices cannot reliably distinguish deep structure from surface features, so unguided attention is often spent on salient-but-irrelevant details. Explicit signaling reduces extraneous processing by cueing what to attend to [Relevant emphasis directs attention and improves learning of the emphasized content.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M], and pre-organizing structures help learners map new information onto a coherent schema. Exploration matters as much as highlighting: comparing multiple contrasting cases before instruction helps learners abstract the invariant features that define a concept [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of deep structure.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M], and prompting learners to explain patterns to themselves consolidates conceptual understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear analysis of which features, patterns, and relationships are actually critical to the learning goal — highlighting the wrong things is worse than highlighting nothing

- Comparison tasks or prompts that require learners to examine *how* cases differ and what stays invariant
- Follow-up questions or [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) prompts that make the pattern explicit in the learner's own words

#### Constraints
- Learner-generated highlighting is often ineffective: students highlight too much and indiscriminately, with weak evidence of benefit [Experimenter-provided underlining is more effective than student self-underlining.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~M] — over-highlighting creates an illusion of mastery without encoding

#### Implementation Variability

- **Contrastive exploration**: presenting non-examples and near-misses so learners must discriminate the critical feature
- **Learner-guided exploration**: pattern-hunting tasks, sorting/classifying activities, "what changed, what stayed the same" comparisons
- **Relational mapping**: concept maps, analogies, and explicit "this is like X because…" prompts

- Novices, who lack the knowledge to detect deep structure unaided and benefit most from external signaling [Relevant emphasis directs attention and improves learning of the emphasized content.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]
- Learners in complex, feature-rich domains (science, mathematics, medicine) where surface features mislead
- Less beneficial for experts, who already extract structure efficiently and may find signaling redundant [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: identifying defining features and distinguishing them from surface features
- Schema formation and abstraction: seeing the big idea across varied instances
- Transfer: recognizing when a familiar pattern applies in a new context

### Instructions
1. Identify the critical features, patterns, and big ideas of the target content through task analysis before designing anything.
2. Open with an [Advance Organizer](../elements/advance-organizers.md) that frames the big idea and the relationships learners should watch for.
3. Present 2–3 contrasting cases side by side and ask learners what varies and what stays constant [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction of deep structure.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+M]; include [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) to sharpen discrimination.
4. Signal critical features in materials — selective underlining, color, headings — rather than asking learners to highlight on their own [Experimenter-provided underlining is more effective than student self-underlining.](../claims/experimenter-underlining-effective-as-student-underlining.md) [~M].
5. Prompt [Self-Explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md): "Why does this work here? Where would it fail?"
6. Connect the pattern back to the big idea explicitly, using [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) where a familiar domain shares the same structure.
