Questioning Techniques¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 mixed) · 17 studies (9 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 3 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 7 of 17 report an effect size

Description¶

Questioning techniques are deliberate patterns of asking — before, during, and after instruction — designed to elicit learner thinking rather than simply check recall. They range from activating questions posed before new content, to Socratic probing during discussion, to self-explanation prompts learners apply to their own work. The technique lies less in the questions themselves than in their timing, cognitive demand, and the response conditions (wait time, follow-up probes) that surround them.

Design Implications¶

Effective questioning converts passive reception into generative processing: answering a question requires retrieving, organizing, or elaborating knowledge rather than recognizing it Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone. [+S]. Questions also function as formative assessment — the answers reveal misconceptions the instructor can act on in real time Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S]. The cognitive demand of the question matters: low-level recall questions produce shallow processing, while questions requiring explanation, prediction, or comparison drive deeper construction of understanding Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Questions sequenced to build: activation before content, probing during, consolidation after (Activation)
  • Sufficient wait time (typically 3+ seconds) after asking, so learners can compose substantive answers
  • A response structure that distributes participation beyond volunteers (cold call, think-pair-share, response systems)
  • Follow-up probes ("Why?", "How does that compare to…?") that press for reasoning, not just answers

Constraints¶

  • Default wait time in classrooms is often under one second, which reliably reduces the length and quality of student responses Increasing wait time improves the quality of student responses. [+S]
  • Rapid-fire low-level recall questioning can feel punitive and suppress participation, particularly for anxious learners [~M]
  • Questions pitched far above learners' current knowledge produce guessing rather than reasoning; effectiveness depends on calibration to prior knowledge [~M]
  • Calling only on volunteers systematically skews formative information toward the most confident learners [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Instructor-generated: Socratic sequences, hinge questions, anticipation guides before reading
  • Learner-generated: question generation and peer questioning, which shift learners into the questioner role and support self-regulated learning [+M]
  • Embedded prompts: written questions inserted into text or video that prompt self-explanation at key points
  • Digital: classroom response systems (e.g., Peer Instruction with clickers) that make every learner answer simultaneously

Target Learners¶

  • Learners of all levels benefit from well-calibrated questioning, but the payoff is largest when questions require generative answers rather than recognition Active learning improves exam performance relative to lecture alone. [+S]
  • Novices need scaffolded question sequences; experts benefit more from open, ill-structured questions that provoke cognitive disequilibrium [~M]
  • Reluctant or anxious participants need low-stakes response structures (think-pair-share, written responses) before public questioning

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: questions requiring explanation and prediction force learners to connect ideas
  • Prior knowledge activation: pre-questions prepare learners to integrate new content Activation improves learning. [+M]
  • Metacognition and self-regulation: learner-generated questioning builds monitoring habits
  • Formative diagnosis: surfacing misconceptions for immediate instructional adjustment Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S]

Instructions¶

  1. Open with activation questions — pose one or two questions that surface what learners already know or believe about the topic (Activation).
  2. Ask at the right cognitive level — favor "why," "how," "predict," and "compare" questions over yes/no or pure recall; reserve recall questions for checking prerequisites.
  3. Wait — hold 3–5 seconds of silence after asking, and after a learner answers, before responding Increasing wait time improves the quality of student responses. [+S].
  4. Distribute responses — use think-pair-share, cold call with norms of safety, or response systems so all learners answer, not just volunteers.
  5. Probe for reasoning — follow answers with "What makes you say that?" or "How would that work if…?" to elicit articulation of underlying thinking.
  6. Act on the answers — treat responses as formative data; reteach or re-sequence when answers reveal misconceptions Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+S].
  7. Close with consolidation questions — ask learners to summarize, generate their own questions about the material, or predict applications.

Related Strategies¶

  • Class Discussion — questioning is the primary engine that drives productive discussion
  • Self-Explanation — questioning turned inward; prompts asking "why does this work?" produce the same generative processing
  • Check-In — low-stakes questioning used to surface learner state and readiness
  • Argumentation — sustained questioning of claims and evidence is the dialogic form of argumentation
  • Apply six questioning techniques in whole-class discussion: simplify, moderate, provoke thought, challenge, follow up, and relate to students

Examples¶

  • Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard) — conceptual questions posed via clickers, individual answer, peer discussion, re-answer; the question sequence is the core of the method (https://blog.peerinstruction.net).
  • Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown) — learners rotate roles including "question asker" while reading expository text, generating their own comprehension questions.
  • Hinge questions in formative assessment — a single multiple-choice question with diagnostic distractors, posed mid-lesson, that determines whether the teacher moves on or reteaches (Black & Wiliam's "inside the black box" practice).
