# Reducing Visual Clutter

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Reducing visual clutter involves minimizing unnecessary or distracting visual stimuli in the learning environment — on classroom walls, slides, web pages, and course materials. The strategy applies the *coherence principle* from multimedia learning research: people learn better when extraneous graphics, decorations, and background information are excluded rather than added [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [+S]. In physical classrooms, it means curating displays so that only content supporting current learning objectives remains visible; in digital materials, it means stripping decorative images, gratuitous animation, and competing layout elements.

## Design Implications

Visual clutter competes for limited attentional resources: young learners in highly decorated classrooms spend more time off-task and score lower on focused learning tasks than learners in sparse classrooms [Fisher et al., 2014] [+M]. Because working memory is severely limited, extraneous visual processing consumes capacity that would otherwise go to schema construction [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S]. The goal is not sterility but *selective* visual design — every visible element should either support the learning goal or be removed.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An audit of the visual environment (walls, slides, LMS pages, handouts) distinguishing instructional content from decoration
- Willingness to remove or rotate displays; anchor charts and reference material should be current, not cumulative
- For digital materials, a layout discipline: one core visual per slide or screen, consistent placement, generous white space ([Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md))
- Awareness that some visual material is functional (labels, worked models, word walls in use) and should be retained

#### Constraints
- Overly sparse environments can feel institutional and may reduce the sense of belonging or warmth that supports engagement [~W] — the effect is on attention, not on motivation broadly
- Highly decorated environments do not harm all learners equally: effects are strongest for young children and learners with attention difficulties; adults with developed attentional control show smaller effects [Fisher et al., 2014] [~M]
- Environmental decluttering cannot compensate for cluttered *instructional materials* — a bare classroom with overloaded slides still produces extraneous load [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]
- Learners may rely on removed reference displays (e.g., word walls, formula charts); removing them without fading support can disrupt established routines

#### Implementation Variability
- **Physical environment:** rotate wall displays to match the current unit; keep a "focus wall" with only current-objective content
- **Slides and multimedia:** apply the coherence principle — remove decorative images, background music, and nonessential animation [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [+S]
- **Digital course design:** simplify LMS navigation, hide unused menu items, use consistent module templates
- **Individual accommodation:** preferential seating away from high-traffic or visually busy areas for learners with attention or sensory-processing needs ([Accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md))

### Target Learners
- Young children (K–2 especially), whose selective attention is immature and most vulnerable to environmental distraction [Fisher et al., 2014] [+M]
- Learners with ADHD or sensory-processing sensitivities, for whom extraneous visual stimuli impose disproportionate attentional cost [~M]
- Novices in any domain, who lack the prior knowledge to distinguish relevant from irrelevant visual information [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+M]
- Less critical for experts, who can filter irrelevant stimuli using domain schemas [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Focused attention during direct instruction, reading, and assessment
- Reduced extraneous cognitive load during acquisition of new, complex material ([Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md))
- Support for early literacy and numeracy, where attention to the instructional display is the task itself

### Instructions
1. **Audit** the visual environment: photograph walls, screens, and materials; classify each element as instructional, reference, or decorative.
2. **Remove or archive** decorative items that do not support current objectives; store rather than discard so displays can rotate.
3. **Curate** what remains: group related items, label them, and align them with current learning goals ([Chunking](../principles/chunking.md) reduces the working-memory cost of what stays visible [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]).
4. **Simplify digital materials** using the same test: one message per screen, no decorative graphics, consistent layout ([Clear Structure & Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md)).
5. **Observe and adjust**: monitor on-task behavior and learner feedback; reintroduce warmth (color, student work, plants) if the environment feels sterile, keeping functional content primary.

## Examples
- **Fisher, Godwin & Seltman (2014)** — Laboratory classrooms decorated heavily vs. sparsely: children in the heavily decorated condition spent significantly more time off-task and performed worse on lessons; the sparse condition is the canonical demonstration of the effect ([study](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614533801)).
- **Mayer's coherence-principle experiments** — Removing interesting-but-irrelevant graphics, photos, and background audio from multimedia lessons consistently improves retention and transfer ([Mayer, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316941355)).
- **Slide design practice** — Presentation guidance in Clark & Mayer's *E-Learning and the Science of Instruction* directs designers to strip decorative clip art and animation from instructional slides, retaining only graphics that explain content.
- **Classroom practice** — A teacher maintains a single "focus wall" with only the current unit's anchor charts, rotating prior charts into a reference binder rather than accumulating them on walls.
