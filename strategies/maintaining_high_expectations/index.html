Maintaining High Expectations¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 5 studies (3 review, 2 quant-synthesis), q2–q4 · 2 of 5 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

In culturally responsive instruction, achievement expectations are determined individually rather than applied uniformly. Students receive praise in proportion to their accomplishments — not lavish praise for simple tasks — and when a student struggles, the instructor guides them positively through the initial steps rather than lowering the demand. The strategy communicates the belief that every student can succeed at challenging work while calibrating support to current performance.

Design Implications¶

High expectations function as a motivational signal: students infer their own capability partly from the difficulty of the work and feedback they receive. Specific, challenging goals produce higher performance than vague or easy ones Specific difficult goals lead to higher performance. [+S], and self-efficacy built through mastery experiences predicts persistence Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]. The key is pairing demanding goals with process-level guidance, so that struggle is framed as solvable rather than as evidence of inability.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Individualized assessment of each learner's current performance and next achievable challenge (Assess Performance)
  • Feedback that is specific, task- and process-focused, and proportional to actual accomplishment (Provide Feedback)
  • Guided entry points for struggling students — modeling or breaking down the first steps — rather than task abandonment (Practice)
  • Consistent communication that difficulty signals the instructor's confidence, not doubt

Constraints¶

  • Inflated praise for trivial success ("Great job!" for minimal effort) can signal low expectations and reduce motivation, particularly for students with low self-efficacy [-M]
  • Perceived as unfair or demotivating when applied without adequate support structures; high expectations without scaffolding widen achievement gaps for underprepared learners [~S]
  • Requires time and effort to understand individual student needs; difficult to sustain in very large classes without systematic assessment routines
  • Teachers' implicit biases can distort individualized expectations downward for some groups; without structured, evidence-based assessment, "individual judgment" reproduces inequity [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Mastery-based structures make individual expectations operational by advancing students on demonstrated competence rather than seat time
  • Formative feedback cycles (Assessment for Learning) let expectations be renegotiated as performance changes
  • In large classes, rubrics and staged task versions approximate individualized expectations at scale

Target Learners¶

  • Students at risk of underperforming, who benefit most from the combination of challenge and guided support Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence. [+M]
  • Learners who have internalized low expectations from prior schooling; proportional praise rebuilds accurate self-assessment
  • Novices, who need process-level guidance rather than outcome-level pressure Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Self-efficacy and persistence: building the belief that effortful work leads to success
  • Accurate self-assessment: praise calibrated to real accomplishment teaches learners to judge their own work honestly
  • Problem-solving independence: guided first steps that fade toward self-directed completion

Instructions¶

  1. Establish each learner's current performance level through diagnostic or formative assessment (Assess Performance).
  2. Set a specific, challenging but attainable goal for each learner — difficult enough to require effort, close enough to current ability to be reachable Specific difficult goals lead to higher performance. [+S].
  3. When the learner struggles, guide them through the initial steps — model, prompt, or break down the task — rather than reducing the goal (Practice).
  4. Give praise proportional to genuine accomplishment, and make feedback process-focused: what the learner did and how to improve next Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+S].
  5. Have learners reflect on progress toward their own goals (Individual Reflection), reinforcing accurate self-evaluation.
  6. Raise the expectation as competence grows, so challenge tracks development rather than remaining static.

Related Strategies¶

  • Growth mindset framing — high expectations only motivate when learners believe ability is developable
  • Mastery learning — the structural version of individually calibrated expectations
  • Proportional feedback — the communication mechanism that makes expectations credible

Examples¶

  • Zearn — digital math curriculum that keeps grade-level content constant for all students while providing built-in remediation of prerequisite skills, embodying "high expectations with guided entry."
  • Expeditionary Learning (EL Education) — school network whose design principles include "high expectations for all students," with revision and critique cycles that pair demanding work with structured support.
  • Writing conferences — a teacher works one-on-one, naming what the student's draft already does well (proportional praise), then sets one specific next challenge and models how to attempt it.
