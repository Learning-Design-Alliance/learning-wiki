# Instructor Feedback Loops

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
An instructor feedback loop is a recurring cycle: the instructor elicits evidence of learning (a check, draft, or performance), interprets it against learning goals, delivers feedback that specifies the gap and how to close it, and then adjusts instruction while learners revise or retry. The loop — not the feedback message alone — is the unit of design; feedback only improves achievement when learners have an opportunity to act on it and the instructor responds to what the evidence shows.

## Design Implications

Feedback is among the most powerful influences on learning, but its effects are highly variable — it can improve or degrade achievement depending on design [Formative feedback practices improve achievement when learners act on them.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Effective loops orient feedback to the task and the process ("what next?") rather than the person ("praise"), and they close: feedback that is delivered but never acted on is administrative overhead, not instruction.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear learning goal against which performance is interpreted ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) aligned to objectives)
- Frequent, low-stakes evidence-gathering — checks, drafts, exit tickets — rather than reliance on summative events
- Feedback that is specific, actionable, and timed so learners can use it while motivation and task context are still active
- Scheduled time for learners to revise, retry, or apply the feedback ([Practice](../elements/practice.md) following feedback)
- Instructor follow-through: adjusting pacing, re-teaching, or regrouping based on the evidence

#### Constraints
- Feedback without an opportunity to act on it produces little or no learning gain [-S] — the "feedback" is wasted unless the loop closes
- Praise and person-directed feedback ("you're so smart") can reduce persistence and performance relative to task-focused feedback [~M]
- Overly detailed or evaluative feedback on weakly motivated tasks can depress performance compared with no feedback at all [~S]
- Checklists and rubric-only evaluation without narrative guidance are ineffective in online settings [Checklist-based evaluation is ineffective for improving online learning work.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Feedback given long after the performance — e.g., weeks-later grades on drafts — loses most of its instructional value [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class loops**: aggregate error patterns from a check, then re-teach to the class rather than commenting on every paper
- **Conferencing loops**: brief individual conversations ([5-Minute Writing Conferences](../strategies/5-minute_writing_conferences.md)) that deliver one prioritized action point per cycle
- **Peer-mediated loops**: instructor trains and calibrates peer feedback, then spot-checks; scales the loop in large classes
- **Automated loops**: adaptive platforms deliver item-level feedback and route learners to remediation ([Adaptive Difficulty](../elements/adaptive-difficulty.md))

### Target Learners
- Struggling learners benefit most from specific, process-level feedback with immediate retry opportunities [+M]
- High-achievers may need less frequent feedback; excessive guidance can reduce their self-regulation [~M]
- Novices need directive feedback ("do this next"); experts benefit more from facilitative feedback that prompts self-evaluation [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Skill refinement: closing the gap between current and target performance
- Self-regulation: learners internalize the loop as self-monitoring and self-correction
- Conceptual correction: surfacing and repairing misconceptions before they consolidate

### Instructions
1. Define the success criteria learners are working toward, and share them before work begins ([Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md)).
2. Collect evidence frequently and cheaply — drafts, exit tickets, quick checks ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md)) — rather than waiting for summative assessment.
3. Diagnose the gap against criteria; identify the highest-leverage error, not every error.
4. Deliver feedback focused on the task and next action, not the person or a grade.
5. Build in immediate time to revise or retry ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)); the loop is not closed until learners act.
6. Adjust instruction based on what the evidence showed — re-teach, regroup, or accelerate — and repeat the cycle.

## Examples
**Formative assessment programs (Black & Wiliam)** — UK mathematics classrooms where teachers used comment-only marking (no grades) plus class-time for students to respond to comments; substantial achievement gains, largest for low achievers.

**Writing process portfolios** — Instructor comments on a draft, students revise before the graded version; the grade attaches to the revision, ensuring the loop closes.

**[Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org)** — Mastery-based practice delivers immediate item-level feedback and routes learners to remedial content; the instructor dashboard aggregates class evidence for whole-class follow-up.

**[ASSISTments](https://www.assistments.org)** — Online math homework providing instant feedback to students and error-pattern reports to teachers the next morning, enabling next-day re-teaching.
