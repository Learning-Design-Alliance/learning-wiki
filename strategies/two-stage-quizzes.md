# Two Stage Quizzes

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
A two-stage quiz (also called a two-stage or collaborative exam) is administered in two passes: learners first complete the quiz individually and submit their answers, then immediately retake the same or overlapping items in small groups, discussing each question until the group agrees on an answer. Grades typically weight both stages (e.g., 75% individual, 25% group), and feedback is delivered through the discussion itself rather than delayed instructor commentary.

## Design Implications

The strategy combines two well-supported mechanisms: retrieval practice from the individual stage and peer explanation during the group stage. The individual-first sequence is essential — committing to an answer before discussion produces the retrieval effort and error exposure that make the subsequent feedback meaningful [Retrieval practice strengthens retention more than restudying.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Group discussion then functions as immediate, targeted feedback: disagreements surface misconceptions at the exact moment they can be corrected, which is far more effective than the same feedback delivered days later [Feedback is most effective when delivered while the task is still active.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Items that are genuinely discussable — conceptual or multi-step questions where reasoning can be debated, not pure recall
- A grading structure that motivates serious effort on both stages (individual score must still matter)
- Groups of 3–5 with heterogeneous preparation, formed in advance
- Time budget: individual stage ~60–70% of the period, group stage the remainder
- Instructor or TA circulation to keep groups on task and adjudicate unresolvable disagreements

#### Constraints
- Groups can converge on a wrong answer with high confidence, especially when one member dominates [Peer discussion without individual accountability can propagate errors.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [-M] — the individual stage mitigates but does not eliminate this
- Weakly motivated individual attempts reduce the group stage to answer-copying, eliminating the retrieval benefit
- Less effective for low-level factual items where discussion adds no reasoning to negotiate
- Logistically heavier than a standard quiz: same-item reuse raises academic integrity concerns across sections, and scheduling two passes takes class time

#### Implementation Variability
- **Two-stage exams** (Gilley & Clarkston): full midterm or final exams with the group stage immediately after individual submission
- **Weekly two-stage quizzes**: low-stakes versions using clickers or LMS quizzes, with the group stage run via [Peer Instruction](../patterns/collaborative-learning.md)-style voting
- **Scratch-card / IF-AT forms**: immediate answer revelation between stages lets groups verify rather than guess
- **Asynchronous variant**: individual online attempt followed by a scheduled synchronous group discussion — weaker, since the delay erodes the feedback advantage

### Target Learners
- Undergraduate and secondary learners in content-heavy courses (STEM, economics, anatomy) where conceptual misconceptions are common [Active learning improves exam performance and reduces failure rates in STEM.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Mixed-ability cohorts: stronger students consolidate by explaining; weaker students receive immediate, peer-calibrated correction
- Less suited to complete novices who lack enough knowledge to contribute to or evaluate group reasoning

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding and misconception repair, not just recall
- Transfer of reasoning: articulating *why* an answer is correct
- Metacognitive calibration — comparing one's confidence against peer judgment exposes overconfidence [Assessment activities that make thinking visible improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Write or select 10–20 conceptual, discussable items; avoid items with a single memorized answer.
2. Set the grading split (e.g., 75/25 individual/group) and announce it in advance so both stages carry stakes.
3. Administer the individual stage under exam conditions; collect or lock answers before any discussion.
4. Form pre-assigned groups of 3–5 and have them retake the quiz, requiring consensus on every item.
5. Circulate to prompt reasoning ("convince me"), note unresolved disagreements, and reveal answers afterward with brief whole-class explanation of the most-missed items.
6. Follow up with targeted re-teaching or practice on items where group answers still diverged from correct ones.

## Related Strategies
- Peer Instruction — the in-class questioning routine that two-stage quizzes generalize to full assessments
- Immediate Feedback Assessment Technique (IF-AT) — scratch-card format delivering answer revelation within the quiz itself
- Team-Based Learning — uses the same individual-then-team Readiness Assurance sequence as its backbone

## Examples
- **University of British Columbia / Carl Wieman Science Education Initiative** — two-stage midterm and final exams adopted across physics, chemistry, and biology courses; group stages typically worth 15–25% ([cwsei.ubc.ca](https://cwsei.ubc.ca)).
- **Gilley & Clarkston's two-stage collaborative exams** in introductory biology — students completed individual exams then immediately retook them in teams, with significant gains on subsequent individual assessments.
- **Team-Based Learning (Michaelsen)** — the Readiness Assurance Process pairs an individual RAT with a team RAT using IF-AT scratch cards ([teambasedlearning.org](https://www.teambasedlearning.org)).
