# Use Structured Critique Protocols

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Structured critique protocols are explicit routines for peer or instructor critique of work-in-progress. Rather than asking learners to "give feedback," the protocol specifies a sequence — e.g., clarify, then value, then raise concerns and suggest ("ladder of feedback"); or T-A-G (Tell something you liked, Ask a question, Give a suggestion) — so critique is specific, actionable, and socially safe. Protocols are typically paired with criteria or rubrics and a revision opportunity, making critique a formative loop rather than a verdict.

## Design Implications

Structure converts critique from opinion exchange into criterion-referenced analysis, which improves both the quality of feedback given and the quality of revisions made [Feedback that answers "Where am I going, how am I doing, what next?" improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Protocols also make evaluative reasoning itself visible and practicable, so learners build judgment, not just receive it [Structured argumentation practice improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]. The revision step is essential: critique without a subsequent opportunity to act on it produces little learning.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared, explicit set of criteria or a rubric so critique targets the work, not taste ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
- A named protocol with defined turns, sentence stems, and time limits
- Work that is genuinely in progress — critique of finished work invites defensiveness and wastes the revision window
- A scheduled revision or iteration step following the critique ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))

#### Constraints
- Unstructured or checklist-only evaluation online is ineffective; generic checklists without exemplars or dialogue do not improve work [Checklist-based evaluation without exemplars or dialogue is ineffective online.](../claims/checklist-evaluation-ineffective-online.md) [-M]
- Critique of finished, unrevisable work yields little benefit — the formative value depends on a live revision loop
- Novice critics often focus on surface features unless the protocol and criteria direct attention to substance
- Without norms of psychological safety, critique triggers defensiveness; protocols with "value first" steps mitigate but do not eliminate this

#### Implementation Variability
- **Ladder of feedback** (Perkins): clarify → value → concerns → suggestions; suits design and writing critique
- **TAG / Two Stars and a Wish**: lightweight stems for younger or novice critics
- **Gallery walk / silent critique**: written sticky-note critique around displayed work; scales to large groups
- **Critical friends / tuning protocols**: extended, facilitator-led examination of one piece of work against shared standards
- **Studio critique** (art/design education): public, instructor-moderated critique of work-in-progress

### Target Learners
- Learners producing authentic artifacts (writing, designs, code, arguments) who can revise [Structured argumentation practice improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]
- Adolescents and adults with enough evaluative vocabulary to apply criteria; younger learners need heavily scaffolded stems
- Less effective for learners with no prior exposure to the domain's quality standards — criteria and exemplars must come first

### Target Learning Goals
- Quality judgment: internalizing criteria for "good work" in a domain
- Revision and iteration skills
- Giving and receiving actionable feedback (a transferable professional skill)
- Deepening understanding through explaining and defending work [Structured argumentation practice improves reasoning quality.](../claims/argumentation-improves-reasoning.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Establish criteria and, ideally, show exemplars at multiple quality levels before any critique ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md)).
2. Ensure work is at a revisable draft stage; publish the protocol and sentence stems in advance.
3. Run the critique in the protocol's sequence — e.g., clarify, value, concerns, suggestions — with time limits per turn ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) or written gallery format).
4. Have authors paraphrase or record the feedback rather than rebut it; defer defense to a later turn.
5. Schedule immediate revision time so critique converts into action ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
6. Debrief the critique itself — which comments were most useful and why — to build evaluative skill over time ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).

## Examples
- **High Tech High (San Diego)** — project-based schools use gallery walks and critique protocols ("kind, specific, helpful") with multiple draft cycles before public exhibition of student work ([https://www.hightechhigh.org](https://www.hightechhigh.org)).
- **Harvard Project Zero "Ladder of Feedback"** — a widely used protocol sequencing clarify → value → concerns → suggestions for classroom and professional critique.
- **National School Reform Faculty protocols** — tuning and critical-friends protocols used for examining student and professional work ([https://www.nsrfharmony.org](https://www.nsrfharmony.org)).
- **RISD studio critique** — art and design education's long-standing model of public, criterion-anchored critique of work-in-progress.
