# Direct Instruction Patterning

## Description
Direct Instruction Patterning is the deliberate design of example sequences, wording, and practice items so that learners can only induce the intended concept or rule from what is presented. Rooted in Engelmann and Carnine's Theory of Instruction, it treats instruction as an engineering problem: the "pattern" of examples (positives, negatives, minimally different cases) is arranged so the learner's generalization lands on the targeted feature and not on irrelevant surface details. It is typically carried out within a [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) lesson through scripted example sets, unison or rapid-fire responding, and tightly sequenced [Practice](../elements/practice.md).

## Design Implications

Patterning operationalizes the insight that learners generalize from whatever varies in the example set — so the designer must control variation precisely, holding irrelevant features constant and varying only the targeted one [Clear example sequencing reduces misgeneralization and error rates.](../claims/example-problem-sequences-reduce-cognitive-load.md) [+M]. This is a form of [Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md): by controlling the input pattern, the design prevents learners from wasting working memory on spurious hypotheses [Overload from unstructured input degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A precise statement of the concept or rule, including its boundaries (what it is and is not)
- A set of positive and negative examples that vary only on the targeted dimension; negatives are as important as positives
- Initial examples presented with maximal sameness (consistent wording, consistent sequence), then varied systematically
- Immediate, high-frequency response opportunities with corrective feedback ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) or teacher-led correction)
- Cumulative review so discriminations are retained and integrated with earlier content

#### Constraints
- Overly rigid sameness early on can produce narrow, context-bound learning; learners may fail to transfer when surface features change [Highly structured guidance can limit transfer to novel contexts.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M]
- For learners with substantial prior knowledge, the same patterning becomes redundant and can depress performance relative to less guided formats [Guidance that helps novices can hurt more expert learners.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~S]
- Scripted sameness requires teacher fidelity; improvised or inconsistent example delivery breaks the intended generalization
- Less suited to ill-structured domains where the "rule" cannot be specified with clear boundaries — [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) or [Concept Attainment](../patterns/concept-attainment.md) with multiple perspectives fits better there

#### Implementation Variability
- **Initial teaching format**: "test" format (teacher presents examples and a rule) vs. "presentation" format (rule stated first, then examples) — chosen based on whether the concept is easy or hard to induce
- **Range of examples**: narrow ranges for initial teaching, widened ranges in later tracks to promote generalization
- **Integration**: later lessons juxtapose newly taught discriminations with earlier ones so learners must discriminate rather than merely identify

### Target Learners
- Novices and struggling learners, who benefit most from low-error, tightly sequenced instruction [Explicit, structured guidance benefits novices most.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [+S]
- Whole-class settings where high response rates keep all learners engaged and expose errors quickly
- Less beneficial for advanced learners, who can handle and benefit from exploratory formats [Guidance that helps novices can hurt more expert learners.](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~S]

### Target Learning Goals
- Concept learning: teaching discriminations and classifications with clear boundaries
- Rule and strategy learning: generalizable procedures (decoding, arithmetic operations, grammar rules)
- Fluency building: rapid, accurate responding through massed then distributed practice

### Instructions
1. Specify the concept or rule precisely, including all cases it covers and does not cover.
2. Draft a positive-example set that varies only on the targeted feature; pair each with a minimally different negative example ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)).
3. Script consistent teacher wording for the initial examples; introduce the rule via [Modeling](../elements/modeling.md) and [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) where the reasoning matters.
4. Sequence guided [Practice](../elements/practice.md) with immediate correction, then fade prompts as accuracy stabilizes ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).
5. Widen the range of examples and interleave earlier content so learners discriminate rather than pattern-match superficially.
6. Schedule cumulative review across subsequent lessons.

## Examples
- **Reading Mastery / DISTAR** (McGraw-Hill) — the canonical application: letter-sound correspondences taught through minimally different example sets, scripted wording, and rapid unison responding, with decoding rules introduced only after the pattern is secure.
- **Corrective Mathematics** (Engelmann & Steely) — subtraction regrouping taught by holding problem format constant across an initial example track, then widening digit ranges once the algorithm is firm.
- **Direct Instruction follow-through evaluations** — large-scale implementations of patterned DI lessons in Project Follow Through showed among the strongest basic-skills and affective outcomes of compared models.
