# Mass, Density, and Buoyancy Experiments

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Mass, density, and buoyancy experiments are hands-on physical science activities in which learners investigate how an object's mass and density affect whether — and how deeply — it floats in different liquids. Typical tasks include comparing a pencil's buoyancy in freshwater versus saltwater, weighing various balls, and measuring the water they displace. The goal is to connect observable phenomena (floating, sinking, water level) to the underlying quantitative relationships (mass, volume, density, displaced fluid).

## Design Implications

Hands-on experimentation makes abstract, invisible properties like density tangible, but physical activity alone does not guarantee conceptual learning — guided inquiry with structured prediction and explanation outperforms unstructured discovery [Furtak et al.'s meta-analysis finds teacher-led guidance is the strongest component of inquiry learning.](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654311425484) [+S]. The design challenge is directing attention to the *relevant* variables (mass, volume, liquid density) rather than salient but irrelevant ones (size, material color).

### Context
#### Requirements
- Freshwater, saltwater, and other liquids of differing density; test objects (pencil, balls of varying mass/volume); balance scale; graduated container for displacement measurement
- A recording sheet that prompts [prediction](../elements/prediction.md) before testing and [self-explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md) after — learners who articulate *why* an object floated gain more than those who only observe [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
- Structured comparison across cases so learners can abstract the density rule from multiple instances [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]

#### Constraints
- Unguided "sink or float" play without measurement or prompts produces activity without abstraction; learners often retain the misconception that "heavy things sink" [Klahr & Nigam found hands-on discovery alone did not yield better conceptual outcomes than direct instruction without explicit evaluation of evidence.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00700.x) [~S]
- Measurement error (imprecise scales, splashing, partial submersion) can produce data that contradicts the target relationship, confusing novices [~M]
- Requires materials, water access, and cleanup time that constrain whole-class implementation [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- Demonstration-plus-prediction format (teacher runs the test, learners predict and explain) for younger or resource-limited settings
- Full student-run investigation with data tables for upper elementary and middle school
- Extension to quantitative density calculation (mass ÷ volume) and prediction of floating in unknown liquids

### Target Learners
- K–8 learners building foundational physical science concepts; the qualitative version suits K–4, quantitative versions suit grades 5–8
- Learners holding common misconceptions ("heavy things always sink") benefit from [cognitive conflict](../elements/cognitive-conflict.md) when a small dense object sinks while a large light one floats [~M]
- Learners new to measurement benefit from [part-task practice](../elements/part-task-practice.md) on weighing and reading displacement before combining both in one investigation [+W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding of density as a mass-to-volume relationship, not a property of "heaviness"
- Science process skills: predicting, measuring, recording, comparing data across conditions
- Evidence-based explanation: linking observations to the principle of displaced fluid

### Instructions
1. Activate prior experience with floating and sinking, surfacing predictions and existing misconceptions [Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md)
2. Demonstrate the measurement procedure (weighing, submerging, reading displacement) so learners attend to the phenomenon rather than the mechanics [Application](../elements/application.md)
3. Have learners predict, then test each object in freshwater and saltwater, recording mass, displacement, and outcome
4. Prompt [self-explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md): "Why did the same pencil float higher in saltwater?" [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]
5. Compare across cases to abstract the rule that buoyancy depends on relative density of object and liquid [Multiple contrasting cases support abstraction.](../claims/multiple-contrasting-cases-support-abstraction.md) [+M]
6. Apply the rule to a novel prediction (e.g., an untested object in cooking oil) [Application](../elements/application.md)

## Examples
- **Floating Pencil (NAEP-style task)** — Learners compare a pencil's buoyancy in freshwater versus saltwater, isolating liquid density as the variable.
- **Rising Waters (KDE)** — Learners weigh various balls and measure water displacement, correlating the two to discover the density relationship.
