Summarizing¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (2 for) · 7 studies (2 causal, 2 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 1 of 7 report an effect size

Description¶

Summarizing asks learners to integrate the most important information from a text, lesson, or problem into a concise overview in their own words. In problem-solving contexts, it extends beyond restating content: learners justify their answer, evaluate whether their solution plan succeeded, and articulate the strategies they used. The act of selecting, condensing, and reorganizing forces deep processing of the material rather than surface reproduction.

Design Implications¶

Summarizing works because it requires learners to reconstruct meaning — identifying what matters, dropping what does not, and building connections — which produces better comprehension and retention than rereading or passive review Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+S]. Summaries function as a form of generative processing: the learner must do the cognitive work of organizing information into a coherent structure, which consolidates it for later retrieval. In mathematics, the strategy doubles as a metacognitive checkpoint — justifying an answer and evaluating the plan exposes gaps in reasoning that would otherwise go unnoticed.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A source text, problem, or lesson with identifiable key information
  • Learners who can distinguish central ideas from supporting detail; where they cannot, scaffolds such as Advance Organizers or Annotating help them locate what matters
  • A clear summary format or length constraint (e.g., "three sentences," "one paragraph," "explain your strategy in two steps") — constraints prevent verbatim copying and force selection
  • Feedback on summary quality, since learners often omit critical content or retain trivia without guidance (Provide Feedback)
  • Explicit instruction in the elements and rules of a good summary (Direct Instruction)
  • Texts at appropriate complexity for practice, escalating over time
  • Feedback on summaries, ideally identifying what was wrongly included or omitted (Provide Feedback)
  • Ongoing Practice with Assessment of summary quality

Constraints¶

  • Learners with weak topic knowledge struggle to identify key information and may produce summaries that are lists of surface features rather than integrated overviews [-M] — these learners need modeling of expert summarizing before summarizing independently
  • Poorly constrained summaries can devolve into transcription; copying without selection produces little generative processing [-M]
  • For novices, summarizing a complex text can add extraneous load before a coherent schema exists [~M] — sequencing matters: summarize after comprehension support, not as a substitute for it
  • Effectiveness declines when summaries are graded on length or polish rather than accuracy of key ideas [-W]
  • Assigning summaries without teaching how to summarize yields little benefit; untaught summarizers default to verbatim copying or topic-listing [-M]
  • Effectiveness depends on text complexity: texts far above learners' reading level overload selection processes and produce poor summaries [-M]
  • Requires significant instructional time; benefits accrue over repeated cycles, not single lessons [-W]
  • As a study technique, summarization is less effective than practice testing for later recall when used without training [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Written summaries — the standard form; a sentence-length limit forces prioritization
  • Oral summaries — partner retells or "sum it up in 30 seconds" routines; lower barrier for learners with writing difficulties
  • Structured frames — fill-in templates ("The problem was about… The strategy I used was… because…") that scaffold the justification component in math
  • Collaborative summarizing — pairs negotiate a joint summary, surfacing disagreements about what is important
  • Summary as assessment — one-sentence summaries or "tweet-length" recaps as quick formative checks
  • Rule-based instruction (Brown & Day's five rules) versus modeling via Think-Aloud of expert summarizing
  • Collaborative summary writing before independent practice
  • Automated feedback tools such as Summary Street score content coverage and paraphrasing, allowing practice without teacher turnaround time
  • Oral summaries, graphic-organizer summaries, or one-sentence "gist" summaries as lower-load variants

Target Learners¶

  • All learners, but strongest effects for those who otherwise use passive study strategies like rereading [+M]
  • Learners who benefit from structured approaches; frames and templates make the strategy accessible to those who cannot yet self-select key ideas
  • Less beneficial without support for learners with limited vocabulary or background knowledge, who cannot yet judge relevance [~M]
  • All writers, especially those who must work with complex expository texts
  • Adolescent and adult readers who have not received explicit comprehension strategy instruction
  • Less effective for learners who already summarize fluently; instruction becomes redundant Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reading comprehension: building a coherent mental model of a text
  • Retention and consolidation: strengthening memory for key content Chunking reduces working memory load. [+M]
  • Metacognition and mathematical reasoning: justifying answers and evaluating solution strategies
  • Study skills: replacing rereading with a generative review strategy
  • Reading comprehension: identifying main ideas and text structure
  • Writing skill: concise, organized restatement in one's own words

Instructions¶

  1. Present the text, problem, or completed solution, ensuring learners have first comprehended the material (support with Advance Organizers if needed).
  2. Model expert summarizing once — think aloud while selecting key ideas and discarding detail, so learners see the selection criteria.
  3. Ask learners to produce a constrained summary in their own words (length or format limit), integrating main points rather than listing them.
  4. In problem-solving contexts, extend the summary to justification: state the answer, explain why the strategy was chosen, and evaluate whether the plan worked.
  5. Provide feedback focused on whether key ideas were captured and connections made, not on style.
  6. Follow with practice — apply the summarized understanding to a new problem or discussion, converting the summary into use.

Related Strategies¶

  • Self-Explanation — the underlying mechanism; summarizing is self-explanation applied to whole texts and solution processes
  • Annotating — a precursor skill; margin notes and highlighting identify the material summaries will draw from
  • Retrieval Practice — summarizing from memory combines generation with retrieval for stronger retention
  • Concept Mapping — an alternative synthesis format that makes relationships explicit rather than prose-based
  • Foster articulation by having students build testable artifacts and explain ideas to other students
  • Annotating — marking important text is a precursor skill to summary selection
  • Advance Organizers — frames what counts as important before reading
  • Reciprocal Teaching — embeds summarizing among comprehension strategies with modeling and fading

Examples¶

  • Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown) — small groups take turns summarizing a text segment as one of four strategies, with the teacher modeling and fading support over sessions.
  • Math journaling — after solving a multi-step problem, students write "My answer is… I solved it by… This worked because…" — the justification and strategy-evaluation form of summarizing.
  • Khan Academy — lesson wrap-ups and unit summaries model concise overviews that students can emulate in their own notes.
  • One-sentence summaries in AP courses — students compress a reading into a single grammatical sentence using a "somebody wanted but so" frame, forcing selection and integration.
  • Summary Street (Kintsch et al., CU Boulder) — intelligent tutoring system that evaluates student summaries against source text for content coverage and flags copying, enabling self-guided practice with automated feedback
  • Reading Apprenticeship (WestEd) — disciplinary literacy routines in which students write and compare summaries of complex subject-area texts
  • Reciprocal Teaching (Palincsar & Brown) — small-group routine in which students take turns leading summarization of text segments, with teacher modeling fading over time
