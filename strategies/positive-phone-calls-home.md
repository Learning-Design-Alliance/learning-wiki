# Positive Phone Calls Home

## Description
Positive phone calls home are brief (2–5 minute) calls or messages from a teacher to a family member that share specific good news about a student — effort, improvement, kindness, or achievement — made *before* any behavioral or academic problem occurs. They are a relationship-building intervention, not a reporting mechanism: the goal is to establish the teacher–family relationship on a positive footing and to signal to the student that their teacher notices and values them.

## Design Implications

Positive contact with families raises student engagement and improves teacher–family relationships, with effects strongest when contact is frequent, early in the term, and positive rather than corrective [The effect of teacher-family communication on student engagement.](https://doi.org/10.1080/19345747.2012.749649) [+M]. The mechanism is partly relational — building a sense of [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) for the student and trust with the family — and partly behavioral, since students who anticipate positive recognition show more on-task behavior [Positive greetings and proactive relationship-building improve academic engaged time.](https://doi.org/10.1177/1098300717753831) [+M]. Calls must be specific and genuine; vague praise or obviously scripted positivity can read as insincere and undermine trust [~W].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A system for tracking which families have been contacted (roster, spreadsheet, or communication log)
- Specific, observable behavior or work to cite in each call — not generic praise
- A translation or interpretation plan for multilingual families
- Time budgeted during planning periods; roughly 3–5 calls per week covers a class within a term

#### Constraints
- Contact framed around problems or complaints reduces — and can reverse — the benefit; families who only hear from school when something is wrong disengage from future contact [-M]
- One-way, teacher-initiated contact without follow-up channels for family response has weaker and shorter-lived effects [~W]
- Logistical barriers (work schedules, disconnected numbers, language mismatch) disproportionately exclude the families who benefit most; calls that never reach anyone consume teacher time without effect [-W]
- Effects fade if contact is a one-off event; sporadic positive calls late in the year after a negative history are less credible and less effective [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Positive greetings at the door** — the same logic applied in-person daily; brief positive interactions at entry improve engaged time and reduce disruptions [Cook et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1098300717753831) [+M]
- **Positive postcards or texts** — lower-cost asynchronous variants; text messaging shows comparable engagement effects where phone contact is impractical [~M]
- **Student-invited calls** — the student chooses what good news is shared, adding an [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md) component
- **Empathic discipline framing** — pairing positive contact with relationship-first approaches to misbehavior reduces suspensions, particularly for marginalized students [Brief empathic discipline intervention cuts suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]

### Target Learners
- Students at risk of disengagement or with prior negative school–family histories, for whom a positive first contact resets the relationship [+M]
- Middle and secondary students, where teacher–family contact is rare and therefore more salient [~M]
- All students benefit, but effects are strongest for those whose families have limited prior positive contact with school [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral: increased engagement, on-task time, and reduced disruptive behavior
- Affective: student sense of [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) and teacher–family trust [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Relational: establishing family–school partnership as infrastructure for later academic communication

### Instructions
1. Build a contact roster with preferred language, phone/text preference, and best contact times ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md) data can supply this).
2. Identify 2–3 specific, recent positives per student — a strong discussion contribution, improved work, an act of helpfulness ([Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) records are a good source).
3. Make the call early in the term, before any negative contact has occurred; keep it under five minutes and end by inviting the family to reach out.
4. Log the call and rotate through the roster; aim for every family at least once per term.
5. Follow up: reference the positive contact with the student ("I told your mother about your lab work — she was proud"), converting the call into a visible [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) signal.
6. When problems later arise, open any difficult conversation by referencing the established positive history ([Action-Oriented Feedback](action-oriented-feedback.md)).

## Examples
- **Kraft & Dougherty (2013) field experiments** — high school teachers made positive calls home about 10 students per class; called students showed measurably higher engagement and positive behavior in class (https://doi.org/10.1080/19345747.2012.749649).
- **Positive Greetings at the Door (Cook et al., 2018)** — teachers greeted each student by name with a brief positive interaction at entry, raising academic engaged time and cutting disruptive behavior (https://doi.org/10.1177/1098300717753831).
- **Cook et al. (2017) empathic discipline study** — a brief online module encouraging relationship-first responses to misbehavior halved suspension rates among middle school students, showing how relational framing changes disciplinary outcomes (https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617704428).
