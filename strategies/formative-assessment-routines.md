# Formative Assessment Routines

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Formative assessment routines are scheduled, repeatable practices — entry tickets, quick polls, whiteboard responses, [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md), exit tickets — that elicit evidence of student thinking during instruction. The teacher (or system) interprets that evidence and adapts instruction in the moment or the next session, closing the loop between what was taught and what was learned.

## Design Implications

Formative assessment is among the highest-yield instructional practices available, with meta-analytic support for substantial achievement gains when feedback and instructional adjustment actually follow the evidence [Formative assessment improves achievement when evidence is used to adapt instruction.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. The routine matters more than the instrument: short, frequent, low-stakes checks produce more usable evidence than occasional elaborate quizzes, because they lower stakes and increase sampling of understanding. Critically, the assessment must be *formative in effect*, not just in name — collecting data without changing instruction produces little benefit [Formative assessment improves achievement when evidence is used to adapt instruction.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A clear learning target against which evidence is interpreted ([Assessment](../elements/assessment.md) aligned to objectives)
- A fast, low-friction elicitation format every learner responds to (all-student response, not volunteer-only)
- Teacher or system routines for interpreting evidence and deciding next actions (reteach, advance, regroup)
- Feedback that tells learners where they are relative to the goal and what to do next

#### Constraints
- Grading formative checks converts them to summative pressure and suppresses honest disclosure of confusion [Formative assessment improves achievement when evidence is used to adapt instruction.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [-M]
- Routines that only a subset of students respond to (hand-raising, volunteers) yield biased evidence about class understanding
- Over-frequent checking without instructional response consumes time and erodes trust in the routine
- Poorly aligned items measure compliance or effort rather than the target construct, leading to wrong adjustments

#### Implementation Variability
- **Entry routines** (warm-up problems, retrieval starters) activate prior lessons and expose retention gaps
- **Mid-lesson routines** (mini-whiteboards, quick polls, think-pair-share with sampling) allow real-time pivots
- **Exit routines** ([3-2-1 reflection](3-2-1_reflection.md), exit tickets) summarize the day's understanding and shape the next session
- **Digital dashboards** (e.g., [Formative](https://www.formative.com), [Socrative](https://www.socrative.com), [Kahoot!](https://kahoot.com)) automate aggregation but require deliberate pacing to avoid turning checks into leaderboard competitions

### Target Learners
- All learners benefit, but gains are largest for lower-achieving students, who are most dependent on timely correction rather than inference from delayed grades [Formative assessment improves achievement when evidence is used to adapt instruction.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- Learners with weak metacognitive monitoring, who otherwise misjudge their own understanding
- Anxious or low-confidence learners, for whom low-stakes routines reduce evaluation threat — provided checks are clearly ungraded

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: diagnosing misconceptions before they consolidate
- Procedural fluency: catching error patterns during early practice
- Self-regulated learning: routines model monitoring one's own comprehension against criteria

### Instructions
1. Define the learning target and the evidence that would show it is (or is not) met.
2. Choose a fast all-student response format (whiteboards, tickets, digital poll) — avoid volunteer-only elicitation.
3. Run the routine at a fixed point in the lesson so it becomes automatic rather than optional.
4. Interpret results against the target: sort responses into "secure / partial / missing" rather than scoring them.
5. Act on the evidence in the same or next lesson — reteach, regroup, or advance — and make the action visible to learners.
6. Keep checks ungraded; reserve evaluation for separate [summative assessment](../elements/assessment.md) points.

## Examples
- **Dylan Wiliam's "five key strategies" work with UK mathematics teachers** — teacher teams embedded daily all-student response routines (no-hands questioning, mini-whiteboards, exit passes) and documented significant learning gains over two years.
- **[Formative](https://www.formative.com)** — live digital assignments where the teacher watches responses stream in and can intervene mid-lesson.
- **Retrieval warm-ups in [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org) classrooms** — mastery-practice openers give teachers a dashboard of retention gaps before new instruction begins.
