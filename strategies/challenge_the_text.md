# Challenge the Text

## Description
Challenge the Text is a critical literacy strategy in which students interrogate a text by generating and answering their own text-dependent questions — questioning what is said, what is omitted, whose voice is privileged, and whose is silenced. Students take multiple perspectives, uncover assumptions and biases, and support their analysis with textual evidence. The strategy can operate during or after reading and places an anti-bias lens on the literacy experience by surfacing issues of power within social contexts, positioning students as participants in — rather than recipients of — textual discourse.

## Design Implications

Question generation transforms reading from comprehension into evaluation, and self-generated questions prompt elaborative processing that improves conceptual understanding [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+M]. The strategy enacts critical literacy by treating texts as authored, interested artifacts rather than neutral information, which builds both comprehension and critical agency.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A central text (literary or informational) rich enough to sustain critical analysis — texts with an identifiable perspective, omission, or ideological stance work best
- Prompts and scaffolds for generating critical questions (e.g., "Whose viewpoint is missing?", "What does the author assume the reader believes?", "Who benefits from this account?")
- Time for students to generate questions, respond individually, and revisit their interpretations
- Facilitation that establishes norms for discussing sensitive topics of power, identity, and bias

#### Constraints
- Students with limited prior knowledge of a topic may generate superficial questions; without domain background, questioning can devolve into literal recall [~M]
- Requires significant scaffolding early on — students unpracticed in critique tend to ask yes/no or comprehension-level questions rather than evaluative ones
- Discussions of bias and power can surface emotionally charged content; without careful facilitation and safety norms, marginalized students may bear the burden of educating peers
- Overuse on every text risks generalized cynicism; the strategy is most powerful when contrasted with texts worth trusting, so students learn *when* to challenge, not just *that* they should

#### Implementation Variability
- During reading (annotating with challenge questions in margins) vs. after reading (structured question-generation rounds)
- Individual written response vs. [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) of pooled questions
- Sentence starters for English language learners to structure effective critical questions
- Teacher-modeled questioning first (I do / we do / you do) before independent question generation

### Target Learners
- Students in grades 6–12 with sufficient decoding fluency to attend to authorial choices rather than word recognition
- Students benefit most when they have some prior knowledge of the topic to anchor evaluative questions [~M]
- Particularly valuable for students from marginalized backgrounds, who gain language for analyzing how texts represent their communities — though facilitation must avoid making them spokespersons

### Target Learning Goals
- Critical literacy: identifying perspective, bias, and omission in texts
- Deep comprehension: making inferences and supporting analysis with textual evidence
- Agency and voice: entering the discourse of a text rather than passively receiving it
- Argumentation: constructing and defending evidence-based claims about a text's credibility and stance

### Instructions
1. Select and assign a central text with a clear perspective or omission using [Assigned Readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md).
2. Model critical questioning aloud on a short excerpt, then support students in generating their own text-dependent challenge questions using [Conceptual Questioning](../elements/conceptual-questioning.md) and prompts (e.g., "What is left out?", "Who is quoted, and who is not?").
3. Have students answer their own and peers' questions in writing through [Individual Response](../elements/individual-response.md), citing textual evidence.
4. Facilitate discussion of the strongest questions, then have students revisit and revise their interpretations through [Reassessment](../elements/reassessment.md) — noting what changed in their reading of the text and why.

## Examples
- A middle school class reading a textbook account of westward expansion generates questions about whose voices are absent (Indigenous nations, women), then compares the account with a primary source from a displaced community.
- A high school media literacy unit asks students to challenge a news article's sourcing: Who is quoted? What claims lack attribution? Students rewrite the lede from an omitted perspective.
- Reading with a critical lens is a core practice in [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md)-style literacy modeling, where the teacher first demonstrates expert questioning before students question independently.
