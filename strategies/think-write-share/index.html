Think-Write-Share¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 4 studies (2 quant-synthesis, 1 causal, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 1 of 4 report an effect size

Description¶

Think-Write-Share is a questioning strategy in which every learner first thinks silently about a posed question, writes an initial response, and then shares that response with peers or the whole class. The written record creates individual accountability and gives learners an artifact to revise, while the sharing phase exposes divergent ideas and drives discussion toward a more accurate shared understanding.

Design Implications¶

The strategy operationalizes active participation for all learners rather than the few who volunteer, and the writing phase functions as retrieval and self-explanation practice that strengthens conceptual understanding Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]. Committing an answer to paper before discussion also reduces the anchoring effect of hearing a peer's answer first, producing more diverse and more honest responses. The sharing phase then acts as formative assessment: the teacher monitors written and spoken responses to identify misconceptions and strategically selects sharers to make those misconceptions discussable.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A focused, conceptually rich question — not one answerable by recall alone
  • Protected silent time for thinking and writing (typically 1–3 minutes)
  • A visible sharing mechanism: document camera, whiteboard, pair talk, or digital response tool
  • A plan for which responses to surface and how to orchestrate revision after discussion

Constraints¶

  • Low-quality or closed questions reduce the routine to compliance; the cognitive benefit depends on questions that require reasoning [~M]
  • Learners unwilling to share authentically (fear of public error) will write safe answers, hiding the very misconceptions the strategy is meant to reveal [-W]
  • If the teacher calls on volunteers only, participation reverts to the usual few and accountability gains disappear [-M]
  • Without a deliberate revision step after discussion, written responses often remain unchanged even when understanding has shifted [-W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Think-Pair-Share: sharing is with one partner before whole-class discussion; lowers the social risk of public error
  • Write-then-revise: learners return to their written response after discussion and explicitly revise it, making conceptual change visible
  • Digital variant: tools such as Padlet or Google Docs collect all written responses simultaneously, allowing the teacher to select contrasting responses anonymously
  • Strategic sharing: the teacher pre-scans written responses and selects students whose answers represent common or contrasting conceptions

Target Learners¶

  • Appropriate for all grade levels and subject areas; widely documented in mathematics discussion practice
  • Particularly valuable for reluctant or anxious participants, since writing first gives every learner a defensible contribution [~W]
  • English language learners benefit from the additional processing time and the written scaffold for oral participation [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding: articulating and refining definitions and explanations Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]
  • Misconception diagnosis: surfacing incorrect conceptions for public examination
  • Discourse and revision norms: learning to evaluate and improve one's own reasoning in light of peer argument

Instructions¶

  1. Pose one specific, open question tied to the target concept.
  2. Provide silent think time, then have learners write an individual response (Individual Response); circulate and scan responses for misconceptions (Assessment).
  3. Have learners share responses with a partner or small group (Peer Discussion), comparing and critiquing answers (Collaboration).
  4. Facilitate whole-class sharing (Whole-Class Sharing), strategically selecting responses that expose contrasting conceptions (Class Discussion).
  5. Ask learners to revise their written responses (Reassessment) and treat the revision as evidence of conceptual change.

Related Strategies¶

  • Wait Time — the silent think phase enacts extended wait time, increasing response quality and participation
  • Cold Calling — often combined after the write phase so every learner can be called on with a prepared answer
  • Exit Tickets — the written component can double as a formative artifact collected at the end of class

Examples¶

  • A fourth-grade teacher asked "What is an equivalent fraction?" Students wrote their responses, shared with partners, and then revised their written definitions after a class discussion surfaced the misconception that equivalence means "looks the same."
  • Illustrative Mathematics curricula embed "Notice and Wonder" routines that follow a think–write–share structure to launch problem-based lessons.
  • In undergraduate STEM, peer-instruction sequences (ConcepTests) pair individual commitment to an answer with peer discussion and re-voting, producing significant learning gains (Smith et al., 2009).
