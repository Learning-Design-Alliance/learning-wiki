# Sentence Starters

## Description
When the instructor asks a question, rather than having students raise hands and selecting one volunteer, every student in the class writes a sentence in response. The written sentence — often framed by a provided stem such as "The solution works because…" — gives every learner active practice articulating reasoning in academic language, and can then be shared, discussed, or collected for formative assessment.

## Design Implications

Requiring a written response from every student converts a passive listening moment into [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md), which reliably improves achievement compared with lecture-only formats [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]. Writing a sentence forces retrieval and verbalization of understanding, exposing gaps that a nod or silence would conceal, and gives the instructor a whole-class formative snapshot rather than a sample of one confident volunteer [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. Provided stems also scaffold disciplinary language — students "get the feel of writing about math" — which is especially valuable for multilingual learners and students with weak academic vocabulary.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A question or prompt answerable in a sentence, with a stem that models the target syntax ("I noticed… so I…", "The evidence shows… because…")
- Time for all students to write (typically 1–3 minutes) and a mechanism for sharing: pair exchange, random selection, or collection
- A low-stakes climate; sentences should be graded for thinking, not mechanics, or participation drops

#### Constraints
- Ineffective if the prompt is recall-only or yes/no — the sentence adds no thinking beyond what a hand-raise would [-W]
- Students who do not understand the concept may produce formulaic sentences that mimic the stem without understanding; stems can mask non-learning if the instructor reads for form rather than substance [-M]
- Adds time per question; overuse across a lesson creates writing fatigue and erodes the participation benefit [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Think-pair-share hybrid:** students write, compare sentences with a partner, then revise before sharing
- **Progressive stems:** sentence frames fade over a unit, from full frames to word starters to no support, mirroring [Fading](../elements/fading.md)
- **Differentiated stems:** weaker writers receive fuller frames; stronger writers receive open prompts or are asked to write two sentences with contrasting reasoning
- **Digital collection:** tools like Padlet or Google Forms let the instructor display and cluster sentences for whole-class analysis

### Target Learners
- Students hesitant to speak publicly — writing is a lower-risk participation channel than cold-calling [~M]
- Multilingual learners and students with weak academic language, who benefit from the syntactic model the stem provides [+M]
- Whole classes in discussion-heavy subjects (math, science) where typical participation skews toward a few volunteers [-M for the hand-raising baseline it replaces]

### Target Learning Goals
- Articulating reasoning: explaining *why* an answer is correct, not just producing it
- Disciplinary literacy: practicing the sentence structures of mathematical or scientific argument [Disciplinary literacy instruction improves comprehension.](../claims/disciplinary-literacy-instruction-improves-comprehension.md) [+M]
- Formative assessment: surfacing whole-class understanding for instructional adjustment

### Instructions
1. Pose a question worth a sentence — one requiring reasoning, not recall.
2. Display a sentence stem that models the target reasoning structure ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can frame the stem's logic).
3. Give silent writing time; every student writes ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
4. Have students share — pairs compare, or the instructor selects and reads several sentences anonymously.
5. Respond to the collected sentences: reteach, contrast strong and weak reasoning ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md)), and [Assess Performance](../elements/assess-performance.md) for the whole class.

## Examples
- **Middle-school math:** after solving ¾ + ⅚, every student completes "The common denominator is needed because…" before the teacher displays three anonymous sentences for comparison.
- **Writing about math routines** in [Cognitively Guided Instruction](../patterns/cognitively-guided-instruction-cgi-for-math.md) classrooms use sentence stems ("I know this because…") to make students' strategies visible to the teacher.
- **[Writing Next](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/edlabs/regions/southeast/pdf/REL_20074024.pdf)-informed ELA instruction:** Graham and Perin's meta-analysis found structured sentence-combining and strategy frames among the most effective writing practices for adolescents [+S].
