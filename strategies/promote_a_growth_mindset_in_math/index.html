Promote a Growth Mindset in Math¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (3 for) · 6 studies (2 causal, 2 review, 1 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), q2–q3 · 0 of 6 report an effect size

Description¶

Promote a growth mindset by giving feedback that values effort, strategy use, and persistence, showing learners that there is always room to improve their skills. In mathematics in particular, learners often hold entity beliefs — that math ability is innate and fixed — which makes errors feel like verdicts rather than information. The strategy operates through three channels: how adults praise (process over person), how errors are framed (normal and informative), and how the classroom narrative describes what mathematicians actually do (struggle, revise, persist).

Design Implications¶

Growth mindset interventions have small but reliable average effects on achievement, with effects concentrated among lower-achieving and at-risk students [~M]. The mechanism is motivational: believing ability is malleable sustains persistence after difficulty, and self-efficacy in turn predicts academic persistence Self-efficacy predicts whether learners persist on academic tasks. [+M]. Because effects are small and heterogeneous, mindset messaging must be paired with genuinely improved learning experiences — praise alone, without better instruction and opportunities to succeed through effort, does not change outcomes.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Consistent process-focused feedback: praise effort, strategy selection, and revision rather than the person or the outcome ("Your diagram showed you the structure" not "You're a math genius")
  • A classroom culture where errors are treated as information — e.g., analyzing erroneous examples [+M] and normalizing revision
  • Tasks at an appropriate difficulty level, so that effort plausibly leads to success; effort praise is hollow when tasks are impossible or trivial
  • Teacher buy-in: teachers' own mindset beliefs and their treatment of struggling students moderate intervention effects

Constraints¶

  • Effort praise can backfire when it is insincere or when the student still fails — it can signal low ability [~M]; Dweck's own work warns against praising effort that is not yielding results, and instead recommends praising strategy choice and directing students to new approaches
  • Effects are near zero for high-achieving students, who already hold growth beliefs and have little room to gain [~M]
  • Brief standalone interventions (a single lesson or video) show weakly durable effects; effects fade without environmental reinforcement [-W]
  • In performance-focused classrooms where grades reward speed and correctness, mindset messaging is contradicted by the assessment culture and loses credibility [~M]
  • The widely cited original studies have faced replication criticism; large preregistered replications (e.g., the National Study of Learning Mindsets) find effects only for lower-achieving students and only modest in size [~M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Feedback-based: embedded in routine grading and verbal feedback (lowest cost, highest frequency)
  • Curriculum-based: structured programs such as YouCubed (Boaler) and Brainology (Dweck's Mindset Works), which combine lessons on brain plasticity with math tasks
  • Error-reframing: routines like "My Favorite Mistake" or grading that rewards revision
  • Teacher-facing: professional development that changes how teachers interpret struggle, which appears to matter as much as direct student messaging

Target Learners¶

  • Lower-achieving students and students with fixed beliefs about math ability — the groups for which effects are largest [~M]
  • Students experiencing learned helplessness after repeated failure, where rebuilding efficacy beliefs supports persistence Self-efficacy predicts whether learners persist on academic tasks. [+M]
  • Less valuable for high achievers, who typically already endorse growth beliefs and show negligible gains [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Dispositional: persistence and willingness to attempt difficult problems
  • Metacognitive: treating errors as diagnostic information and revising strategies
  • Long-term trajectory: course-taking and persistence in mathematics pathways (effects on immediate test scores are small)

Instructions¶

  1. Audit your own feedback language: replace person praise ("You're smart") and outcome praise ("Perfect score") with process praise naming the strategy or effort that produced the result.
  2. When a student succeeds through effort, name the strategy, not just the effort — if effort is not working, redirect to a new approach rather than praising persistence alone.
  3. Normalize errors publicly: analyze a common mistake as a class, or use flawed worked solutions so learners see errors as objects of study.
  4. Design tasks where effortful strategies visibly pay off — appropriately challenging problems with multiple entry points, so success is attributable to controllable causes.
  5. Frame math itself accurately: discuss how mathematicians struggle and revise, and avoid messages implying speed equals ability (e.g., timed drills as the primary measure of competence).
  6. Reinforce consistently across grading, parent communication, and classroom routines; a single lesson does not shift a belief the environment keeps contradicting.

Related Strategies¶

  • Provide specific, strategy-level feedback — the primary delivery mechanism for mindset messaging
  • Teach that errors are informative — pairs error analysis with the belief that mistakes drive learning
  • Set mastery-oriented goals — process goals outperform outcome goals for novices Process goals outperform outcome goals for novice learners. [+M]

Related Elements¶

  • Coaching — ongoing process feedback during practice is where mindset messaging becomes credible
  • Check-ins — surface learners' beliefs about their own ability so they can be addressed
  • Assessment — assessment culture either reinforces or undermines the growth narrative

Tools¶

  • YouCubed — Jo Boaler's research-based math mindset curriculum and teacher resources
  • Mindset Works Brainology — structured student program based on Dweck's research
  • Khan Academy — mastery-based progression with unlimited retries models "not yet" rather than failure

Examples¶

  • Instead of "You're good at math," say "I can see you worked really hard on this, and it paid off" or "I like the way you kept trying different strategies until you found one that worked."
  • A teacher displays an anonymous student error, has the class diagnose the misconception, and thanks the error's author for the learning opportunity — making mistakes a routine object of study.
  • A school replaces timed fact drills with accuracy-and-strategy goals, removing the speed-equals-ability signal that supports fixed beliefs.
