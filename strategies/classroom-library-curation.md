# Classroom Library Curation

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Classroom library curation is the intentional design of a classroom's book collection: selecting titles that reflect students' identities, interests, and cultures; organizing them for easy browsing and access; and refreshing the collection as readers grow. It treats the library itself as an instructional design decision rather than an inherited room feature.

## Design Implications

Access to appealing, self-selected books is one of the strongest correlates of reading volume, and reading volume drives vocabulary, comprehension, and background knowledge growth [+M]. Curation matters because access alone is insufficient — collections that are too hard, culturally irrelevant, or poorly organized suppress voluntary reading [~M]. Curated collections that affirm students' identities increase engagement and belonging [+W], and fiction reading in particular supports perspective-taking and empathy [Fiction reading improves empathy.](../claims/fiction-reading-improves-empathy.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A range of text levels spanning below, at, and above each reader's current level, so every student can find an accessible entry point
- Books that mirror students' identities and open windows into others' lives (mirrors-and-windows framing)
- Browsing-friendly organization: front-facing displays, labeled bins by genre/interest, and low shelves for young readers
- A refresh cycle — rotating titles, soliciting student requests, and weeding worn or dated material
- Protected time to read from the library (independent reading, [Choice Boards](../elements/choice-boards.md))

#### Constraints
- Leveled-only organization can stigmatize struggling readers and reduce browsing motivation [~M] — labeling bins by reading level signals status; organizing by genre or interest avoids this
- Overly large, disorganized collections reduce circulation; students browse less when they cannot find appealing titles quickly [~W]
- A static collection loses engagement value within a school year; without rotation, voluntary borrowing declines [~W]
- Decorative or display-only book arrangements do not improve learning and can consume shelf space better used for accessible titles [Decorative illustrations do not improve learning.](../claims/decorative-illustrations-do-not-improve-learning.md) [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Interest-first curation**: student surveys and request systems drive acquisition; strongest for reluctant readers
- **Themed collections**: bins rebuilt around curriculum units, connecting independent reading to content instruction
- **Student curators**: rotating committees select and display books, building ownership and autonomy [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- **Digital + audio pairing**: pairing print titles with [Audiobooks](../principles/audiobooks.md) extends access for readers whose decoding lags comprehension

### Target Learners
- Reluctant and developing readers, who are most sensitive to whether appealing, accessible books are visibly available [+M]
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from bilingual and high-interest graphic titles that build comprehension while decoding develops [Dialogic reading improves expressive language.](../claims/dialogic-reading-improves-expressive-language.md) [+M]
- Students whose identities are underrepresented in standard curriculum, for whom a mirror-rich collection supports engagement and belonging [+W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Reading volume and stamina: increasing minutes of engaged independent reading
- Vocabulary and background knowledge growth through wide reading
- Perspective-taking and empathy through diverse fiction [Fiction reading improves empathy.](../claims/fiction-reading-improves-empathy.md) [+M]
- Lifelong reading identity and motivation for voluntary reading

### Instructions
1. **Audit the current collection** — inventory levels, genres, and whose identities are represented; identify gaps against the actual students in the room.
2. **Survey students' interests** — use reading interest inventories or quick polls; let demand drive acquisition priorities.
3. **Acquire across a level range** — include graphic novels, magazines, and informational titles, not only novels; pair print with [Audiobooks](../principles/audiobooks.md) where possible.
4. **Organize for browsing** — face covers outward, bin by genre/interest/author, and avoid level labels on bins.
5. **Schedule access and time** — predictable checkout routines plus protected in-class reading time; the library only pays off if used during the school day.
6. **Refresh and involve students** — rotate displays monthly, add student-requested titles, and invite student curators to feature books.

## Related Strategies
- Independent reading with conferring — the instructional routine that makes a curated library consequential
- Text sets for content units — curation applied to disciplinary reading
- Book talks and read-alouds — the promotion layer that drives circulation of curated titles

## Examples
- **DonorsChoose / First Book Marketplace** (https://www.firstbook.org) — common channels teachers use to fund and acquire diverse, low-cost titles for classroom collections.
- **We Need Diverse Books** (https://diversebooks.org) — provides booklists and curation guidance for building identity-affirming collections.
- **Reading Reconsidered (Lemov, Driggs & Woolway, 2016)** — the "Plague of Independent Reading" chapter argues for curated, text-dependent independent reading rather than unstructured choice alone.
