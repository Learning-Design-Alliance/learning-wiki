# Use eye tracking and neurophysiological measures to assess expertise and human factors in simulated medical emergencies

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 7 claims (6 for, 1 against) · 2 studies (2 associational), `q2` · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 7 claims rest on one study

## Description
The article recommends complementing classical simulation assessment (checklists, SAGAT, NASA TLX) with eye tracking, which objectively captured expert-novice differences the questionnaires missed, and suggests adding physiological measures. It concludes that "A neuroscientific approach with new technology like eye tracking could provide new objective and more sensitive insights on human factors in simulated medical emergency situations", and names portable eye tracking and EEG as contributions to better characterize expertise.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A fixed binocular eye-tracking device, defined regions of interest, and analysis software such as OGAMA
- A standardized video or scenario so gaze data are comparable across participants
#### Constraints
- The article notes a critical lack of efficient tools at the behavioral and cognitive-mechanism levels and calls for multicenter studies with more participants

### Target Learners
- anesthesia residents
- experienced anesthetists
- medical trainees in emergency simulation

### Target Learning Goals
- assessing situation awareness and visual expertise in crisis management
- evaluating simulation-based training outcomes
