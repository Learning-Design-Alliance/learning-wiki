Essential Questions¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 against) · 15 studies (8 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 6 of 15 report an effect size

Description¶

An essential question is an open, arguable question — "When is estimation better than calculation?" or "Who gets to tell history?" — that frames a unit or course rather than a single lesson. It is carried out by posing the question at the outset, returning to it across activities, and having learners revisit and refine their answers as understanding develops. Unlike recall prompts, essential questions have no single settled answer and are designed to be re-asked with increasing sophistication.

Design Implications¶

Essential questions function as advance organizers: they give learners a schema into which new content is filed, improving encoding and retrieval Advance organizers improve learning. [+M]. Because they create a gap between what learners currently believe and what the content reveals, they can also induce the productive dissatisfaction that motivates conceptual change Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change. [+M]. Their power depends on genuine use: a question posed once and never revisited is decoration, not structure.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A question that is genuinely open — experts in the discipline would disagree about the best answer
  • Explicit return points: lesson openings, discussion prompts, and culminating tasks that require learners to revisit and revise their answer
  • Learner-generated tentative answers early in the unit, so later content has something to confirm or challenge
  • Alignment between the question and the assessment — the culminating task should require learners to answer the question with evidence

Constraints¶

  • Questions that are merely rhetorical or that have a predetermined "right" answer reduce the strategy to a lecture hook and can depress engagement when learners detect the sham [-M]
  • Open framing without sufficient structure can overload novices, who lack the background knowledge to navigate unguided inquiry Cognitive overload degrades learning. [-S] — pair the question with advance organizers or worked content rather than free exploration
  • If the question is too broad ("What is art?") it provides no organizing schema; if too narrow it collapses into a lesson objective [~M]
  • Revisiting the question costs instructional time; units packed with content often drop the return visits, eliminating the benefit

Implementation Variability¶

  • Unit-framing: one question anchors a multi-week unit; learners keep a running answer document they revise
  • Lesson-launch: a shorter-cycle question opens each lesson and connects to the unit question
  • Student-authored: learners generate their own essential questions from a topic, which supports autonomy Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M]
  • Cross-disciplinary: a shared question ("What makes a system sustainable?") threads through multiple courses

Target Learners¶

  • Middle school through adult learners; novices need more scaffolding around the question than advanced learners
  • Learners who benefit from seeing the purpose of content before encountering details Advance organizers improve learning. [+M]
  • Less effective as pure discovery for complete novices — the question should frame structured instruction, not replace it [~S]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Conceptual understanding and big-idea retention, rather than procedural fluency
  • Argumentation and evidence-based reasoning, since answering the question requires defending a position Argumentation improves reasoning. [+M]
  • Transfer: questions phrased in general terms invite application across contexts Analogical reasoning improves transfer. [+M]

Instructions¶

  1. Identify the big ideas of the unit and draft an open question that a learner could answer differently at the start and end.
  2. Open the unit by posing the question and having learners record a tentative first answer (activation).
  3. Design each lesson or task so its content bears on the question; open lessons by reconnecting to it.
  4. Use class discussion or structured argumentation at midpoints to have learners compare and defend evolving answers.
  5. Close with a culminating task — essay, case study, or debate — in which learners answer the question with evidence, then reflect on how their answer changed.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating prior knowledge — the tentative first answer to an essential question is an activation move that gives new content something to attach to
  • Case-based discussion — cases supply the concrete material learners use to argue an answer to the question
  • Debate — a format for making the arguable nature of an essential question explicit

Examples¶

  • Understanding by Design (Wiggins & McTighe) — units are backward-designed from essential questions and understandings before activities are planned; see ASCD.
  • C3 Framework social studies inquiries — each inquiry is organized around a compelling question (e.g., "Was the New Deal a success?") with supporting questions and a culminating argument; see C3 Teachers.
  • OpenSciEd — middle school science units launch from anchoring phenomena and an essential question students revisit as models are revised; see openscied.org.
