# Comparing Contrasting Cases

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Learners examine two or more cases that differ on one or more critical dimensions and are prompted to explain *why* they differ. The comparison surfaces deep features that any single case leaves invisible — variation across cases isolates what matters. The strategy is often used as a preparation phase: learners struggle productively with the contrast before being told the canonical explanation.

## Design Implications

Comparison is one of the most consistently supported learning strategies: contrasting cases improve both immediate learning and transfer by directing attention to structural rather than surface features [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S]. The mechanism is discriminative: without variation, learners cannot tell which features of a case are essential and which are incidental [Multiple varied cases support flexible, transferable knowledge.](../claims/cognitive-flexibility-theory-multiple-cases.md) [+M]. Comparison works best when learners generate the differences themselves rather than reading them pre-listed [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [~M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Cases that are minimally different — varying on the target dimension while holding surface features constant (or vice versa, depending on the discrimination goal)
- An explicit comparison prompt ("How are these the same? How are they different? Why?") rather than mere juxtaposition
- A telling or consolidation phase after comparison, in which the instructor names and formalizes what the contrast revealed ([Schwartz & Bransford's](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) "a time for telling")
- Sufficient working-memory headroom; two cases plus a prompt is usually the ceiling for novices ([Cognitive Load Management](../principles/cognitive-load-management.md))

#### Constraints
- Comparison fails when cases differ on too many dimensions at once — novices compare surface features and miss the deep structure [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [-M]
- Learners with very low prior knowledge may lack the vocabulary to articulate differences; unstructured comparison can produce frustration rather than preparation for future learning [~M]
- High-similarity pairs can be *worse* than no comparison when the critical difference is subtle and learners conflate the cases [-W]
- Comparison imposes extraneous load when cases must be held in mind simultaneously and cannot be viewed side by side (e.g., sequential presentation) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Problem-solution comparison**: two worked solutions to the same problem, compared for efficiency or generality (Rittle-Johnson & Star's approach)
- **Preparation for future learning**: contrasting cases explored *before* instruction, to make learners ready to understand the lecture that follows
- **Non-example contrast**: one correct and one flawed case, sharpening discrimination of error conditions ([Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md))
- **Multiple varied cases**: three or more cases across contexts, supporting flexible abstraction ([Cognitive Flexibility Theory](../patterns/cognitive-flexibility-theory.md))

### Target Learners
- Novices who cannot yet distinguish deep from surface features [Comparing contrasting cases improves learning.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S]
- Intermediate learners consolidating multiple procedures — comparing solution methods improves flexibility in strategy choice [~M]
- Less effective as pure discovery for learners with minimal prior knowledge; pairing with brief instructional support restores benefit [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual discrimination: identifying which features define a concept and which are incidental
- Transfer: preparing learners to recognize the same structure in new contexts
- Strategy evaluation: judging when one method is preferable to another
- Preparation for future learning: building perceptual readiness for subsequent direct instruction

### Instructions
1. Select or author a case set that varies systematically on the target dimension; keep surface features either constant or deliberately varied depending on the discrimination goal.
2. Present cases side by side with a structured comparison prompt; require learners to articulate differences in their own words ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) or a comparison table).
3. Let learners attempt to explain the pattern before revealing it — productive struggle with the contrast is the point.
4. Follow with a "telling" phase: instruction that names the deep feature the comparison exposed ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) can frame this consolidation).
5. Follow with practice on new cases, asking learners to predict outcomes using the discriminated feature ([Practice](../elements/practice.md)).
6. Fade the scaffolds: move from guided comparison prompts to independent case analysis as expertise grows ([Fading](../elements/fading.md)).

## Related Strategies
- [Non-Examples](../elements/non-examples.md) — a two-case comparison where one case is deliberately wrong; the minimal contrast form
- [Worked Examples](../principles/worked-examples.md) — comparing two worked solutions is the most studied variant of this strategy
- [Analogical Reasoning](../principles/analogical-reasoning.md) — comparison across cases is the engine of analogical abstraction
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — extended case work; contrasting cases is the compressed, comparison-focused form

## Examples
- **Rittle-Johnson & Star's algebra studies** — students compared two worked solutions to the same equation (e.g., substitution vs. elimination), improving procedural flexibility and conceptual knowledge relative to studying solutions sequentially.
- **Schwartz & Bransford's statistics "A Time for Telling"** — students explored contrasting data displays before lecture; the comparison group outperformed lecture-only and invention-only groups on transfer to novel data situations.
- **[PhET Interactive Simulations](https://phet.colorado.edu)** — learners vary one parameter at a time across runs, effectively comparing contrasting cases of a physical system.
- **[Facing History and Ourselves](https://www.facinghistory.org)** — historical case pairs (e.g., differing responses to the same event) compared to isolate decision points and their consequences.
