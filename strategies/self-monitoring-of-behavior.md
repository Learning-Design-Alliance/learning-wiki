# Self-Monitoring of Behavior

## Description
Self-monitoring is a strategy in which learners assess and record their own behavior — whether they were on-task, followed rules, or met a performance target — at regular intervals. Two common variants are self-monitoring of **attention** (SMA: "Was I paying attention?") and self-monitoring of **performance** (SMP: "Did I complete and correctly answer the work?"). The act of observation itself tends to change the behavior being observed, and the practice builds the foundational self-regulation skill of evaluating one's own actions against a standard.

## Design Implications

Self-monitoring works primarily through reactivity: simply attending to and recording a behavior shifts its frequency, making it a low-cost, active intervention that also offloads monitoring work from the instructor [Self-regulation interventions, including self-monitoring, produce moderate positive effects on behavior and academic outcomes.](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]. Effectiveness depends on precise operational definitions of the target behavior, a simple recording mechanism, and a reliable cue (timer, tone, checklist prompt) that signals when to self-assess. Accuracy matters: training learners to match their ratings to instructor or video-standard ratings improves both accuracy and behavioral outcomes, and accuracy training can be faded once behavior improves.

### Context
#### Requirements
- An operationally defined target behavior with observable indicators (e.g., "eyes on the speaker and pencil moving" rather than "paying attention")
- A self-monitoring form, tally sheet, or digital equivalent matched to the learner's age and developmental level
- A cueing mechanism — timer, audio tones, or scheduled check-ins — that prompts self-assessment at consistent intervals
- Initial training: modeling the rating process, practice ratings against a standard, and feedback on accuracy ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
- A baseline measure of the behavior for later comparison

#### Constraints
- Learners often inflate self-ratings, especially when ratings carry consequences; positive-accuracy training (reinforcing accurate *or* overestimated ratings) mitigates this but does not eliminate it [-M]
- Effectiveness drops when the target behavior is vaguely defined or when cueing intervals are too long for the learner's attention span [-M]
- Requires sustained instructor support during initial implementation; abandoning the scaffold before the behavior is established leads to regression [~M]
- Less useful for behaviors that are covert, low-frequency, or that the learner is not motivated to change [-W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **SMA vs. SMP**: attention monitoring suits engagement problems; performance monitoring suits work-completion and accuracy problems
- **Cue type**: audio tones, vibrating timers, or self-initiated checkpoints at task transitions
- **Fading**: move from instructor-cued to self-cued monitoring, then to intermittent monitoring, as behavior stabilizes
- **Combined forms**: pairing self-monitoring with self-evaluation (comparing self-ratings to a standard) or goal-setting strengthens effects

### Target Learners
- Students with attention difficulties, including ADHD, for whom self-regulation interventions show consistent benefits [+M]
- K–12 learners needing support with on-task behavior, work completion, or social interactions
- Adult and higher-education learners building study habits and independent work routines
- Younger learners need simpler forms, shorter intervals, and picture-based rating scales

### Target Learning Goals
- Behavioral self-regulation: increasing on-task behavior and reducing disruption
- Executive function development: attention control and task persistence
- Metacognitive awareness: recognizing one's own engagement and performance states
- Independence: shifting responsibility for behavior management from instructor to learner

### Instructions
1. Define the target behavior in observable terms and collect baseline data.
2. Choose the monitoring focus — attention (SMA) or performance (SMP) — and design a simple recording form.
3. Train the learner: model a rating, have them practice rating against a standard, and give feedback on accuracy.
4. Set the cueing interval (start short, e.g., every 5–10 minutes) and begin monitoring with the [Practice](../elements/practice.md) routine in place.
5. Review the record with the learner, compare self-ratings with instructor observations, and adjust intervals or definitions.
6. Fade cues and instructor verification as accuracy and the target behavior stabilize.

## Related Strategies
- **Self-Evaluation** — adds a comparison against a standard or goal, strengthening the reactive effect
- **Goal-Setting** — gives the monitored behavior a target to move toward
- **Check-Ins** — scheduled instructor–learner conversations that review self-monitoring records ([Check-In](../elements/check-in.md))
- **Token Economies / Reinforcement Systems** — self-monitoring records can feed reinforcement contingencies, though rewards for accuracy must be handled carefully

## Examples
- **SMA with audio cueing**: a teacher gives a student who loses focus during whole-class instruction a tape or app that plays tones at random intervals; at each tone the student marks "Was I paying attention?" on a card. On-task behavior typically rises within the first weeks of implementation.
- **SMP for work completion**: a student tallies correctly completed math problems per work period and graphs the daily total against a self-set goal, making progress visible.
- **Digital study tools**: apps such as [Forest](https://www.forestapp.cc) or [RescueTime](https://www.rescuetime.com) operationalize self-monitoring for adult learners by logging focused time and prompting reflection on session goals.
