Mentors can help students with visual impairments focus their job-search time and effort while they also work on systemic barriers¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 8 claims (7 for, 1 mixed) · 1 study (1 causal), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 8 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article recommends that students, mentors, and service providers target the controllable parts of job seeking — invested time and effort — with mentor guidance, while remaining aware of systemic barriers. The authors write that "Invested time and effort are aspects of job seeking that students can control" and that mentors can free "time and resources to deal with systemic challenges such as employer attitudes and competition."

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A mentor experienced in the student's career field, as in the study's mentee-mentor matching

Constraints¶

  • The article reports few statistically significant group differences, so the mentoring benefit is supported by trends and mentee ratings rather than strong outcome evidence

Target Learners¶

  • college students with legal blindness within one year of graduation

Target Learning Goals¶

  • job-seeking skills and transition from college to employment

Claims¶

  • Employed college graduates with visual impairments mostly obtained professional or skilled positions with benefits, full-time hours, and competitive salaries [+W]
  • At least 80% of employed students in both groups maintained or advanced their job standing in salary, position, benefits, or hours from posttest to follow-up [+W]
  • Students with visual impairments submitted many job applications but received comparatively few interviews, and securing interviews was a commonly reported challenge [+W]
  • Mentees more often found jobs on their own or through personal contacts, while comparison students more often used employment agencies or recruiters [+W]
  • Mentees rated the program's assistance with the college-to-employment transition significantly higher than comparison students [+W]
  • Employment rates did not differ significantly between mentees and comparison students at posttest or follow-up, though a higher percentage of mentees were employed at follow-up [+W]
  • Lack of transportation was the most frequently reported employment barrier among mentees, with employer discrimination or negative attitudes also common in both groups [+W]
  • College students with legal blindness assigned to a career mentor showed a non-significant trend of spending less time on job seeking than comparison students [~W]
