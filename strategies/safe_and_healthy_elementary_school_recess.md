# Safe and Healthy Elementary School Recess

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Safe and healthy recess treats recess as an instructional setting rather than unstructured downtime. Recess is planned like a lesson — with game structures, role assignments, and trained adult facilitation — so that play deliberately builds cooperation, conflict resolution, and physical activity. Programs such as [Playworks TeamUp](https://www.playworks.org) organize recess around inclusive games, junior coach roles, and explicit conflict-resolution routines (e.g., rock-paper-scissors as a dispute protocol), aiming to reduce bullying and disciplinary referrals while increasing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity.

## Design Implications

Structured recess leverages the fact that social skills and self-regulation are learned through guided practice in authentic peer settings, not through instruction alone. Physical activity during the school day also supports attention and on-task behavior in subsequent lessons [Classroom physical activity improves attention and on-task behavior.](../claims/classroom-physical-activity-improves-attention.md) [+M]. Adult facilitation matters: simply adding structure without coaching and modeling tends to produce compliance rather than transferable social skills.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A recess plan developed like a lesson plan: game rotations, equipment inventory, and space zoning
- Trained recess staff (coaches, paraprofessionals, or teachers) who can [model](../elements/modeling.md) game rules and conflict-resolution language
- Student leadership roles (e.g., junior coaches) that distribute ownership of game facilitation
- A consistent, low-friction conflict-resolution protocol all students know (e.g., rock-paper-scissors for disputed calls)
- School leadership commitment to protecting recess time and sustaining staffing

#### Constraints
- Over-structuring recess can eliminate the autonomy and free play that make recess restorative; benefits depend on balancing organized games with student choice [~M]
- Effects fade without sustained staffing and coaching; one-off training or short-term programs show weaker impacts on climate and behavior [~M]
- Requires space, equipment, and supervision ratios that under-resourced schools may struggle to maintain
- Gains in recess behavior do not automatically transfer to classroom behavior without explicit connection (shared language, [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md), debriefs)

#### Implementation Variability
- Full-service models (Playworks TeamUp: full-time coach embedded in the school) versus training-only models (existing staff trained in recess facilitation)
- Peer-led variants where older students run games as junior coaches, deepening their own leadership and [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) skills
- Climate-integrated variants that connect recess routines to classroom norms, morning meetings, and [whole-class sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md) debriefs

### Target Learners
- Elementary students (K–5/6), with the strongest documented effects in high-poverty urban schools where unstructured recess frequently produces conflict
- Students who struggle with unstructured social settings benefit from explicit game structures and assigned roles [~M]
- Older elementary students gain additional value from leadership roles that build responsibility and belonging [Belonging interventions improve student outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Social-emotional: cooperation, inclusion, conflict resolution, self-regulation
- Behavioral climate: reduced bullying and disciplinary referrals
- Physical health: increased moderate-to-vigorous physical activity
- Leadership: facilitation and peer-coaching skills for student coaches

### Instructions
1. Audit current recess: observe conflict hotspots, inactive students, and exclusion patterns; inventory equipment and space.
2. Co-develop a recess plan with staff and students — game rotations, zone maps, and role assignments ([assigned positions](../elements/assigned-positions.md) such as junior coaches and game leaders).
3. [Model](../elements/modeling.md) each core game and the conflict-resolution protocol explicitly before launching, using [act-it-out](../elements/act-it-out.md) demonstrations of common disputes and their resolution.
4. Facilitate actively: coaches [coach](../elements/coaching.md) in the moment — prompting inclusion, reinforcing positive interactions, and redirecting conflict to the shared protocol ([application](../elements/application.md) of the routine in authentic situations).
5. Structure peer interaction deliberately through small-sided, inclusive games that require cooperation ([peer interaction](../elements/peer-interaction.md)).
6. Debrief: use brief [whole-class sharing](../elements/whole-class-sharing.md) or morning-meeting [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) to name what worked, resolve lingering disputes, and connect recess norms to classroom expectations.
7. Monitor and iterate: track activity levels, incident reports, and student feedback; adjust game offerings and roles.

## Examples
- **Playworks TeamUp** — an independent evaluation across multiple urban districts found improvements in recess organization, student cooperation and conflict resolution, and reductions in bullying and disciplinary referrals relative to comparison schools [~M].
- **Junior Coach programs** — upper-elementary students lead games for younger students, applying [social learning theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md): younger children imitate peer models, while junior coaches deepen their own leadership through teaching.
