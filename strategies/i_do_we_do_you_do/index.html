I Do, We Do, You Do¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 3 claims (2 for, 1 mixed) · 4 studies (2 causal, 1 review, 1 theoretical), q3 · 0 of 4 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

I Do, We Do, You Do (gradual release of responsibility) is a three-phase instructional sequence: the instructor models the task with explicit reasoning ("I do"), learners attempt it collaboratively or with instructor guidance ("we do"), and finally learners perform it independently ("you do"). It operationalizes the principle that control of a task should shift from expert to learner as competence develops, rather than being released all at once or never at all.

Design Implications¶

The sequence embodies Scaffolding and Fading: each phase removes support as learners demonstrate readiness. The "I do" phase works best as a Demonstration with Think-Aloud narration, since silent modeling leaves learners to infer the reasoning Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]. The "we do" phase is where most designs fail — it must involve genuine learner attempts with responsive feedback, not a second teacher-led explanation Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [+S].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A well-defined task or procedure that can be modeled completely (Demonstration)
  • Instructor narration that makes decisions and monitoring visible (Think-Aloud)
  • A structured collaborative attempt in the "we do" phase with instructor monitoring and immediate correction (Feedback, Guided Practice)
  • An independent task closely matched to the modeled one, with support available on request

Constraints¶

  • Treating the sequence as rigid and linear wastes time for learners who already have partial expertise — they sit through redundant modeling Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]
  • A weak "we do" phase (teacher re-explains instead of learners attempting) produces passive learners who fail at independent work Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [-S]
  • Poorly matched independent tasks (too different from the model) break the transfer bridge; too similar and learners copy mechanically [~M]
  • Ill-suited to open-ended, ill-structured tasks with no single expert approach; modeling one path can anchor learners to it

Implementation Variability¶

  • Compressed cycle: the full sequence within a single task (model one paragraph, co-write the next, learners write the third) rather than across whole lessons
  • Reversed check: begin with a "you do" diagnostic to decide whether the "I do" phase is needed at all (responsive/expertise-sensitive variant)
  • Distributed release: alternate we-do and you-do rounds, fading support in steps (Fading)
  • Peer-mediated "we do": Collaborative Learning pairs replace instructor guidance once the model is established

Target Learners¶

  • Novices encountering a procedure or genre for the first time Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices. [+M]
  • Learners with low prior knowledge who would otherwise engage in unguided search
  • Less appropriate for advanced learners, for whom full modeling is redundant and slows progress Worked-example guidance becomes less effective as learner expertise increases. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Procedural fluency: strategies, algorithms, lab techniques, writing processes
  • Strategy acquisition in reading, problem solving, and self-regulated learning
  • Not well suited to goals requiring divergent production or personal meaning-making

Instructions¶

  1. I Do: Model the complete task while verbalizing decisions, self-monitoring, and error recovery (Demonstration, Think-Aloud); keep the model aligned with Clear Structure and Chunking to manage load.
  2. We Do: Learners attempt a parallel task in pairs or with instructor guidance; circulate, prompt rather than tell, and correct errors immediately (Guided Practice, Feedback).
  3. You Do: Learners complete an independent task of comparable structure; withhold help initially to build independence, then review (Practice, Assess Performance).
  4. Fade and cycle: On subsequent lessons, shorten or skip the "I do" phase as fluency grows (Fading).

Related Strategies¶

  • Worked Example → Completion → Problem Fading — a problem-solving-specific version of the same release sequence
  • Think-Aloud Modeling — the core technique of the "I do" phase
  • Direct Instruction — the broader lesson design within which this sequence typically sits
  • Gradual-release coaching model (I do, we do, you do) for building turnaround capacity

Patterns That Use This Strategy¶

  • Direct Instruction — the modeled–guided–independent practice arc is its central structure
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — modeling → coaching → fading maps directly onto the three phases
  • 4C/ID — supportive information and fading task classes implement gradual release across complex-learning tasks

Examples¶

  • Explicit writing instruction: Teacher models writing a persuasive paragraph aloud (I do), the class co-constructs the next one on the board (we do), students write their own (you do) — the standard structure of The Writing Revolution method.
  • Mathematics: A teacher works a ratio problem with full reasoning, pairs solve a near-transfer problem while the teacher circulates, then students solve independently — consistent with worked-example practice research Example-based sequences outperform problem-only practice for novices, and fading support is argued to aid transfer [+S].
  • Khan Academy (khanacademy.org) — narrated video models (I do), hint-scaffolded exercises approximating guided attempts (we do), then independent mastery practice (you do).
