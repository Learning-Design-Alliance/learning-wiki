Letter Names and Letter Sounds Instruction¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 4 claims (4 for) · 9 studies (3 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 9 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

This strategy provides explicit, direct instruction in letter names and letter–sound (grapheme–phoneme) correspondences, typically in kindergarten and early first grade. Letters are taught systematically — a planned sequence rather than incidental exposure — with frequent opportunities to name letters, produce sounds, and map sounds to letters in reading and writing. Letter–sound knowledge, more than letter-name knowledge, is the critical bridge to decoding; letter names can scaffold sound learning (e.g., /b/ in "bee") but must not substitute for it.

Design Implications¶

Explicit letter–sound instruction outperforms incidental or whole-word approaches for building generalizable decoding skill Systematic phonics instruction produces stronger decoding and word-reading outcomes than non-phonics or whole-word approaches. [+S]. Automatic letter recognition matters because it frees working-memory resources for higher-level reading processes Automatic word recognition frees cognitive resources for comprehension. [+S]. Instruction should be cumulative, with newly taught letters reviewed and interleaved with previously taught ones, since distributed review improves retention Spaced repetition improves long-term retention. Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A planned teaching sequence (high-utility letters first; visually and auditorily confusable letters separated, e.g., b/d, m/n)
  • Multisensory routines: tracing, writing, and articulating sounds while viewing the letter, consistent with dual-coding benefits Media combinations affect recall and retention. [+M]
  • Daily brief practice with letter cards, dictation, and sound-spelling mapping (Practice)
  • Immediate corrective feedback on sound production and letter formation (Coaching)

Constraints¶

  • Letter-name drill alone does not produce decoding; teaching names without sounds leaves children unable to read unfamiliar words [-S]
  • Instruction that is explicit but unsystematic (teaching letters in arbitrary order with no review cycle) yields weaker retention and slower mastery [~M]
  • Whole-alphabet memorization as an isolated goal consumes instructional time that could go to sound mapping and blending; alphabet recitation is not evidence of letter knowledge [-M]
  • For a small proportion of learners with severe phonological deficits, letter instruction must be paired with phonological awareness training or it will not stick [-S]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Letter-name-first vs. sound-first sequencing: sound-focused instruction produces stronger early reading; names remain useful for spelling talk and alphabet access [~M]
  • Embedded (letters taught within meaningful words/names) vs. isolated (letter of the week) formats; embedded approaches can raise engagement but require careful sequencing to stay systematic [~W]
  • Speeded retrieval practice (timed letter naming) builds automaticity for children who know letters but retrieve them slowly [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Kindergarten and first-grade students with little or no letter knowledge [+S]
  • Struggling readers and older poor spellers, for whom rapid, accurate alphabet writing is a useful diagnostic and remediation target [+M]
  • English learners, who may know letter names from another orthography but need explicit English phoneme–grapheme mapping [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Letter recognition and rapid, automatic letter naming
  • Phoneme–grapheme correspondence as the basis for decoding and encoding
  • Foundational spelling: mapping sounds to letters in dictation and writing

Instructions¶

  1. Introduce 2–4 letters per cycle with name, sound, and formation, using explicit modeling (Direct Instruction)
  2. Have learners trace and write the letter while articulating the sound, pairing visual and motor codes
  3. Run brief daily review of all taught letters using cards or a cumulative review deck (Practice)
  4. Provide immediate correction and reteaching of confused letters and sounds (Coaching)
  5. Assess recognition, sound production, and speed; use results to regroup and adjust pacing (Assessment)

Related Strategies¶

  • Act It Out — multisensory enactment that can extend sound articulation into whole-body engagement for young learners
  • Activating Prior Knowledge — connecting new letters to familiar words and children's own names anchors new graphemes in known vocabulary

Examples¶

  • Jolly Phonics — systematic letter–sound program teaching 42 sounds with actions, songs, and writing routines; widely used in UK and international early-years classrooms.
  • Reading Recovery — one-to-one intervention that includes rapid letter identification work as an early component for the lowest-attaining first graders.
  • Kindergarten alphabet arc and letter-card routines — common classroom implementations in which children place, name, and sound letters daily, then build and write target words.
