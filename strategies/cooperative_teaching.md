# Cooperative Teaching

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Cooperative teaching structures learning so that students take instructional roles toward one another — verbally or visually explaining problems, sharing knowledge, and assisting peers. The core mechanism is *learning by teaching*: preparing to explain, and explaining itself, forces reorganization of knowledge that benefits the student-teacher as much as the student-learner. It is carried out through structured pair, small-group, or whole-class activities in which students articulate content to peers under instructor guidance.

## Design Implications

Cooperative teaching works because explanation requires learners to retrieve, organize, and monitor their own understanding — gaps surface when one tries to teach [~S]. However, the benefit depends on structure: unstructured group work without individual accountability allows some students to free-ride while others dominate [Group rewards combined with individual accountability are required for cooperative learning to improve achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. The instructor's role shifts from delivering content to designing tasks, assigning roles, and monitoring the quality of peer explanations.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A structured task or problem concrete enough for students to explain to one another
- Time explicitly allocated for peer interaction and teaching, not treated as leftover activity time
- Individual accountability mechanisms (each student must explain or produce something) so participation cannot be avoided
- Instructor monitoring and facilitation to correct misconceptions before they propagate through peer networks

#### Constraints
- Without individual accountability, higher-achieving students do the teaching and lower-achieving students passively receive it, widening gaps [Group rewards combined with individual accountability are required for cooperative learning to improve achievement.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [-S]
- Peer explanations can transmit misconceptions; instructor verification of explanations is necessary, since feedback at the task and process level is what drives improvement [Feedback is most effective when it addresses the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- Students with weak prerequisite knowledge may be unable to explain anything useful; pairing them as explainers without preparation produces frustration rather than learning
- Poorly matched group composition (large ability spreads, social friction) reduces the quality of interaction

#### Implementation Variability
- **Reciprocal roles**: partners alternate between teacher and learner on successive problems, ensuring both experience explaining
- **Jigsaw structures**: each student masters one segment and teaches it to the group, making every member indispensable
- **Prepare-to-teach**: students study material knowing they must teach it, which alone improves retention even before teaching occurs [~M]
- **Teach-back**: students explain a solution to a peer or the class after individual practice, as a formative check

### Target Learners
- K–12 through adult learners; effects are documented across levels, with strong evidence in elementary mathematics and reading [Peer tutoring produces consistent achievement gains for both tutors and tutees.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M]
- Students who benefit from verbalizing reasoning — explaining aloud exposes gaps that silent study hides
- Lower-achieving students benefit as tutees only when the interaction is structured and monitored; as unguided tutors they gain little [-M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Conceptual understanding: explaining forces elaboration beyond memorized procedures
- Metacognitive monitoring: anticipating a peer's confusion requires evaluating one's own understanding
- Communication and explanation skills: verbal and visual articulation of reasoning
- Retention: retrieval and reorganization during teaching strengthens memory

### Instructions
1. Assign a structured problem or concept and pair or group students with complementary strengths ([Practice](../elements/practice.md))
2. Assign explicit teaching roles and rotate them so every student explains at least once
3. Have students explain their reasoning aloud or visually, not just state answers ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
4. Circulate, listen to explanations, and correct misconceptions with task- and process-level feedback ([Coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
5. Hold each student individually accountable through a brief individual product or explanation check
6. Gradually reduce instructor scaffolding as groups learn to self-monitor their explanations ([Fading](../elements/fading.md))

## Examples
- **Math teach-back**: after solving a multi-step problem, students explain their solution path to a partner before the class debrief; the partner must restate the reasoning in their own words.
- **Jigsaw science units**: each group member masters one subsystem (e.g., one stage of photosynthesis) and teaches it to teammates, who are individually quizzed on all segments.
- **PALS (Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies)** — a widely researched structured peer-tutoring program for reading and mathematics in K–6 classrooms ([https://interventioncentral.org](https://www.interventioncentral.org)).
- **Reciprocal teaching in reading**: students take turns leading dialogue as the "teacher," modeling predicting, questioning, clarifying, and summarizing.
