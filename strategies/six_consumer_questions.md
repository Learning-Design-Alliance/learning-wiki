# Six Consumer Questions

## Description
A set of six questions designed to help evaluate the trustworthiness of a news story: **who** made it, **how** it was made, **why** it was made, **when** it was made, **what is missing**, and **where to go from here**. The questions convert an intuitive "does this feel credible?" judgment into a structured inquiry routine that directs attention to source, purpose, and evidence rather than surface features like professional design or confident tone.

## Design Implications

Structured evaluation routines improve students' ability to judge online information compared with unguided instruction or checklists alone [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]. The questions work because they shift evaluation away from superficial cues — which both novices and experts misread — and toward investigating the producer and purpose of a story. They function as a scaffold: with repeated use, the questions become an internalized habit of mind rather than a worksheet.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Access to authentic news stories, including questionable or biased ones (sanitized examples reduce transfer)
- Background knowledge of news production: sourcing, editorial process, advertising, native advertising, and press-release recycling
- Time for guided practice with feedback, not just a one-time presentation of the questions
- A way to record and compare answers across stories ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) or a graphic organizer)

#### Constraints
- Learners without prior knowledge of media institutions cannot answer "who made this?" meaningfully — the questions presuppose background knowledge that must be built first [~M]
- Applied one story at a time in isolation, the routine does not transfer; students need multiple contrasting cases before evaluation generalizes [~S]
- The questions can devolve into a box-checking exercise when used as a compliance worksheet rather than a genuine inquiry
- Less effective for evaluating visual misinformation (doctored images, manipulated video), where the questions address provenance but not image forensics

#### Implementation Variability
- **Full routine vs. triage:** use all six questions for deep analysis, or the first three (who/how/why) as a quick triage filter
- **Lateral adaptation:** pair "who made it?" with leaving the page to investigate the source — lateral reading outperforms staying on the page [~S]
- **Age adaptation:** younger learners use simplified wording ("Who wrote this? Are they trying to sell me something?"); older students apply the questions to primary documents and social media posts, not just articles

### Target Learners
- Secondary and post-secondary students encountering news and social media content independently [Civic online reasoning instruction improves students' evaluation of sources.](../claims/civic-online-reasoning-instruction-improves-evaluation.md) [+S]
- Adults in media literacy and library information-literacy instruction
- Weakest fit for learners who already have strong evaluation habits, for whom the routine adds little beyond speed

### Target Learning Goals
- Evaluating credibility of sources and claims
- Identifying purpose, perspective, and omission in media messages
- Transferable critical reading habits applicable to any information source

### Instructions
1. **Select an authentic story** with a genuine provenance question — a syndicated piece, a partisan site, or a sponsored post ([Case Studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
2. **Ask "Who made this?"** — identify the author, outlet, and funding; leave the page to investigate the source rather than judging it in place
3. **Ask "How was it made?"** — trace sourcing: named experts, documents, aggregation, or unattributed claims
4. **Ask "Why was it made?"** — distinguish informing, persuading, selling, and entertaining
5. **Ask "When was it made?"** — check date, timeliness, and whether old news is recirculated as new
6. **Ask "What is missing?"** — identify absent perspectives, counter-evidence, and context
7. **Ask "Where to go from here?"** — decide what to do: verify elsewhere, share, discard, or seek a better source
8. **Debrief across stories** — compare answers on two contrasting stories so learners see how the questions discriminate ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
