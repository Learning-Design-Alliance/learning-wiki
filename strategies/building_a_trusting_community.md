# Building a Trusting Community

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Building a trusting community means deliberately establishing relational safety before — and alongside — academic work: learners are given structured time to reflect and write about elements of their lives, share those reflections with peers, and see their identities and voices treated as intellectually relevant. Highlighting student voices is essential in making the connections that lay the ground for fruitful discussions, particularly on emotionally or politically charged content such as race and equity.

## Design Implications

Trust is not a soft prerequisite; it changes what learners are willing to do cognitively. When students believe their contributions will be treated with respect, they take the intellectual risks — asking questions, voicing partial understanding, engaging with disconfirming perspectives — that discussion-based and collaborative learning depend on [~M]. Relational trust between teachers and students is associated with measurable gains in engagement and persistence, and sense of belonging predicts academic persistence, especially for students from marginalized groups [Self-efficacy and belonging predict academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]. Structured reflection and sharing routines work because they make mutual knowledge of one another — not just teacher knowledge of students — part of the classroom's working capital.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A commitment to creating inclusive and equitable learning environments; willingness to address and mitigate personal biases
- Structured, low-stakes reflection routines (journals, check-in prompts, identity-relevant writing) with clear norms for confidentiality and respect
- Facilitation skill for discussion: norms co-created with students, teacher modeling of respectful response to disagreement
- Time — trust accumulates through repeated, predictable interactions, not a single icebreaker ([Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) as a recurring practice rather than a one-off)

#### Constraints
- Forced or premature sharing can backfire: requiring disclosure before norms are established produces defensiveness and erodes rather than builds trust [~M] — sharing must be invitational, with students controlling depth and audience
- Discussion of race and identity without skilled facilitation can reproduce the very harms it aims to address; teachers report discomfort and avoidance, and unstructured conversations can devolve into stereotype reinforcement [~M]
- Trust built through personal disclosure does not transfer automatically to academic risk-taking; it must be paired with consistent follow-through on grading, feedback, and fairness
- In large or short-duration settings (MOOCs, single-session workshops), the repeated interactions trust requires may be impractical [~W]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Journaling with teacher response** — private reflection read by the instructor, building dyadic trust without public exposure
- **Structured sharing protocols** — e.g., daily song-lyric or artifact shares, where the shared object mediates vulnerability
- **Expert guests** — inviting professionals of color into the classroom signals whose expertise counts and expands students' sense of what is possible
- **Restorative or advisory circles** — formalized community-building structures common in secondary schools

### Target Learners
- Students from marginalized or minoritized groups, for whom belonging signals predict persistence and engagement [Self-efficacy and belonging predict academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Diverse classrooms where discussion of identity, race, or equity is part of the curriculum
- All levels, particularly those working with diverse student populations; adolescents and adults benefit from reflection-and-share structures, while younger learners need more concrete, play-based equivalents

### Target Learning Goals
- Socioemotional: belonging, trust, willingness to take intellectual risks
- Discussion readiness: creating conditions for complex conversations about race, power, and inequity
- Identity development: supporting positive racial identity formation and perspective-taking ([Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md))
- Dispositional: valuing the course community as a site of learning, which raises task value and engagement [Task value increases motivation and engagement.](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Open with private reflection.** Students write about elements of their lives — experiences, feelings about the class, cultural artifacts that matter to them — before any sharing occurs.
2. **Structure low-stakes sharing.** Use rotating, invitational formats (a different student brings a song lyric each day; small-group shares before whole-class) so exposure is gradual and controlled. See [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) for discussion norms.
3. **Connect student material to academic content.** Tie what students share — the mood of a song, a personal story — directly into the course text or problem, making student voice intellectually consequential rather than ornamental.
4. **Establish and enforce discussion norms.** Co-create agreements for respectful disagreement; model receiving challenge without defensiveness.
5. **Bring in identity-relevant expertise.** Invite engineers, poets, or biologists of color to speak, connecting community trust to expanded possibility models ([Case Study](../elements/case-study.md) and [Application](../elements/application.md) can extend expert visits into analysis).
6. **Sustain the routine.** Repeat check-ins and shares regularly; trust is cumulative.

## Examples
- A high school math teacher has students write in journals so he can connect with them and how they're feeling about his class.
- A literature teacher asks a different student each day to bring in lyrics to a favorite song. Each morning, she plays the selected song and devotes 10 minutes to discussing its lyrics — tying what students observe about the characters or mood into the class text.
- Bringing people of color who are experts in their field (engineers, poets, biologists) to speak to the class, signaling whose knowledge belongs in the discipline.
- **Facing History and Ourselves** (https://www.facinghistory.org) — published curriculum using journaling, identity-focused reflection, and structured discussion protocols to build classroom trust before engaging with difficult histories.
