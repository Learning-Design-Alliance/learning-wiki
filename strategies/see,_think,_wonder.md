# See, Think, Wonder

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
See, Think, Wonder is a thinking routine developed by Harvard Project Zero's Visible Thinking project. Learners examine a stimulating artifact — an image, object, text, or data display — and respond to three prompts in sequence: *What do you see?* (evidence-only observation), *What do you think about it?* (interpretation), and *What does it make you wonder?* (questions). The routine separates observation from inference and culminates in curiosity, ensuring every learner can begin from what is directly perceivable before moving to interpretation.

## Design Implications

The routine's power comes from its explicit separation of evidence and inference, which makes reasoning visible and gives learners a low-stakes entry point — anyone can report what they see [Making thinking visible through structured routines improves the quality of student reasoning.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]. The Wonder step converts observation into inquiry, positioning learners to generate questions that drive subsequent investigation [Cognitive disequilibrium motivates conceptual change.](../claims/cognitive-disequilibrium-motivates-conceptual-change.md) [+M]. Because the first prompt has no wrong answers, the routine activates prior knowledge and normalizes participation before interpretive demands rise [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A rich, ambiguous, or evocative stimulus (image, primary source, artwork, data display, specimen) selected to reward close looking
- A three-column organizer or shared board with See / Think / Wonder headings
- Individual thinking time before sharing, so responses are not anchored by peers
- A facilitator who presses for evidence ("What in the image makes you say that?") when learners move from seeing to thinking

#### Constraints
- Weakly chosen stimuli (obvious, unambiguous, or low-detail images) collapse the routine into surface description with nothing to wonder about [-M]
- Without facilitator pressure for evidence, learners conflate seeing and thinking, and the See column fills with inferences [-M]
- As a standalone one-off activity it produces little durable learning; it must connect to a subsequent inquiry or content sequence [~M]
- Whole-class sharing dominated by a few voices undermines the "everyone starts from success" design; use individual writing or pair-share structures first

#### Implementation Variability
- **Zoom In variant**: reveal an image in successive enlargements, running the routine at each stage to model how evidence changes interpretation
- **Ten-Two variant**: ten minutes of observation, two minutes of recording, repeated — for dense stimuli
- **Silent or written-first variant**: all responses posted on sticky notes or a shared digital board (e.g., Padlet) before discussion, equalizing participation
- **Disciplinary variants**: in science, Wonder becomes hypothesis generation; in history, it becomes sourcing questions about a primary source; in mathematics, it can open a [Three-Act Task](../strategies/three-act-tasks.md) with a perplexing image or video

### Target Learners
- Novices and multilingual learners, because the observation prompt guarantees an accessible entry point before interpretive language is required [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]
- Reluctant participants, since descriptive responses carry no risk of being "wrong"
- All levels from elementary through higher education and museum settings; the stimulus, not the routine, carries the difficulty adjustment
- Less productive for learners who already have deep familiarity with the stimulus, for whom the See step is redundant [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Observation and evidence discipline: separating what is present from what is inferred
- Question generation and curiosity: building the disposition to inquire before being told
- Interpretation and justification: linking claims to visual or textual evidence
- Activation of prior knowledge at the launch of a new topic [Activation improves learning.](../claims/activation-improves-learning.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. **Present the stimulus** without title, caption, or context, and allow silent looking ([Attention](../elements/attention.md)).
2. **Ask "What do you see?"** — collect observations only; redirect any interpretation back to the visible evidence ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) on a projected image works well here).
3. **Ask "What do you think about it?"** — press for justification: "What makes you say that?" ([Articulation](../elements/articulation.md)).
4. **Ask "What do you wonder?"** — record questions without answering them yet; these become the inquiry agenda ([Check-in](../elements/check-in.md) or question wall).
5. **Share and connect** — pair-share, then whole-class synthesis ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md)), then reveal context or begin the lesson the questions point toward.

## Examples
- **Project Zero / Visible Thinking** ([pz.harvard.edu](https://pz.harvard.edu/thinking-routines)) — the source repository, with the routine documented for arts, museum, and classroom use.
- **History class, primary source**: students view an uncaptioned Lewis Hine child-labor photograph; See yields concrete details, Think yields inferences about conditions, Wonder yields sourcing questions ("Who took this? Why?") that launch a document analysis sequence.
- **Science class**: students observe a time-lapse of germinating seeds; Wonder questions become student-generated hypotheses tested in the subsequent lab.
- **Mathematics, Three-Act Tasks** ([Dan Meyer, dy/dan](https://blog.mrmeyer.com)) — the Act 1 image or video functions as the stimulus; "What do you wonder?" generates the question the lesson then answers.
