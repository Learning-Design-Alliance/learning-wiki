# Connecting Math to ESOL

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Connecting Math to ESOL embeds numeracy — money, time, measurement, data interpretation, comparison shopping, schedules — into English language instruction, so that mathematical reasoning supplies the *purpose* for language use. Rather than teaching math as a separate subject in English, the instructor treats quantitative tasks as authentic communicative contexts: learners must read, discuss, negotiate, and explain in English in order to complete them. The approach draws on the distinction between conversational fluency and academic language proficiency — learners need structured opportunities to develop the specialized vocabulary and discourse of quantitative reasoning, which does not emerge from everyday conversation alone [Cummins, J. (1979)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543049002222) [+M].

## Design Implications

Contextualized, meaningful tasks direct learner attention and effort more effectively than decontextualized drill, and relevance of content to learners' goals sustains engagement [Relevancy of emphasis directs attention.](../claims/relevancy-of-emphasis-directs-attention.md) [+M]. Because quantitative tasks impose a double load — new language *and* new mathematics — instructors must manage the combined demand on working memory by controlling linguistic complexity and mathematical complexity independently [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic quantitative materials drawn from learners' daily lives: receipts, bus schedules, pay stubs, unit prices, utility bills, remittance forms
- Explicit pre-teaching of the specialized language of the task — comparatives ("cheaper than"), quantity terms, question forms for clarification — using [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)
- Tasks calibrated so that either the math or the language is familiar; introducing both as new at once overloads learners
- Structured opportunities for learners to explain their reasoning aloud, since verbalizing solution processes builds both conceptual understanding and academic language [Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding.](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Embedding math in language lessons fails when the mathematical demand is itself unfamiliar — learners with interrupted formal education may need the math taught explicitly first, not merely "contextualized" [-M]
- Word problems written in dense or idiomatic English measure reading comprehension rather than numeracy; linguistic complexity can mask or distort mathematical ability [~S]
- If the quantitative context is artificial (contrived "real-world" scenarios learners never encounter), the motivational benefit disappears and the task becomes harder than a plain-language version would be [-W]
- Whole-class instruction at a single mathematical level fails in mixed-proficiency classrooms; math skill and English proficiency vary independently, so one learner may be strong in each differently [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Light integration**: a weekly numeracy warm-up (interpreting a receipt, comparing prices) inside a standard ESOL conversation class
- **Thematic units**: a unit on money and banking, health and dosage instructions, or time and schedules, with math tasks as the communicative core
- **Team-taught or math-infused models**: ESOL and adult basic education instructors co-plan, as in the Math-in-CTE professional development model, where contextualization is paired with explicit connection of the math concept to the context [Stone, Alfeld, & Pearson (2008)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831208317468) [+S]
- **Multilingual scaffolding**: allowing learners to reason first in their strongest language, then rehearse the English explanation

### Target Learners
- Adult ESOL learners who need functional numeracy for work, family, and community life — budgeting, dosing medication, helping children with homework
- Learners whose conversational English outpaces their academic language; contextualized tasks build the register of quantitative discourse [+M]
- Learners with confidence gaps in mathematics: success in familiar contexts builds the self-belief that predicts persistence in further education [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]
- Less suitable as-is for learners with very beginning English proficiency, who may need the task language reduced before the math can serve as a communicative context [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Functional numeracy: interpreting quantitative information in authentic documents
- Academic language development: comparatives, quantities, procedures, and explanation genres
- Communicative competence: negotiating meaning around shared tasks (clarifying, confirming, disagreeing about an answer)
- Transferable problem-solving habits: estimating, checking, and justifying

### Instructions
1. **Select an authentic artifact** from learners' lives (a store flyer, a transit schedule, a utility bill) and identify the math it demands.
2. **Pre-teach the language** of the task with [Analogies](../elements/analogies.md) and visual supports, keeping vocabulary and syntax accessible.
3. **Model the task** with [Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md) and a think-aloud that makes both the math steps and the language moves visible.
4. **Have learners work the task in pairs or small groups**, using [Act It Out](../elements/act-it-out.md) or role-play (customer and clerk, patient and pharmacist) to make the exchange genuinely communicative.
5. **Close with structured explanation**: learners justify their answers to another group, with the instructor giving feedback focused on reasoning and language use rather than a bare right/wrong verdict [Feedback most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

## Examples
- **[EMPower (Extending Mathematical Power), TERC](https://www.terc.edu)** — an adult basic education mathematics curriculum built on open, discussion-based problem solving; widely adapted in ESOL-linked adult classrooms because its language demands are controlled and its tasks draw on adult experience.
- **SABES Math in the ESOL Classroom (Massachusetts)** — professional development and classroom materials from the Massachusetts adult literacy system ([sabes.org](https://www.sabes.org)) showing how numeracy tasks (unit pricing, measurement, interpreting data) can anchor ESOL units.
- **[The Change Agent (World Education)](https://changeagent.worlded.org)** — an adult education newspaper whose issues pair learner-written stories with math activities on themes like money, health, and work, designed for multilevel ESOL and ABE classrooms.
- **Math-in-CTE model (National Research Center for Career and Technical Education)** — experimental studies showed that contextualizing math instruction within meaningful occupational contexts improved math achievement without sacrificing the context course [Stone, Alfeld, & Pearson (2008)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831208317468) [+S].
