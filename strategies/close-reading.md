# Close Reading

## Description
Close reading is a strategy in which learners engage with a short, complex text through multiple readings, each with a distinct purpose — first for literal comprehension, then for how the text works (structure, word choice, author's craft), and finally for interpretation and evaluation. Discussion and questioning are anchored in evidence from the text itself ("text-dependent questions") rather than prior knowledge or personal response alone.

## Design Implications

Close reading concentrates effort on a small amount of high-quality text, allowing working memory resources to be devoted to analysis rather than volume [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Its signature move — requiring answers to be grounded in the text — trains evidence-based reasoning and discourages gist-level reading. Because it depends on rereading, it works best with texts that reward it: dense, ambiguous, or structurally complex passages rather than straightforward informational prose.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A short, complex, worth-rereading text (typically 200–600 words for a single lesson)
- A sequence of purposeful readings, each with a distinct question or lens ([Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) supports this)
- Text-dependent questions that cannot be answered without the text itself
- A culminating discussion or written task requiring textual evidence ([Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

#### Constraints
- Rereading without a new purpose or question yields minimal gains; repetition alone is one of the weakest study strategies [Rereading as a study technique shows low utility relative to other strategies.](../claims/rereading-is-a-low-utility-study-strategy.md) [-S]
- Overuse on accessible texts wastes time and can dull reading motivation [~M]
- Heavy emphasis on text-only evidence can disadvantage learners whose prior knowledge would enrich comprehension; some [Activation](../principles/activation.md) before the first read is usually needed [~M]
- Less effective for novice readers still building decoding fluency — the cognitive cost of rereading dense text can exceed capacity [Cognitive overload degrades learning.](../claims/cognitive-overload-degrades-learning.md) [-M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Whole-class discussion model** (Fisher & Frey): teacher-led short text lesson with successive readings and text-dependent questions
- **Annotation-driven model**: learners mark the text independently across readings, then discuss annotations
- **Disciplinary variant**: close reading of primary sources, data claims, or proofs, where "evidence" is discipline-specific [Shanahan & Shanahan's disciplinary literacy framework](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) [+W]
- **Digital annotation**: collaborative margin tools (e.g., Perusall) make the second and third readings socially visible

### Target Learners
- Adolescent and adult readers who can decode fluently but default to gist reading [+M]
- Struggling middle-school readers when texts are short and teacher-supported [Fisher & Frey report gains for struggling middle school readers with scaffolded close reading.](../claims/scaffolded-close-reading-improves-comprehension-for-struggling-readers.md) [+W]
- Less appropriate for beginning decoders; comprehension instruction should not precede fluency [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Deep comprehension and interpretation of complex texts
- Evidence-based reasoning: citing textual support for claims
- Analysis of author's craft, structure, and point of view
- Disciplinary literacy: reading like a historian, scientist, or critic

### Instructions
1. Select a short, complex, self-contained text worth rereading.
2. First reading: learners read for literal understanding; surface unfamiliar vocabulary ([Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md)).
3. Second reading: pose text-dependent questions about structure, word choice, or author's moves; learners respond with [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md).
4. Third reading: interpretive or evaluative questions requiring synthesis across the whole text.
5. Culminate in discussion or writing in which every claim must cite the text ([Argument Construction](../elements/argument-construction.md)).
6. Fade teacher questioning toward independent analytic reading over time [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M]

## Examples
- **Fisher & Frey's close reading lesson routine** — a widely adopted three-reading, text-dependent-question structure used in K–12 intervention settings.
- **[ReadWorks](https://www.readworks.org)** — free K–12 passages paired with text-dependent question sets designed for repeated reading.
- **AP English Literature classrooms** — timed close analysis of short poetry passages, a canonical assessment application.
- **[Perusall](https://www.perusall.com)** — social annotation platform where students' second readings happen visibly in shared margins.
