Combine a graphic organizer with varied grouping formats to scaffold non-routine problem solving¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 4 claims (3 for, 1 against) · 1 study (1 causal), q2 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 4 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article's design recommendation, drawn from its own study, is to employ two types of scaffolding concurrently: a four-square graphic organizer and a variety of grouping formats alternating teacher-led work, groups of four, pairs, and individual work. The authors report that classroom use of a variety of scaffolds led to an increase in correct responses and more detailed explanations, and that students found group scaffolding helpful in initiating solution pathways.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Both classes covered the same instructional content over nine weeks; groups of four composed of one high, two average, and one low student by prior assessment scores

Constraints¶

  • The study was conducted during a first year of block scheduling (80-minute daily math classes) and full inclusion, which the authors note could have affected findings

Target Learners¶

  • sixth-grade middle school mathematics students

Target Learning Goals¶

  • solving non-routine mathematics tasks
  • geometry concepts
  • problem-solving initiation

Claims¶

  • Varied grouping scaffolds produced significantly higher daily-task scores than graphic-organizer-only instruction in sixth-grade geometry [+W]
  • No significant difference between scaffolding and control classes on the teacher-made geometry unit test [-W]
  • Experimental class showed a larger gain on the state standardized geometry assessment than the control class [+W]
  • Students perceived group scaffolding as helpful for initiating solution pathways on non-routine problems [+W]
