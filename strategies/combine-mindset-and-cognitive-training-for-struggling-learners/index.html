Combine growth mindset and cognitive training for students with learning difficulties¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (5 for, 1 against) · 3 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), q3–q4 · 2 of 3 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The authors recommend that "interventions that combine growth mindset and cognitive training may be especially beneficial for students with learning difficulties." This follows their findings that children with lower prior math skills showed greater growth-mindset gains and that training improved a wider range of math skills particularly in children with math learning difficulties. The strategy pairs direct skill tutoring with experiences that let low-achieving children see their abilities improving through effort.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Tutoring-based training that produces visible skill improvements so low-achieving children experience their abilities improving through effort

Constraints¶

  • Based on a study of 7–10-year-olds; authors note need for active control groups matched for expectations of improvement

Target Learners¶

  • children with math learning difficulties (WJ-III Math Fluency below 25th percentile)

Target Learning Goals¶

  • math skills
  • growth mindset

Claims¶

  • Children with lower growth mindset before training showed greater growth-mindset gains during cognitive training [+W]
  • Four weeks of cognitive training produced larger increases in growth mindset in children than business-as-usual schooling [+W]
  • Changes in dorsal ACC–striatum connectivity predict growth-mindset gains, with dACC–striatal connectivity the strongest individual link [+W]
  • Growth-mindset gains were correlated with increased activation of dorsal ACC, right dorsal striatum, and right hippocampus during difficult math problem solving [+W]
  • Growth mindset prior to training predicted post-training math skills in the training group but not in controls [+W]
  • Growth-mindset interventions have weak average effects on achievement, including a negligible GPA gain (d = 0.11) for lower-achieving US ninth-graders in one preregistered national trial [-W]
