Teach word-problem schemas in a graduated sequence: concrete linear models first, then nonlinear, then closed nonlinear patterns¶

Strategy · All strategies
Evidence · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 causal), q1 · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description¶

The article's implementation sequence for the PP approach with at-risk students: begin with concrete modeling of linear patterns, then introduce nonlinear patterns once linear paths are secure, and only later extend to closed nonlinear patterns. In the first sessions "at-risk students practiced building models for linear PPs through colorful colored clay and pipe cleaner models" in small groups, and the researcher supplied more help in the first steps of structuring nonlinear PPs.

Design Implications¶

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Teacher models identifying main information and relating known data to unknowns before students select operators independently
  • Different colors for embedded linear sub-paths within nonlinear PPs

Constraints¶

  • Students initially voiced a dislike of the new PP approach and could not design PPs correctly
  • The shift to nonlinear PPs was very difficult and required additional researcher support

Target Learners¶

  • at-risk primary students in grades 2 and 3 with mathematical difficulties

Target Learning Goals¶

  • identifying problem structure and the main solution path in word problems

Affordances¶

  • Problem Patterns Pp Approach

Claims¶

  • Closed nonlinear problem patterns were very difficult for at-risk students, particularly second graders [+W]
  • Nonlinear word problems with more than one operation posed extreme difficulty for at-risk students, even within PP instruction [+W]
  • At-risk grade 2 students taught word problems with the Problem Patterns approach scored significantly higher on the posttest than control students taught traditionally [+M]
  • At-risk grade 3 students taught with the Problem Patterns approach showed significantly higher posttest ranks than control students [+M]
  • Experimental and control groups were similar on pretests but differed vastly on posttests, with experimental posttest means near triple control means [+M]
  • The schema-building portion of PP instruction contributed most to the experimental groups' performance advantage [+W]
