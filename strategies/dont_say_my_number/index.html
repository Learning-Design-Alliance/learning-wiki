Don’t Say My Number¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 2 claims (1 for, 1 against) · 4 studies (2 causal, 2 review), q2–q3 · 1 of 4 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

Description¶

A group counting game in which learners count aloud in sequence, but one (or more) designated "forbidden" number(s) must be silently skipped — often replaced with a clap, a gesture, or simply silence. Learners must hold the rule in working memory, monitor their own position in the sequence, and inhibit the prepotent response of saying the forbidden number, combining counting fluency practice with attention and inhibition demands.

Design Implications¶

The game layers a working-memory and inhibition load on top of number-sequence practice, which builds counting fluency precisely because learners must actively reconstruct the sequence rather than recite it automatically [~M]. Skipping a number forces learners to notice the structure of the sequence (what comes before and after the forbidden number) rather than relying on rote rhythm, supporting deeper number-sense development [~W]. Because the forbidden number is a salient, distinctive target, it directs attention to that region of the sequence Relevancy of emphasis directs attention. [+M].

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Learners who can already count in the target range with reasonable accuracy — the game assumes, rather than teaches, the basic sequence
  • A clear, agreed rule for what happens on the forbidden number (clap, silence, "buzz") so errors are unambiguous
  • A facilitator who restarts the count after errors without penalty, keeping the tone playful (Provide Feedback should be immediate and low-stakes)

Constraints¶

  • For learners who have not yet stabilized the counting sequence, the added inhibition demand can overload working memory and produce frustration rather than learning Chunking reduces working memory load. [-M] — the game adds load; it does not reduce it
  • Becomes repetitive and stops demanding attention if the forbidden number stays fixed for too long; the challenge collapses into rhythm once the pattern is automatized
  • Group play means one learner's error can derail the count for all; learners who are anxious about public mistakes may disengage
  • Little benefit for learners already fluent in the range — the game does not by itself extend to place value or operations without deliberate variation

Implementation Variability¶

  • Multiple forbidden numbers (e.g., skip all multiples of 3) turns the game into early skip-counting and multiples practice
  • Counting by twos, fives, or tens shifts the game toward skip-counting fluency and pre-multiplication structure
  • Reverse counting (count backward from 30, skipping a number) targets a notoriously harder direction of the sequence
  • Learner-chosen forbidden numbers increase engagement and ownership; changing the number each round keeps attention demand high
  • Pair or small-group play reduces public-error anxiety and allows peers to Collaborate on reconstructing the sequence after a slip

Target Learners¶

  • Early elementary learners (roughly ages 5–8) consolidating the number sequence within 20–100
  • Learners who can count but do so mechanically; the forbidden number disrupts automaticity and forces active processing [~W]
  • Not yet appropriate for learners still acquiring the sequence itself — use Practice with full support first, then introduce the constraint

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Counting fluency and number-sequence knowledge within a familiar range
  • Inhibitory control and sustained attention in an academic context
  • Early structural insight into skip-counting and multiples (with variations)

Instructions¶

  1. Establish the counting range and the forbidden number; model one round yourself, deliberately skipping the number with the agreed gesture (Demonstration before group play).
  2. Begin counting aloud around the group; when a learner reaches the forbidden number, they perform the agreed action instead of speaking.
  3. If anyone says the forbidden number or loses the count, restart from one (or from a nearby "checkpoint" number for older learners) — treat restarts as part of the game, not failure.
  4. After each round, briefly ask learners how they knew what came after the forbidden number (Self-Explanation of the strategy makes the sequence structure explicit).
  5. Vary the forbidden number, the counting step, or the direction each round to keep the attention demand alive.

Related Strategies¶

  • Counting circle variants — same turn-based oral structure without the forbidden number; use as the on-ramp for learners not ready for the inhibition load
  • Skip-counting games — the multiples-forbidden variation is a direct bridge toward multiplication fluency

Related Elements¶

  • Practice — the game is distributed, repeated practice of the counting sequence embedded in a motivating format
  • Provide Feedback — errors are immediately visible (the count breaks), making feedback intrinsic to play
  • Demonstration — modeling a round, including a deliberate skip, shows learners what successful play looks like
  • Collaboration — group counting makes the sequence a shared product, allowing peers to support one another

Examples¶

  • "Buzz" / "Fizz-Buzz" — a classic classroom and party game; in Fizz-Buzz, multiples of 3 are replaced with "Fizz" and multiples of 5 with "Buzz," scaling the game up to divisibility practice in upper elementary (see e.g. NRICH's Fizz Buzz activity, https://nrich.maths.org/7381).
  • Kindergarten morning-circle counting — teachers commonly run a 1–20 round with one forbidden number as a 3-minute warm-up, varying the number daily.
  • Intervention settings — the format parallels the number-board-game tradition used by Ramani and Siegler, in which repeated, engaging number activities produce gains in numerical knowledge for young children.
