# Address role understanding, perceived expertise, interprofessional culture, and power differentials — and build in bi-directional exchange — when designing and implementing MSF programs in residency

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 6 claims (6 for) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q2` · 0 of 1 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

## Description
This design strategy draws directly on the study's four barrier themes. MSF provided by AHCPs must reflect understanding of residents' responsibilities, match assessors to competencies by perceived expertise, account for ward culture and interprofessional divides, and address power differentials. The authors write that "Modification to the current MSF process as a response to the themes identified in this study should be considered in the design and implementation of future MSF programs."

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Assessors' feedback must reflect an understanding of residents' responsibilities to optimize feedback uptake.
- MSF programs must address power differentials and provide an environment where feedback is viewed as transformative rather than punitive.
- The feedback process must take the culture of interprofessionalism into consideration.
#### Constraints
- Findings come from a nonrandom convenience sample of self-motivated participants in a single pediatric inpatient institution, so views may not be generalizable.
- Responses on this sensitive topic may reflect social desirability.

### Target Learners
- junior and senior pediatric residents
- postgraduate medical trainees

### Target Learning Goals
- communication and professionalism competencies
- self-reflection and behavioral change through formative feedback
