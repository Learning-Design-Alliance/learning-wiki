# Sequence the activity from student-captured first-quarter Moon photographs through proportionality, geodesics, and spherical triangles, challenging teams of learners over one or two 60-minute classes per topic

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 2 studies (1 qualitative, 1 design), `q1`–`q2` · 0 of 2 report an effect size · 2 claims rest on one study

## Description
An implementable activity recipe: students take their own smartphone photographs of the Moon through a simple telescope, then progress through crater-diameter proportionality, geodesic distances, and spherical-triangle areas, realizing that flat calculations differ enormously from documented values. The article recommends photographing "within a few days of the ﬁrst quarter phase, when the sun's rays highlight the moon's features" and states "the best way to use this approach is by challenging teams of learners," with each topic taking one or two 60-minute classes.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Students capture their own clear photographs; photos near first quarter phase (not full moon) so illumination highlights features; GeoGebra for measurements and an optional spreadsheet
#### Constraints
- Telescopes invert images, which must be mirrored or rotated; northern and southern hemisphere images are reversed; a soccer or basketball photo is suggested as an initial complementary activity before the Moon

### Target Learners
- high-school students
- college newcomers
- high-school math teachers in teacher courses
- summer astronomy workshop participants

### Target Learning Goals
- understanding the usefulness of curved (non-Euclidean) geometry
- measuring geological structures on spherical bodies
- transferring flat-geometry concepts to curved surfaces
