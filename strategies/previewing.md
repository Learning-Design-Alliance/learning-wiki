# Previewing

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Previewing prepares learners for upcoming tasks or materials by giving them a brief, structured advance look — a summary of main points, key vocabulary, the task's purpose, or what success will require. It is one of the simplest and most flexible strategies: the instructor or system tells learners what is coming, why it matters, and what to attend to, before the task begins. Previews can be tailored to individual needs (anxiety, attention, organization) and must be followed through — the lesson should match what was promised.

## Design Implications

Previewing works by orienting attention and reducing uncertainty: learners who know the structure of what is coming can allocate working memory to content rather than to figuring out what they are supposed to do [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]. Advance organizers — abstract previews presented before material — produce small but consistent positive effects on learning and retention across a large meta-analytic base [+M]. Previewing also supports emotional readiness: knowing what to expect increases learners' sense of control, which reduces anxiety and supports engagement [Control-value appraisals shape achievement emotions such as anxiety and boredom.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.4.531) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Ability to plan ahead and anticipate learner needs
- A clear structure for the upcoming task so the preview is accurate ([Clear Structure Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md))
- Follow-through: the task must deliver what the preview promised, or trust and orientation break down

#### Constraints
- Requires additional time and effort from the instructor; in fast-paced settings previews are often skipped
- Previews that are too detailed can substitute for engagement with the material itself — learners may feel they already "know" the content and disengage [~M]
- If the preview misrepresents the task's difficulty or structure, it can create false confidence and increase anxiety when the mismatch surfaces [-M]
- Benefits fade for learners with strong prior knowledge, for whom an organizer adds redundant information [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Content previews**: summary of main points and key vocabulary before a reading or lecture
- **Task previews**: stating the goal, steps, and success criteria before an assignment
- **Transition previews**: brief "what's next" statements between activities, valuable for learners with attention or executive-function challenges
- **Personalized previews**: individual check-ins or advance copies of materials for learners with anxiety or processing needs
- **Anticipation guides**: statements learners judge before reading, then revisit after

### Target Learners
- All learners benefit, but especially those with anxiety, attention challenges, impulse-control difficulties, or weak organization skills
- Novices facing unfamiliar task structures, who otherwise spend effort orienting rather than learning [Chunking reduces working memory load.](../claims/chunking-reduces-working-memory-load.md) [+M]
- Anxious learners, for whom predictability increases perceived control and reduces avoidance [Control-value appraisals shape achievement emotions such as anxiety and boredom.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.4.531) [+M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Orientation and attention: directing learners to what matters in upcoming material
- Vocabulary and schema preparation: pre-teaching terms so new content has anchors
- Self-regulation: helping learners plan, anticipate, and organize their effort
- Reduced anxiety as a precondition for engagement and persistence [Self-efficacy predicts academic persistence.](../claims/self-efficacy-predicts-academic-persistence.md) [+M]

### Instructions
1. Identify what will be hardest or most disorienting about the upcoming task (new vocabulary, unfamiliar format, multi-step structure).
2. [Gain attention](../elements/gain-attention.md) with a brief framing statement: what the task is, why it matters, what it will look like.
3. [Recall prior knowledge](../elements/recall-prior-knowledge.md) by connecting the preview to what learners already know ([Activation](../principles/activation.md)).
4. Present key vocabulary or an advance organizer — a short summary, outline, or set of guiding questions ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md)).
5. State expectations and success criteria explicitly ([Clear Structure Presentation](../principles/clear-structure-presentation.md)).
6. Follow through: deliver the task as previewed, and revisit the preview's guiding questions during or after the task.

## Examples
- Before a reading assignment, the teacher provides a brief summary of the main points and key vocabulary, then returns to those points during discussion.
- An anticipation guide in science class: students agree or disagree with claims ("Heavier objects fall faster") before a unit, then revisit their answers after instruction.
- A university syllabus week that walks through each major assignment's structure and rubric, reducing later anxiety and clarification requests.
- For a learner with executive-function challenges, a daily morning preview of the schedule and each task's first step, provided individually.
