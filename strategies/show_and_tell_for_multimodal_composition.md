# Show and Tell for Multimodal Composition

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 3 claims (3 for) · 7 studies (5 quant-synthesis, 2 causal), `q3`–`q4` · 4 of 7 report an effect size

## Description
Show and tell for multimodal composition devotes a few minutes each week for learners to display the effects of different multimodal combinations (image, sound, gesture, layout, moving image) or to narrate their composition process to peers. It creates a space akin to the author's chair in writing workshops, but extended beyond print: learners explain *why* they paired a mode with a meaning, making design decisions — usually tacit — explicit and discussable. The strategy treats multimodal design as a teachable, critiquable craft rather than an intuitive act.

## Design Implications

Explaining one's own work to peers converts composing knowledge into articulate, transferable reasoning, and benefits the presenter as much as the audience [Explaining to others improves the explainer's own understanding and retention.](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M]. Because multimodal meaning-making is distributed across modes, verbalizing design choices helps learners notice relationships between mode and meaning that they otherwise deploy without awareness [~W]. Peer response during show and tell also supplies process- and task-level commentary that teacher grading alone rarely provides [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A recurring, low-stakes slot (2–5 minutes per presenter) with a predictable protocol
- Prompts that push beyond "here is my project" toward design rationale: "Why this mode for this meaning?" "What did you revise?"
- Audience norms for response — descriptive first ("I noticed…"), then evaluative — so critique stays productive
- A shared vocabulary for modes and affordances (e.g., salience, framing, gaze) so feedback is specific rather than impressionistic
- Designated, predictable time for show and tell (e.g., a weekly slot)
- A structured format for sharing: what was made, which modes were combined, why, and what the student would revise
- A shared project or composition in progress, so every student has something to show or tell

#### Constraints
- Without a design-vocabulary scaffold, feedback collapses into taste statements ("I like the colors") that do not build semiotic knowledge [-W]
- Presenters with strong social anxiety or English-language learners may find live performance of work costly; recorded or asynchronous alternatives preserve the rationale-sharing function [~M]
- Weekly public display can privilege confident performers and polished products, marginalizing iterative, in-progress work unless "messy drafts" are explicitly welcomed [-W]
- Large classes make whole-class show and tell impractical; small-group rotations preserve frequency but weaken the whole-class community function [~M]
- Unstructured sharing degrades into show-off sessions; without facilitation prompts, feedback stays at the "I like it" level and carries little instructional value [-M]
- Relies on active participation from all students; a few confident presenters can dominate unless turns are distributed [-M]
- Students with limited metalanguage for multimodal design may struggle to articulate decisions; they need vocabulary support and modeled examples first [-W]
- Whole-class sharing consumes substantial instructional time; with large classes, small-group formats are more sustainable [~M]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Design-focus rounds**: all presenters show the *same* design problem solved differently, enabling comparison across cases [Comparing multiple contrasting cases supports abstraction of underlying structure.](../claims/comparing-contrasting-cases-improves-learning.md) [+S]
- **Process rounds**: presenters show two versions of a segment and narrate what changed and why
- **Asynchronous variants**: annotated screencasts or voice-over slides posted to a class forum, with written peer response
- **Expert models**: instructor or professional designer shows and tells first, modeling the rationale language before students take the chair
- **Show focus:** students display two versions of a text with different multimodal combinations and the class compares effects
- **Tell focus:** students narrate their composition process, including false starts and revisions, making [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) and drafting decisions visible
- **Small-group rounds:** trios share and give structured feedback, then one insight per group is reported to the class
- **Digital gallery:** recorded screencasts or voice-over walkthroughs shared on a class platform, useful for asynchronous or shy students

### Target Learners
- Adolescents through adults producing multimodal texts in writing studios, media arts, or content-area courses
- Learners developing design awareness who need to move from intuitive to deliberate mode choices [~W]
- Presenters benefit most; even reluctant audiences gain exposure to alternative design solutions [Explaining to others improves the explainer's own understanding.](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M]
- K–12 and postsecondary students learning multimodal composition, from early writers combining drawing and text to adolescents producing video and digital texts
- Multilingual learners, who benefit from communicating about design through images and demonstration before verbal explanation [+W]
- Less beneficial as a standalone routine for students who already have strong design vocabulary — they need harder critique structures rather than more sharing [~W]

### Target Learning Goals
- Metacognitive awareness of one's own composing and design decisions
- Multimodal literacy: understanding the affordances and meaning-potentials of different modes
- Peer critique and academic discourse skills
- Community building around an authentic audience for student work
- Metacognitive awareness of one's own composition and design process
- Metalanguage for describing how mode choices (image, sound, layout, gesture) shape meaning
- Communication and critique skills through structured peer feedback
- Community norms of collaborative improvement in a writing-workshop model

### Instructions
1. Establish a shared design vocabulary early in the term (salience, framing, mode affordances) using [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) of mentor multimodal texts.
2. Schedule a recurring slot; rotate presenters so each student presents at least twice per term.
3. Give presenters a two-part prompt: show a specific multimodal choice, then tell the rationale and one revision they made.
4. Structure peer response descriptively-then-evaluatively, directing comments at design decisions rather than the creator — consistent with process-level [Feedback](../elements/assessment.md) norms.
5. Periodically run comparison rounds where several students address the same design problem, then debrief the patterns across cases.
6. Close the cycle by having presenters log one takeaway in a design journal, connecting the discussion to their next composition.

## Examples
- **First-year composition multimodal units** (per Selfe, 2007): students present digital video or web-text projects weekly, explaining sound-image juxtapositions and revision decisions before final submission.
- **Elementary media arts classrooms**: after producing an illustrated podcast episode, students show a two-minute excerpt and tell why they chose music versus narration for a key moment.
- **Teacher education programs**: pre-service teachers show and tell slide decks or infographics they designed, building the design-vocabulary they will later teach.
- **Author's chair in a writing workshop** (Graves & Hansen, 1983) — the print-era model: young writers sit in a special chair, read their work, and field class questions and compliments; show and tell for multimodal design extends this to image, sound, and layout choices.
- **Upper-elementary digital storytelling unit** — students share a two-minute screencast walking through their storyboard, explaining why they paired narration with particular images, and peers suggest alternative multimodal combinations.
- **Secondary media arts class** — a weekly "designer's chair" where students show two cuts of a public service announcement and the class discusses how music and pacing change the persuasive effect.

## Description
Devoting time each week for students to show the effects of different multimodal combinations or tell about their multimodal composition process. Similar to an author's chair in writing workshop, students explain their design decisions and receive constructive feedback from their peers. These student-led discussions generate concrete examples of the various processes used for multimodal composition.

## Design Implications

Show and tell converts private design decisions into public, discussable reasoning, functioning as a form of [Articulation](../elements/articulation.md) within a [Cognitive Apprenticeship](../patterns/cognitive-apprenticeship.md) cycle. 4. Facilitate structured [Class Discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) and peer feedback, steering comments toward process and task level ("What effect does the color choice have?") rather than evaluation of the person.
5. Have presenters record one takeaway or revision commitment, and close by connecting the shared example to the class's growing list of design principles.
6. Follow up with [Coaching](../elements/coaching.md) conferences for students who need support articulating their decisions.
