Value Affirmation Before Assessment¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 1 claim (1 for) · 2 studies (2 causal), q3 · 0 of 2 report an effect size

Description¶

Value affirmation asks learners to select their most important personal values (e.g., relationships, creativity, community) from a list and write for 10–15 minutes about why those values matter to them. Completed shortly before an exam or other evaluative event, the activity reduces the anxiety and identity threat that depress performance, particularly for learners who enter the situation under stereotype or belonging threat. It is a self-affirmation intervention (Cohen & Sherman, 2014), not a confidence or optimism exercise — learners affirm who they are, not how they will perform.

Design Implications¶

Evaluative settings trigger threat responses that consume working memory and depress performance, especially for stereotyped or marginalized groups; affirmation buffers self-integrity, freeing cognitive resources for the task itself Self-affirmation reduces defensive processing and improves performance under threat. [+M]. Effects are largest at the top of the score distribution for threatened groups — affirmation narrows achievement gaps rather than raising everyone uniformly Self-affirmation reduces racial achievement gaps in threatening evaluative contexts. [+M]. The intervention is cheap, brief, and scalable, but it is a buffer, not a substitute for competent instruction.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • A genuinely evaluative, threatening context — affirmation does little in low-stakes settings
  • A values list broad enough that every learner can find something personally important (avoid values that cue the threatened domain itself)
  • Sufficient time (10–15 minutes) and privacy for honest writing
  • Administration before the assessment, not after failure

Constraints¶

  • No effect — and sometimes small negative effects — for learners not under threat; affirming values can reduce effort when the task is not self-relevant Self-affirmation effects depend on threat being present; affirmed non-threatened individuals may disengage. [~M]
  • One-shot administrations show fading effects over time; benefits attenuate without reinforcement or a supportive course climate Self-affirmation effects fade without a reinforcing environment. [~M]
  • Cannot compensate for poor preparation or low-quality instruction; it removes a performance tax, it does not build knowledge
  • If learners perceive the activity as remedial or targeted at them, it can signal low expectations and backfire Self-affirmation backfires when it implicitly labels recipients as at-risk. [-M]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Rank-and-write: choose top 2–3 values from ~10–12 options, then write why they matter (the standard Cohen protocol)
  • Control condition: write about why a least important value might matter to someone else — preserves the writing-time comparison
  • Repeated administration: at exam 1 and exam 2 in a course, sustaining effects across the term
  • Domain-general variants: affirming relationships or family rather than abstract values, for younger learners

Target Learners¶

  • Students from groups stereotyped as underperforming in the domain (e.g., women in STEM, Black and Latino students in standardized testing) Self-affirmation reduces racial achievement gaps in threatening evaluative contexts. [+M]
  • First-generation college students and others facing belonging uncertainty in academic settings Belonging interventions improve outcomes for students at risk of belonging uncertainty. [+M]
  • High-achieving members of threatened groups benefit most; effects are minimal for unthreatened learners Self-affirmation effects depend on threat being present. [~M]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Not a content-learning goal: the target is performance under evaluation — accurate demonstration of existing competence
  • Equity outcomes: narrowing achievement gaps in gateway courses and standardized testing
  • Retention and persistence in threatening fields (e.g., STEM majors)

Instructions¶

  1. Schedule before the evaluative event. Administer in class or online 1–7 days before the exam; never after grades are returned.
  2. Present the values list. Offer 10–12 values (relationships with family/friends, creativity, independence, community, etc.) and ask learners to rank their top choices.
  3. Set the writing task. Learners write 10–15 minutes about why their top value(s) matter to them, with a specific incident if possible. The control group writes about a low-ranked value from a third-person perspective.
  4. Frame as universal. Present the activity to the whole class as a standard exercise about values — never as support "for students who struggle" (see Belonging).
  5. Pair with a supportive climate. Affirmation works inside courses that also use Assessment for Learning and growth-oriented Feedback; it cannot offset a threatening course culture alone.

Related Strategies¶

  • Belonging narrative intervention — a sibling social-psychological intervention; both target threat, and both require universal framing to avoid stigmatizing recipients
  • Growth mindset framing — often combined with affirmation in multi-component "wise interventions," though effects are independent

Examples¶

  • Cohen et al. (2009) — A 15-minute values-affirmation writing assignment administered in seventh-grade classrooms; Black students' grade point average improved and the racial achievement gap was reduced across the term (Science).
  • Miyake et al. (2010) — Values affirmation at the start of a university physics course substantially narrowed the gender gap in exam scores and course grades for women (Science).
  • PERTS / College Transition programs — Online social-belonging and affirmation modules used in gateway course redesigns (PERTS).
