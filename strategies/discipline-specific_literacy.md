# Discipline-Specific Literacy

> **Strategy** · [All strategies](index.md)

## Description
Discipline-specific literacy (also called disciplinary literacy) teaches students how texts are constructed, interpreted, and used within a particular field — how historians source documents, how scientists read data and methods sections, how mathematicians parse proofs. It rests on the finding that literacy skills are not generic: the strategies that make a strong reader in one discipline can be counterproductive in another [Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008](https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.78.1.n77784r701489l70) [+S]. Instruction typically pairs [mentor texts](mentor-texts.md) with explicit modeling of disciplinary reading and writing moves, followed by guided practice and feedback.

## Design Implications

Disciplinary literacy works because it makes the hidden conventions of expert text practices visible and teachable, rather than leaving students to infer them from exposure alone [Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008](https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.78.1.n77784r701489l70) [+M]. Because experts' tacit practices must first be surfaced, instructors benefit from [Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) modeling of how they actually read and write in their field.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Authentic mentor texts from the discipline (primary sources, journal articles, technical reports)
- Explicit modeling of disciplinary reasoning — how an expert approaches the text and why ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md))
- Discipline-specific graphic organizers, checklists, and vocabulary supports ([Advance Organizers](../elements/advance-organizers.md), [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md))
- Opportunities to write for [authentic audiences and purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) with [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) at the task and process levels [Feedback is most effective when directed at the task and process rather than the self.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]

#### Constraints
- Instructors are typically trained in content, not in their discipline's literacy practices; without professional development they default to generic "reading strategies" that do not reflect disciplinary norms [Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008](https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.78.1.n77784r701489l70) [-M]
- Generic comprehension strategies (summarizing, predicting) show diminishing returns for adolescents who already decode fluently; adding more generic strategy instruction on top of disciplinary work adds little [Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008](https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.78.1.n77784r701489l70) [~M]
- Mentor texts and exemplars are unevenly available across disciplines; in less text-rich fields, instructors may need to curate or author materials
- Overemphasis on discipline-specific conventions can narrow writing instruction; broad writing strategy instruction (planning, revising, sentence combining) carries much of the effect on writing quality [Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445) [+S]

#### Implementation Variability
- **Read–write–think–discuss cycles**: students read a mentor text, analyze its disciplinary moves, discuss, then produce their own text — reduces anxiety through collaborative engagement
- **Cognitive Apprenticeship framing**: instructor models, coaches, then fades support as students appropriate disciplinary practices [Scaffolding](../principles/scaffolding.md)
- **Multiple contrasting cases** of disciplinary texts (e.g., a news article vs. a research report on the same event) to make genre conventions visible [Cognitive Flexibility Theory](../patterns/cognitive-flexibility-theory.md)

### Target Learners
- Adolescent and post-secondary students who decode adequately but struggle with discipline-specific text structures and conventions [Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008](https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.78.1.n77784r701489l70) [+M]
- Novices to a discipline, who lack the schemas experts use to parse specialized texts; explicit modeling reduces unguided search [Worked examples reduce unnecessary search for novices.](../claims/worked-examples-reduce-novice-search.md) [+M]
- Students with reading difficulties need foundational supports first; disciplinary literacy supplements rather than replaces intensive intervention for struggling decoders [~M]

### Target Learning Goals
- Comprehension of discipline-specific texts and data representations
- Discipline-appropriate writing and argumentation (e.g., claim–evidence–reasoning in science)
- Epistemic understanding: how knowledge is created and warranted in a field
- Reduced anxiety and increased engagement with challenging course texts

### Instructions
1. Select an authentic mentor text exemplifying the target disciplinary practice.
2. Model expert reading/writing of the text aloud, narrating decisions and monitoring ([Think-Aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md), [Direct Instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md)).
3. Provide structured supports — graphic organizers, checklists, [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) routines, [Accessible Vocabulary & Syntax](../principles/accessible-vocabulary-syntax.md) scaffolds.
4. Have students apply the practice collaboratively, then independently ([Practice](../elements/practice.md), [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md)).
5. Give process-focused [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) and fade supports as competence grows [Fading support promotes transfer of responsibility.](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M].

## Related Strategies
- [Activating Prior Knowledge](../strategies/activating-prior-knowledge.md) — disciplinary comprehension depends heavily on relevant prior knowledge being retrieved before reading
- [Annotating](../principles/annotating.md) — a core disciplinary reading routine (e.g., sourcing and annotating in history)
- [Case-Based Learning](../patterns/case-based-learning.md) — uses authentic disciplinary materials as the object of analysis

## Examples
- **Reading Like a Historian** (Stanford History Education Group, [sheg.stanford.edu](https://sheg.stanford.edu)) — students source, contextualize, and corroborate primary documents, then write evidence-based arguments; extensively studied and freely available.
- **Science writing heuristic** approaches in secondary chemistry, where students write claim–evidence–reasoning explanations modeled on how scientists report findings.
- **Writing in the disciplines programs** in higher education (e.g., WID initiatives at Michigan State and Harvard), which pair faculty from across departments to articulate and teach field-specific writing conventions.
