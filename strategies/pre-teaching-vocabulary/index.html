Pre-Teaching Vocabulary¶

Strategy · All strategies Evidence · 7 claims (6 for, 1 against) · 19 studies (8 quant-synthesis, 6 causal, 4 review, 1 associational), q2–q4 · 7 of 19 report an effect size

Description¶

Pre-teaching vocabulary involves identifying the words most critical to understanding an upcoming text or lesson and explicitly introducing them — with student-friendly definitions, examples, and quick activities — before learners read or engage with the material. The goal is not exhaustive word study but targeted preparation: removing the specific lexical barriers that would otherwise derail comprehension. It is typically carried out through brief direct explanation, discussion of examples and non-examples, and quick checks for understanding.

Design Implications¶

Pre-teaching reduces the extraneous cognitive load of decoding unfamiliar words while simultaneously trying to construct meaning from a text Cognitive overload degrades learning. [+S]. Vocabulary instruction produces reliable but modest gains in comprehension, with the strongest effects when words are taught deeply (multiple encounters, varied contexts) rather than briefly defined Vocabulary instruction improves reading comprehension. [+M]. Because working memory cannot juggle novel word meanings and text-level inference at the same time, front-loading key terms functions as a form of Cognitive Load Management and pairs naturally with Advance Organizers as a preparation phase.

Context¶

Requirements¶

  • Selection of a small set of high-utility words — typically 5–10 — drawn from the text and chosen for how central they are to its meaning (Beck, McKeown, & Kucan's "Tier Two" criterion)
  • Student-friendly explanations with examples, Analogies, or visuals rather than dictionary definitions
  • Brief active engagement: learners say, sort, or apply the words, not just hear them
  • A follow-up encounter with the words in context (Assigned Readings, discussion, or writing)
  • Selection of a small set of high-utility words — typically 5–10 — that appear in the text and recur across contexts
  • Multiple meaningful encounters with each word before and during reading, ideally with Active Learning such as Class Discussion of usage
  • A follow-on reading or lesson where learners encounter the words in context

Constraints¶

  • Pre-teaching too many words dilutes instruction and overloads working memory Chunking reduces working memory load. [-M] — comprehensiveness is the enemy of effectiveness
  • Brief, definition-only pre-teaching yields weak comprehension gains; effects depend on depth of processing and multiple subsequent encounters Vocabulary instruction improves reading comprehension. [~M]
  • Pre-teaching can preempt productive word-learning from context: learners who never struggle with an unfamiliar word in context lose opportunities to infer meaning, a skill that itself needs development [~W]
  • Word selection is consequential — pre-teaching peripheral words consumes time without aiding comprehension of the text's main ideas
  • Teaching words in isolation from context produces knowledge that often fails to transfer to comprehension; definitions alone are insufficient [-M]
  • Effects on general vocabulary growth are small when words are taught only once and never revisited; without Spaced Repetition, gains fade [-M]
  • Learners with strong vocabularies gain little from pre-teaching words they can infer from context — the approach shows an expertise-reversal pattern for known words [~W]

Implementation Variability¶

  • Full pre-teaching: explicit introduction of all critical words before reading (suitable for dense informational texts)
  • Selective pre-teaching: teach only words unguessable from context; leave inferable words for during-reading strategy work
  • During-reading support instead: margin glosses, footnoted definitions, or just-in-time explanation — often as effective for digital texts where interruption is cheap
  • Rich instruction: extended routines such as word walls, semantic mapping, and Frayer-type four-square analysis across multiple days
  • Full pre-teaching: all critical words taught before reading with rich activities — appropriate for dense, unfamiliar texts
  • Selective pre-teaching: only words essential to the passage's main ideas are taught; other words are handled with marginal glosses or during-reading support
  • Preview-in-context: brief exposure to words within a related Advance Organizers or summary rather than isolated word study
  • Learner-generated: students predict meanings from morphology and context first, then compare with taught definitions — combining pre-teaching with Self-Explanation Self-explanation improves conceptual understanding. [+M]

Target Learners¶

  • Upper elementary through high school readers encountering discipline-specific or academic vocabulary beyond their current level
  • Struggling readers and English learners, for whom unaddressed vocabulary gaps compound into comprehension failure Graphic organizers support novice comprehension. [+M] — the same novice-protection logic applies to lexical scaffolds
  • Less necessary for proficient readers with strong background knowledge of the topic, who can infer most word meanings from context [~W]
  • Struggling readers and English language learners, who lack the vocabulary breadth to infer unfamiliar word meanings from context [+M]
  • Upper elementary through secondary students reading content-area texts with high densities of technical vocabulary
  • Less beneficial for proficient readers with strong background knowledge, for whom brief glosses suffice [~W]

Target Learning Goals¶

  • Reading comprehension: enabling learners to construct meaning from complex texts
  • Vocabulary acquisition: building durable word knowledge through multiple encounters
  • Background knowledge activation: connecting new terms to what learners already know Activation improves learning. [+M]
  • Reading comprehension of texts containing unfamiliar vocabulary [+M]
  • Word knowledge depth: multiple meaning facets, collocations, and morphological relatives
  • Activation of background knowledge and schema building before reading

Instructions¶

  1. Select 5–10 words that are (a) central to understanding the text and (b) unlikely to be known; skip words learners can infer from context.
  2. Explain each word in student-friendly language with an example, Analogy, or image — avoid dictionary-style definitions (Dual Coding supports recall Dual coding improves recall. [+M]).
  3. Engage learners actively: have them sort examples/non-examples, rate their familiarity, or use the word in a sentence.
  4. Check quickly that learners can recognize or use the words before reading begins.
  5. Revisit the words during and after reading through discussion and writing, so learners meet them repeatedly in context.

Related Strategies¶

  • Activating Prior Knowledge — the companion preparation move; vocabulary pre-teaching is often its lexical subset
  • Direct Instruction — the explicit-explanation format most pre-teaching takes
  • Annotating — a during-reading alternative when pre-teaching everything is impractical
  • Chunking — limiting the word set to a teachable chunk respects working memory limits
  • Spaced Repetition — revisiting pre-taught words across sessions converts initial exposure into durable word knowledge

Examples¶

  • Bringing Words to Life — Beck, McKeown, and Kucan's robust-vocabulary instruction framework, the standard reference for selecting Tier Two words and designing engaging introductions.
  • Text Talk (Beck & McKeown) — a published read-aloud routine that introduces sophisticated vocabulary from children's literature with repeated, decontextualized word study after initial exposure.
  • Reading Rockets — practitioner-facing guidance and video examples of pre-teaching vocabulary routines in elementary classrooms.
  • Beck & McKeown's Text Talk — a published instructional routine in which teachers pre-select Tier Two words from read-alouds and engage children in rich, interactive word discussion before and after reading.
  • Word Generation (https://wordgeneration.org) — a free middle-school curriculum that pre-teaches high-utility academic words weekly across subject areas with discussion and writing activities.
  • Content-area pre-reading in science — before a unit on photosynthesis, the teacher introduces chlorophyll, glucose, and stomata with diagrams and examples, so the subsequent reading supports concept integration rather than decoding unfamiliar terms.
