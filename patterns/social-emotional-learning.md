# Social Emotional Learning

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 38 claims (28 for, 10 mixed) · 53 studies (16 review, 15 causal, 14 quant-synthesis, 4 design, 2 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 17 of 53 report an effect size · 24 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable configuration for teaching named social and emotional skills (CASEL's five competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills and responsible decision-making) as part of school or programme life: **a predictable, relationally safe climate → explicit teaching of one named skill at a time → the adult models it aloud → learners rehearse it in low-stakes structured practice with feedback → they use it inside real academic and social work, coached in the moment → they reflect on what they did → the skill is cued across settings until it is used unprompted**. The intended change is in the skill itself (observed or rated social and emotional competence, a `skill-performance` and `attitude-motivation` target), and, more weakly, in conduct, emotional distress and academic achievement (`general-achievement`), at horizons from the end of a programme to years later.

**What the evidence tests, and what it does not.** Unlike most patterns in this wiki, this one is tested as a whole, at programme grain: meta-analyses of universal, school-based SEL programmes for kindergarten to grade 12 compare participating students with controls ([SEL programs improve social-emotional skills](../claims/sel-programs-improve-social-emotional-skills.md) [+S], [benefits persist at follow-up](../claims/sel-benefits-persist-follow-up.md) [+S], [and achievement, by a smaller and contested amount](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [~S]). Those programmes differ in content, and the syntheses report that programmes using four practices (sequenced, active, focused, explicit: "SAFE") and those without implementation problems did better. **No claim tests this page's seven steps, their order, or any one step against its absence**; no claim tests SEL with adults; and none compares integrating the skills into academic lessons with teaching them as stand-alone lessons. So the sequence below is the earlier page's, kept as the default, each step labelled with its evidence status, and the response-dependent branches are untested proposals.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this page. [Self-Regulated Learning](../principles/self-regulated-learning.md) (and its [pattern](self-regulated-learning.md)) owns planning, monitoring and acting on a learning task; this page's self-management strand overlaps it but targets emotion and conduct as well as study. [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) owns the perspective-taking model behind the social-awareness strand, including its limits on dose and horizon; this page does not repeat it. [Engagement](../principles/engagement.md) owns the claim that engagement is a mediator, not an outcome, which applies here too: a calmer, more engaged class is a route to learning only if the work it engages in is aligned. [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) owns the recurring self-report routine used in step 1, and [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md) the controlling side of rewards and praise. This page owns the programme-level configuration that combines them, and the evidence about SEL programmes as such.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Age or stage (`child`, `adolescent`, `adult`; the evidence is `pre-k-12`, with one synthesis of ages 2–6). Before teaching, observe the target skill in an ordinary situation: what a learner does when frustrated by a task, when a peer disagrees, when asked to join a group. Record the situation and who observed it (teacher, the learner, a peer, a parent), since ratings differ by informant. Note adversity, trauma or chronic stress where known, language and literacy, and neurodivergence, without reading an atypical emotional expression as a deficit. |
| The skill | Which competency and which named skill within it ("identify the emotion, then choose a strategy"; asking for help; disagreeing without insult). One skill per unit of teaching. Whether learners already have the words for it in the language of instruction. |
| Context and activities | Setting (`classroom`, `online-instructor-led`, `online-self-paced`, `workplace-clinical`), mode (all in the room, some remote, phone-only), `days-weeks` or `term-plus`, minutes a week available, group size and number of adults, **who will deliver it** (the learners' own teacher, other school staff or an outside provider), and whether the school's discipline and reward practice agrees or conflicts with what is taught. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask what the learner wants from it: fewer fights with a particular friend, to stop panicking in tests, to be picked for a team, to handle patients or customers. Adolescents and adults especially may not value "SEL" as named. Record disagreement with the designer objective. |
| Designer objective | Which outcome is the target: the skill itself, conduct (referrals, suspensions, disruption), emotional distress, belonging, or academic achievement. Name the one the evaluation will weigh: the evidence says they move by very different amounts. |
| Outcome | An observed or performance measure of the skill in a new situation, beside any self-report; conduct and achievement measured separately; immediate and `delayed` (a term or a year later). Say whether the measure was written by the programme (aligned and closer to the intervention) or is independent. |

A request to "add SEL" or "build students' resilience" does not specify these. Ask which skill, for which learners, delivered by whom, how many minutes a week for how long, and measured how, before choosing a curriculum, a lesson order or a routine.

## Sequence and conditional policy

The default sequence is the earlier page's. The SAFE practices reported as moderators in the programme syntheses bear on it as a whole (steps 2 and 4 make it explicit and active; one skill at a time makes it focused; the order makes it sequenced); no step is tested on its own. Durations and progressions are **untested proposals** unless a claim is cited.

**Dose across a programme (untested proposal).** One explicit lesson of 20–30 minutes a week for each named skill, for at least a school term per competency, with 2–5 minutes of daily cueing and use inside ordinary lessons; plan for a full school year, and repeat with new skills in following years. In the first two or three weeks of a skill, the adult supplies the scenarios, scripts and sentence starters and models every step; in the middle weeks, learners rehearse with scenarios drawn from their own class life and the adult coaches; in the last weeks, learners choose the situations, prompts are withdrawn one at a time, and the adult only cues. A learner moves on from a skill when they use it unprompted in two different settings (for example in group work and at break) on two separate occasions, observed by an adult or reported by a peer; a learner who still needs the prompt keeps the script while the class moves on. From the first week of each skill, send the other adults who see the learners (and, in early childhood, families) a short note naming the skill, its steps and one way to cue it, so the cueing in step 7 is shared rather than left to one teacher.

1. **Climate-setting: predictable routines and relational safety.** Greet learners individually; run a short, regular [check-in](../elements/check-in.md); co-author a few class norms. Proposal: daily, 2–5 minutes, from the first week and throughout. Evidence status: [positive greetings at the door](../claims/positive-greetings-at-the-door-improve-engagement.md) [+M] raised academic engaged time and reduced disruption in a randomized evaluation with middle-school teachers against an attention control; it is a climate step tested on engagement, not on social and emotional skill. In one national youth-programme evaluation, facilitators and youth described adult [co-regulation](../claims/co-regulation-supports-climate-and-youth-emotion-regulation.md) [+W] (a short shared breathing routine at the start of class, calm adult responses) as making the room feel safe for questions and helping youth manage high emotions: focus-group accounts, not a comparison. The check-in is untested here (see the [Check-ins principle](../principles/check-ins.md)).
2. **Explicit skill instruction.** Name and teach one skill in steps ("notice the feeling, name it, choose a strategy, try it") through [direct instruction](direct-instruction.md) and [demonstration](../elements/coaching.md). Proposal: 20–30 minutes once a week. Evidence status: carried from the programme syntheses' SAFE moderator ([SEL skills](../claims/sel-programs-improve-social-emotional-skills.md) [+S]). The one recorded test of explicit teaching against its absence is small: in a randomized comparison of six preschool classrooms of children with developmental delays (38 children), a social-skills curriculum (instruct, model, practise) plus reinforcement raised observed sharing well above reinforcement alone (ES 1.12), while time spent in a group rose by a non-significant amount (ES 0.28) ([Taking Part training](../claims/taking-part-training-increases-sharing-behaviors.md) [+W]). For the naming step, [emotion labelling](../claims/emotion-labeling-improves-regulation.md) [~M] reduced physiological fear a week later, but not self-reported fear, in spider-fearful adults under exposure, a clinical laboratory study, not a classroom one.
3. **Modelling.** The adult narrates their own regulation and social decisions aloud during real moments ("I'm getting frustrated with this projector, so I'm going to take a breath and try the other cable"). Proposal: whenever a genuine moment arises, at least once a lesson in the first weeks. Evidence status: untested as a step; the [co-regulation accounts](../claims/co-regulation-supports-climate-and-youth-emotion-regulation.md) [+W] above describe adults regulating with youth, reported experience only.
4. **Guided practice with feedback.** Learners rehearse the skill in low-stakes structured activities: [role-play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md), [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) of scenarios, and structured [cooperative learning](cooperative-learning.md). Give feedback on what the learner did and how (the task and the strategy), not praise of the person. Proposal: 10–15 minutes of the weekly lesson, scenarios supplied early and drawn from learners' own situations later. Evidence status: the "active" SAFE practice in the syntheses ([SEL skills](../claims/sel-programs-improve-social-emotional-skills.md) [+S]); in a meta-analysis of 104 secondary-school programmes, as summarised in a policy review, programmes giving students practice of the skill during the sessions were among the more effective ([SEL moderators](../claims/sel-effects-moderators-implementation-delivery.md) [+M]); for group practice, [group rewards resting on each member's learning](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M] raised achievement in school cooperative methods, carried here to social practice it did not test; for the feedback, [task- and process-level feedback](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M], carried from feedback on learning tasks to feedback on social behaviour.
5. **Embedded application.** Learners use the skill inside real academic work (peer feedback, collaborative problem-solving, a group project), with the adult [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) in the moment. Proposal: in at least two ordinary lessons a week, the skill named in the lesson plan. Evidence status: untested as a step. In the syntheses, programmes taught by learners' own classroom teachers showed significant skill and academic effects where programmes run by non-school personnel did not ([SEL and achievement](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [~S]); that is a delivery finding, not a test of embedding. A design-principles report argues for teaching social and emotional skills alongside subject content ([integrated skills](../claims/integrated-skills-habits-mindsets-instruction.md) [+W]), from a neuroscience rationale with no comparison.
6. **Reflection.** Learners name what they did and what they would do differently, through short structured [reflection](../elements/reflection.md) prompts or journals, privately as well as aloud. Proposal: 3–5 minutes at the end of the weekly lesson and after any coached incident. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
7. **Reinforcement across settings, with the whole staff and families, then fading.** Cue and acknowledge the skill in corridors, group work, assessment situations and at home; tell the other adults who see the learners (subject teachers, support staff, families) which skill is being taught this term and the words used for it, so they cue it the same way; remove prompts one at a time once a learner uses the skill unprompted (the moving-on rule above). Evidence status: untested as a step. The [follow-up synthesis](../claims/sel-benefits-persist-follow-up.md) [+S] shows programme gains outlasting the programme (6 months to 18 years after) but does not say which programmes reinforced across settings. In the syntheses summarised by [Greenberg (SEL moderators)](../claims/sel-effects-moderators-implementation-delivery.md) [~M], secondary programmes involving the entire school staff, and preschool programmes combining family and school components, did better than those without; a classroom teacher alone was weaker in the preschool synthesis. A brief reports one study in which students used [mindfulness-based conflict-resolution strategies with siblings and friends](../claims/mindfulness-conflict-resolution-transfer-out-of-school.md) [+W] outside school, a second-hand account with no design details.

## Fitting the design to a situation

**The three facts that most change the decision:**

- **Who will deliver it, who else will cue it, and how often.** The older school-age synthesis found skill and academic effects for programmes run by learners' own teachers and not, or not significantly, for programmes run by outside personnel or as multicomponent programmes, and implementation problems lowered effects. Later syntheses, summarised in a policy review, qualify "the teacher alone": in preschool, family-and-school programmes did better and a classroom teacher alone did worse, and in secondary school the more effective programmes were longer and involved the entire staff ([SEL moderators](../claims/sel-effects-moderators-implementation-delivery.md) [~M]). So the default is the learners' own teacher teaching it, with the other adults around them cueing it. If unstated, ask: will the learners' own teacher teach and cue this every week, for how long, and who else (staff, families) will know the skill?
- **Which outcome the brief is buying.** Social and emotional skills moved most; conduct, distress and attitudes less (across twelve meta-analyses, conduct problems ES 0.13–0.33 and emotional distress 0.10–0.31: [behaviour and distress](../claims/sel-programs-reduce-behavior-problems-distress.md) [+M]); academic achievement least, and in rigorous recent trials by a negligible amount. If unstated, ask: what result would count as success, and how will it be measured?
- **Age or stage, and whether the learners chose to be there.** The evidence is from school children and adolescents (and one synthesis of ages 2–6); nothing here tests adults. If unstated, ask: how old are the learners, and will they see this as theirs or as something done to them?

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **The programme as a whole, on the skills.** [SEL programs improve social-emotional skills](../claims/sel-programs-improve-social-emotional-skills.md) [+S]: across 213 universal school-based programmes for K–12 students, social and emotional skills improved most at post-test (mean ES = 0.57 over 68 interventions, against 0.22–0.27 for attitudes, positive social behaviour, conduct problems, emotional distress and academic performance); skill effects were significant for classroom programmes run by teachers (0.62) and not for multicomponent programmes; the SAFE practices and implementation problems moderated outcomes; heterogeneity was high (I² = 91%). In centre-based early childhood (ages 2–6, 63 studies), universal curricula improved social competence (d = 0.30) and emotional competence (d = 0.54), and early learning skills by less (d = 0.18). Both are syntheses against controls, mostly at post-test; skill measures include programme-aligned ones.
- **Behaviour and achievement.** [Universal school-based SEL programs improve students' behaviour, with a small and, in recent randomised trials, negligible effect on academic achievement](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [+S] and [SEL Programs Improve Academic Achievement](../claims/sel-programs-improve-behavior-and-achievement.md) [~S] carry the same three syntheses. The older one reports academic performance ES 0.27 (35 interventions; the authors translate it as 11 percentile points), significant only when school staff delivered the programme (teacher delivery 0.34; non-school personnel 0.12, not significant). A contemporary synthesis of 424 studies reports improved behaviours, school functioning and achievement (abstract only, no effect size read). A synthesis restricted to randomised or rigorous quasi-experimental studies with elementary and middle-school students found overall achievement g = 0.08, g = 0.063 in randomised trials, larger for GPA (g = 0.33). A policy review of twelve SEL meta-analyses gives the same ordering: academic performance ES 0.18–0.28 ([engagement and academic performance](../claims/sel-programs-improve-academic-performance.md) [+M]), conduct problems 0.13–0.33 and emotional distress 0.10–0.31 ([behaviour and distress](../claims/sel-programs-reduce-behavior-problems-distress.md) [+M]); the "11 percentile points" repeated in that review, in another report and in a charter-school brief ([schoolwide SEL and achievement](../claims/schoolwide-sel-meta-analytic-11-percentile-achievement-gain.md) [+W]) is Durlak et al.'s figure retold, not further studies. This is the main limit on the pattern: plan SEL for its social and emotional outcomes, and treat an achievement gain as possible and small.
- **Durability.** [Social-emotional learning benefits persist at follow-up](../claims/sel-benefits-persist-follow-up.md) [+S]: a synthesis of 82 universal school-based programmes found participants ahead of controls on skills, attitudes and well-being 6 months to 18 years later, similar across race, socioeconomic status and school location (abstract only, no pooled effect read); a randomized trial of an enriched Head Start curriculum with 356 low-income preschoolers found advantages on 7 of 11 well-being measures at age 21–22 (d = .21 to .33). The older synthesis's follow-up studies still showed academic effects (ES 0.32, k = 8).
- **The adults' side.** [Brief intervention empathic discipline cuts suspensions](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M]: a brief online exercise for middle-school mathematics teachers halved year-long suspensions in one randomized field experiment (9.6% to 4.8%); a preregistered, active-placebo replication found a small, marginal overall effect and narrowed the Black/Hispanic disparity from 10.6 to 5.9 percentage points. It targets teachers' responses to students, not students' skills, and its outcome is an administrative record.
- **Programme and delivery features.** [Program and delivery characteristics moderate SEL effects](../claims/sel-effects-moderators-implementation-delivery.md) [~M]: a policy review's summary of two meta-analyses. In 104 secondary-school studies, the more effective programmes were longer, included insight building and problem-solving, gave practice during sessions and involved the whole staff; in 33 preschool studies, family-and-school programmes did better and programmes delivered by a classroom teacher alone did worse. Higher implementation quality went with stronger outcomes, and stronger designs gave somewhat smaller effects. These are moderator analyses across studies, not tests of adding one feature to one programme; they qualify, rather than contradict, the older synthesis's finding for own-teacher delivery over outside personnel.
- **Explicit teaching against reinforcement alone.** [Taking Part training](../claims/taking-part-training-increases-sharing-behaviors.md) [+W]: the only recorded comparison of explicit social-skills teaching with its absence, a randomized trial in six preschool classrooms (38 children with developmental delays): sharing ES 1.12, being in a group ES 0.28, not significant. Small, early-childhood and disability-specific.
- **The surrounding climate.** [SEL programs cannot produce long-term growth in punitive or exclusionary environments](../claims/sel-requires-educative-inclusive-environments.md) [~W] is a policy report's assertion, with no study. The nearest tested evidence is from belonging interventions at college entry ([belonging interventions](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [~M]), where a brief message raised first-year completion only at institutions that afforded students' groups opportunities to belong. No claim in the wiki tests an SEL programme in a punitive against a supportive school climate.
- **One step's mechanism.** [Emotion Labeling Improves Regulation](../claims/emotion-labeling-improves-regulation.md) [~M]: 88 spider-fearful adults randomly assigned to affect labelling, reappraisal, distraction or exposure alone; labelling gave a larger drop in skin conductance a week later (d = 0.64–0.85) and no difference in self-reported fear. It supports naming emotions as one part of a routine, not as a classroom effect.

Do not rank SEL against academic instruction from these claims: none compares the two for time spent, and the achievement effects are small and contested. Do not read rising self-report, or a calm SEL lesson, as the skill attained. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (this sequence, or one step of it, against the same programme without it, on an observed skill measure a term later) and the local observation that would stand in for it.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested design.* A Year 7 form tutor (`adolescent` learners, 28 in the class, `classroom`, one 25-minute tutor period a week plus 10 minutes each morning) is asked to "do SEL" after a term of arguments in group work. The table changed the default: she chooses one skill, disagreeing without insult, and frames it around fairness rather than "managing feelings"; the morning slot carries a greeting and a written one-word check-in; the weekly period carries the explicit lesson and paired role-play, with scenarios she writes for the first three weeks and pupils write after that; the science and English teachers name the skill in one group task each a week and note who uses it unprompted. One pupil says he wants to stop being sent out of maths; the tutor's objective is fewer group-work conflicts. They agree to track both. After six weeks she compares her incident log, the subject teachers' notes and the pupils' own ratings, and plans to look again at the end of the year. Nothing in the claims tests this configuration with this age group at this dose; these are local choices to observe.

Record: **skill and competency → learners, age and starting observation, with informant → who delivers, minutes a week and for how long → scenarios used and who chose them → each learner's observed use of the skill, prompted or unprompted, by setting and date → feedback and coaching given → self-report beside observed or rated measures → conduct and achievement records, kept separate → candidate interpretations (skill not known, known but not cued, situation too intense, framing rejected, adults' responses driving incidents) and their basis → reobservation at the stated horizon**. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs, diagnose a learner, or assign numerical probabilities of social or emotional competence without a calibrated measure.

[Check-ins](../elements/check-in.md), [coaching](../elements/coaching.md), [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [reflection](../elements/reflection.md), [belonging](../elements/belonging.md) and [role-play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md); the strategies [SEL curricula](../strategies/social-emotional-learning-curricula.md), [SEL routines](../strategies/social-emotional-learning-routines.md), [SEL integration](../strategies/social-emotional-learning-integration.md) and the ['What's my emotion?' check-in](../strategies/whats_my_emotion_game_check-in.md). Principles the pattern draws on: [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md), [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md), [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md), [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md), [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md), [Self-Regulated Learning](../principles/self-regulated-learning.md), [Engagement](../principles/engagement.md) and [Autonomy](../principles/autonomy.md). Learner variables: [belonging](../learner-variables/belonging.md) and [self-regulation](../learner-variables/self-regulation.md).

This pattern is scoped to sustained, explicit teaching and reinforcement of named social and emotional skills, mostly in schools. A one-off workshop, a whole-school behaviour policy, clinical treatment of anxiety or trauma, and professional formation for adults need other configurations or additional evidence. The policy supports observation and design reasoning; which of its steps matter, at what weekly dose, for adults, and against what achievement cost in teaching time, remains to be tested.

## Related Patterns
- [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) — depends on the relationship skills SEL develops; the two patterns are mutually reinforcing when group work carries individual accountability
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — cases with interpersonal and ethical dimensions provide authentic contexts for practicing decision-making and perspective-taking
- [Formative Assessment](formative-assessment.md) — its feedback culture overlaps with SEL's self-management goal; both depend on students interpreting feedback as information, not judgment
- [Self-Regulated Learning](self-regulated-learning.md) — owns planning, monitoring and acting on a learning task; SEL's self-management strand overlaps it and extends to emotion and conduct
- [Direct Instruction](direct-instruction.md) — the format of the explicit skill lesson in step 2
- [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) — both treat social presence as a precondition for deep engagement, though RC operates at classroom-routine grain rather than discourse grain

## Examples

**[PATHS](https://www.pathseducation.com) (Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies):** A widely evaluated K–6 curriculum teaching emotion labeling, self-control, and problem-solving through scripted lessons plus "Feelings Fair" classroom integration; central to the Durlak et al. evidence base.

**[Second Step](https://www.secondstep.org):** A K–8 SEL curriculum (Committee for Children) using weekly lessons, puppet-based modeling in early grades, and peer-narrated video scenarios in middle school, with skills reinforced through teacher cueing during academic work.

**RULER approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence):** Teaches Recognizing, Understanding, Labeling, Expressing, and Regulating emotion through tools like the Mood Meter; implemented school-wide so adults and students use a shared emotional vocabulary.

**Medical education — reflective practice seminars:** Residency programs use structured case discussions and Balint-style groups to develop self-awareness, empathy, and relationship skills with patients — SEL competencies reframed as professional formation.
- **[Center for Responsive Schools](https://www.responsiveschool.org)** — the developer organization; publishes RC professional development, the *Responsive Classroom* course sequence, and implementation fidelity tools used in the evaluation studies below.
- **Fairfield, Connecticut elementary schools (i3-scale RCT):** A large randomized controlled trial across 24 elementary schools found significant gains in math and reading achievement and improved teacher efficacy in schools implementing RC with fidelity (Ottmar et al., 2017).
- **Morning Meeting in a 3rd-grade classroom:** 25-minute daily meeting — greeting around the circle, two student shares with peer questions, a quick cooperative game, and preview of the day's learning goals — followed by interactive modeling of the new math-station routine.

## Description
Social Emotional Learning is a framework for developing five interrelated competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making (CASEL, 2020). SEL operates through two channels — explicit instruction in named skills (e.g., emotion labeling, conflict resolution) and integration of those skills into academic content and classroom routines. The pattern addresses the problem that academic learning depends on emotional and interpersonal conditions: students who cannot regulate distress, persist through frustration, or collaborate productively learn less regardless of instructional quality.

## Implications

SEL is grounded in the finding that social and emotional competencies predict academic and life outcomes, and that these competencies are teachable rather than fixed traits. A meta-analysis of 213 school-based programs found consistent improvements in social-emotional skills, attitudes, and academic achievement (+11 percentile points) [Durlak et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x) [+S], with effects persisting years after program completion [Taylor et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12864) [+S]. Because emotion and cognition are intertwined in learning — anxiety consumes working memory, belonging shapes persistence — SEL functions as a precondition pattern that raises the yield of other instructional patterns.

### Context
#### Requirements
- A safe, predictable classroom climate; skills practiced in threatening environments do not transfer
- Explicit, named instruction in specific skills (e.g., "identify the emotion, choose a strategy"), not only general climate work
- Repeated practice embedded in authentic academic and social situations, not isolated lessons alone
- Adult modeling: teachers demonstrating regulation and relationship skills themselves

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Climate-setting** — Establish predictable routines and relational safety with regular [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) and community norms co-authored with students
2. **Explicit skill instruction** — Name and teach a specific competency (e.g., emotion labeling, help-seeking) through [direct instruction](../patterns/direct-instruction.md) and [demonstration](../elements/coaching.md) by the teacher
3. **Modeling** — Teacher narrates their own emotional regulation and social decision-making, making internal processes visible
4. **Guided practice** — Students rehearse the skill in low-stakes structured activities: [role-play](../strategies/acting-role-play.md), [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), and structured [cooperative learning](../patterns/cooperative-learning.md) with individual accountability [Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves outcomes.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]
5. **Embedded application** — Students apply the skill inside authentic academic work (peer feedback, collaborative problem-solving), with the teacher [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) in the moment
6. **Reflection** — Students name what they did and what they would do differently through structured [reflection](../elements/reflection.md) prompts or journals
7. **Reinforcement across settings** — Skills are cued and reinforced in multiple contexts (hallway, group work, assessment situations) until they become default behavior

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — SEL supplies the relationship skills (turn-taking, conflict resolution, perspective-taking) that make collaborative structures productive rather than merely busy
- [Check-ins](../principles/check-ins.md) — routine emotional temperature-taking normalizes emotion talk and gives teachers actionable data on classroom climate
- [Building Empathy](../principles/building-empathy.md) — the social-awareness competency is enacted directly through perspective-taking routines, literature discussion, and structured contact across difference
- [Clear Structure](../principles/clear-structure.md) — predictable routines and explicit expectations reduce the ambient cognitive and emotional load that competes with learning
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — process-level, growth-oriented feedback supports self-management without the ego threat that undermines it

### Personalization

**Students with trauma histories or chronic stress:** Prioritize regulation skills and predictability before social skills; avoid public emotional disclosure activities that can be re-traumatizing. Offer private reflection channels alongside group ones.

**Adolescents:** Frame SEL around autonomy, identity, and real stakes (friendship, fairness, future goals) rather than compliance. Peer-led discussion and student-chosen scenarios outperform teacher-scripted lessons at this age [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]

**Students with language or learning differences:** Supplement verbal emotion vocabulary with visual supports (emotion scales, choice cards); extend practice cycles and reduce the pace of fading.

**Neurodivergent learners:** Explicit, systematic instruction in social scripts is often helpful rather than redundant — unlike neurotypical peers, they may not acquire these skills implicitly. Avoid interpreting atypical emotional expression as skill deficits.

**Diverse cultural contexts:** Adapt scenarios, emotion vocabulary, and norms for emotional expression to the community; involve families and students in defining what competent behavior looks like locally.

## Related Patterns
- [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) — depends on the relationship skills SEL develops; the two patterns are mutually reinforcing when group work carries individual accountability
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — cases with interpersonal and ethical dimensions provide authentic contexts for practicing decision-making and perspective-taking
- [Formative Assessment](formative-assessment.md) — its feedback culture overlaps with SEL's self-management goal; both depend on students interpreting feedback as information, not judgment

## Examples

**[PATHS](https://www.pathseducation.com) (Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies):** A widely evaluated K–6 curriculum teaching emotion labeling, self-control, and problem-solving through scripted lessons plus "Feelings Fair" classroom integration; central to the Durlak et al. evidence base.

**[Second Step](https://www.secondstep.org):** A K–8 SEL curriculum (Committee for Children) using weekly lessons, puppet-based modeling in early grades, and peer-narrated video scenarios in middle school, with skills reinforced through teacher cueing during academic work.

**RULER approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence):** Teaches Recognizing, Understanding, Labeling, Expressing, and Regulating emotion through tools like the Mood Meter; implemented school-wide so adults and students use a shared emotional vocabulary.

**Medical education — reflective practice seminars:** Residency programs use structured case discussions and Balint-style groups to develop self-awareness, empathy, and relationship skills with patients — SEL competencies reframed as professional formation.

# Responsive Classroom

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 5 claims (5 for) · 12 studies (4 causal, 4 review, 3 quant-synthesis, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 12 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Responsive Classroom (RC) is a research- and evidence-based social-emotional learning (SEL) approach for K–8 classrooms developed by the Northeast Foundation for Children. It treats social skills and academic learning as inseparable: teachers use structured routines — most notably the daily [Morning Meeting](../elements/check-in.md), rule creation with students, interactive modeling, academic choice, and positive teacher language — to build a caring classroom community in which academic engagement can flourish. The approach solves the problem of treating behavior management and instruction as separate systems by embedding relationship-building and self-regulation into the same daily structures that deliver content.

## Implications

RC rests on the finding that students' sense of belonging and autonomy drives both motivation and achievement: students learn more when they feel known, have voice in classroom norms, and experience [choice](../elements/choice-boards.md) in their learning [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]. Belonging interventions and community-building routines produce measurable gains in outcomes, particularly for students at risk of disengagement [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]. Proactive, relationship-based discipline — teaching expectations explicitly through interactive modeling rather than reacting with punishment — reduces exclusionary discipline, with empathic relationship-building shown to cut suspensions [Brief empathic discipline intervention cuts suspensions.](../claims/brief-intervention-empathic-discipline-cuts-suspensions.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Daily time protected for community routines (Morning Meeting, closing circle) — typically 20–30 minutes
- Teacher training in RC practices; fidelity matters more than partial adoption
- School-wide consistency for full effect; isolated classroom adoption yields weaker results
- Small enough class sizes to give every student a voice in meetings and rule creation

#### Constraints
- Requires significant instructional time; schools under intense test-pressure regimes often cut meeting time first [~M]
- Effects depend on implementation fidelity — low-fidelity adoption shows little benefit [~M]
- Designed for K–8 developmental range; routines need substantial adaptation (and lose evidence support) at secondary level
- Whole-school adoption demands sustained professional development; one-off workshops do not produce lasting practice change [-M]

#### Grain Size
Lesson-level routines (Morning Meeting, academic choice) nested within a school-wide program; strongest evidence comes from multi-year whole-school implementation.

### Target Goals
- Social-emotional competencies: cooperation, assertiveness, responsibility, empathy, self-control (CARES)
- Academic engagement and on-task behavior
- Positive school climate and reduced exclusionary discipline
- Student voice and ownership of classroom norms [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]

### Target Learners
- K–8 students, originally developed for elementary classrooms
- Particularly beneficial for students at risk of exclusionary discipline or weak school belonging [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]
- Teachers seeking an integrated alternative to separate behavior-management and SEL curricula

### Theory
#### Supporting

- [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) (Deci & Ryan) — student rule creation, academic choice, and positive teacher language support the autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs that drive intrinsic motivation
- [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) (Bandura) — interactive modeling teaches social and academic behaviors through observation and rehearsal rather than rules alone
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — students co-construct classroom norms and learning goals rather than receiving them
- [Students' success in school and beyond is inextricably linked to healthy social and emotional development](../claims/school-success-linked-social-emotional-development.md) [+W]

- [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md) — RC deliberately replaces reward/punishment contingency management with logical consequences and community accountability; strict behavioral systems conflict with its teacher-language practices

## Design

### Sequence
A typical RC day/week follows this practice architecture:

1. **Morning Meeting** — daily [check-in](../elements/check-in.md) with greeting, sharing, group activity, and news/announcement; builds community and sets academic purpose
2. **Interactive Modeling** — teacher models a behavior or routine, students observe, then rehearse and give feedback ([demonstration](../elements/articulation.md) plus peer feedback rather than rule announcement)
3. **Co-created Rules** — students generate hopes and dreams; class rules derive from them, giving students ownership of norms
4. **Academic Choice** — lessons offer structured [choice](../elements/choice-boards.md) in how to practice or demonstrate learning (e.g., choice of product, partner, or problem set)
5. **Positive Teacher Language** — reinforcing, reminding, and redirecting language that names specific behaviors rather than judging the child
6. **Logical Consequences** — misbehavior met with repair-and-restore responses ("you break it, you fix it," loss of privilege tied to the behavior, take-a-break) instead of punitive sanctions
7. **Closing Circle / Reflection** — end-of-day [reflection](../elements/reflection.md) consolidating learning and community

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — Morning Meeting sharing and group activity structures make peer interaction a daily, taught routine rather than an occasional activity
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — academic choice and interactive modeling require students to do, rehearse, and respond rather than passively receive rules or content
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — teacher language and closing reflections give continuous, low-stakes feedback loops within the school day
- [Belonging](../elements/belonging.md) — greetings by name, sharing, and co-created norms directly build each student's sense of being known and valued

### Personalization

**Young learners (K–2):** Shorten Morning Meeting to 10–15 minutes; use songs, movement, and props in greetings; make rules concrete and pictorial.

**Upper elementary / middle grades (5–8):** Shift sharing toward academic discourse; let students lead meeting components; use advisory-style meetings to fit adolescent identity needs.

**Students with behavioral challenges:** Use proactive [check-ins](../elements/check-in.md) and take-a-break spaces before escalation; pair logical consequences with explicit re-teaching of the expectation through interactive modeling.

**Students with language differences or disabilities:** Provide visual schedules and sentence frames for sharing; allow nonverbal participation in greetings; apply [accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md) within, not instead of, the community routines.

**Low-belonging or newcomer students:** Assign consistent greeting partners and structured sharing roles so every student is addressed by name daily — the routine itself functions as a belonging intervention [Belonging interventions improve outcomes.](../claims/belonging-interventions-improve-outcomes.md) [+M]

## Related Patterns
- [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) — shares the premise that structured peer interaction drives both social and academic outcomes; RC supplies the community routines that make cooperative structures sustainable
- [Direct Instruction](direct-instruction.md) — RC is complementary rather than opposed: interactive modeling applies explicit teaching to social skills, while academic content can still be delivered through direct instruction
- [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) — both treat social presence as a precondition for deep engagement, though RC operates at classroom-routine grain rather than discourse grain

## Examples
- **[Center for Responsive Schools](https://www.responsiveschool.org)** — the developer organization; publishes RC professional development, the *Responsive Classroom* course sequence, and implementation fidelity tools used in the evaluation studies below.
- **Fairfield, Connecticut elementary schools (i3-scale RCT):** A large randomized controlled trial across 24 elementary schools found significant gains in math and reading achievement and improved teacher efficacy in schools implementing RC with fidelity (Ottmar et al., 2017).
- **Morning Meeting in a 3rd-grade classroom:** 25-minute daily meeting — greeting around the circle, two student shares with peer questions, a quick cooperative game, and preview of the day's learning goals — followed by interactive modeling of the new math-station routine.
