# Constructive Alignment

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 10 claims (5 for, 5 mixed) · 13 studies (5 quant-synthesis, 3 theoretical, 2 causal, 2 review, 1 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 6 of 13 report an effect size · 7 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable course- or programme-level policy for checking that what learners are told to achieve (intended outcomes), what they spend their time doing (activities) and what they are scored on (assessment tasks) call for the same performance, and for repairing the system where they do not. It instantiates the converted [Assessment for Learning principle](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md), which models how assessment evidence is used during instruction (shared criteria, stakes that let partial understanding show, a revision step), and it depends on the judgement modelled by the converted [Competency-Based Assessment principle](../principles/competency-based-assessment.md): whether a task represents the capability, whether its threshold is defensible, and whether assessors agree. This page owns the more general relationship around both: **whether the outcome, the practice and the assessment are about the same capability at the same level**. It does not repeat either sibling's model; use them for the reading of a single response and for the quality of a single judgement.

The relationship the policy assumes is this: for learners working towards a capability they have not yet reached, when the assessed task demands the capability named in the outcome, and the activities give repeated practice of that capability with feedback before the assessed task, performance on the assessment (`general-achievement`, or `skill-performance` for a `complex-skill`) is expected to be higher, and learners' study effort is expected to go to the intended capability rather than to whatever the assessment happens to reward. **No claim in the wiki tests alignment as such**: none compares an aligned course with a misaligned one, or measures whether realigning a course shifts learners from surface to deep study. The claims below test parts of it (practice of the assessed content, active work against lecture, assessment used during learning, stated objectives) and one qualifies its central "same verb" rule. The study configurations are evidence; the policy, the audit and the table of responses are **untested design proposals**. Biggs (1996), the source of the pattern, is in the frontmatter; it has no claim page here, so nothing below rests on it.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Intended outcomes as written | The outcomes the course actually states, verbatim, with their verbs and conditions. Topic lists ("know about", "understand") are recorded as such; they are an observation, not yet an outcome. |
| What is assessed, and who controls it | Every graded task, its weight, its format, its criteria, and whether the designer can change it (an external examination, an accreditation rubric or a shared departmental exam usually cannot be changed). |
| What learners actually do | The activities and the time spent on each, from the timetable or a week of observation, not from the syllabus; which of them produce a response the teacher sees. |
| Learners and starting observation | Population band (`pre-k-12`, `postsecondary`, `adult-workplace`), expertise on the course's tasks (`novice`, `intermediate`, `advanced`), and a short sample of independent work on a representative task. Ask how learners have prepared for past assessments in this subject. |
| Goal kind and level | `verbal-association`, `concept`, `procedure`, `principle`, `complex-skill` or `attitude-motivation`, and the level at which it must be performed (recall, application, analysis, design). |
| Learner-valued goal | What learners want from the course (a pass, a credential, a job skill, interest), kept apart from the designer's objective. |
| Grain and time | Programme, course or unit; the number of weeks; how many outcomes the course carries. |
| Outcome and horizon | The instrument that will show the capability, a locally chosen criterion, and whether a later use (the next course, the workplace) is a target. |

A request to "align the course" does not specify these inputs. Ask for them before rewriting outcomes or tasks. If the assessment cannot be changed, record that first: it decides which half of the policy below is available.

## Sequence and conditional policy

1. **Audit before redesigning.** Build a three-column grid with one row per intended outcome: the outcome's verb and conditions, the activities that require that performance (with the class time each takes), and the assessed items that require it (with their weight). Mark every outcome with no activity, every outcome with no assessment, and every assessed item that matches no outcome. Untested proposal; from the earlier page's requirement that outcomes, activities and assessment "point in the same direction".
2. **Fix the outcomes first, and keep them few.** Rewrite topic statements as performances with an action verb and conditions. Aim for a short list per course (the earlier page warned that "long checklists of granular outcomes fragment learning"; it gave no number, so a figure such as five to eight per course is an untested proposal). Write them at the level the course is meant to reach: alignment amplifies whatever level the outcomes specify, so an outcome written as "list" aligns the whole course to listing. Untested; from the earlier page.
3. **Give each outcome practice with feedback before it is assessed.** At least one activity per outcome in which learners perform it and get feedback before the graded task; more for a `complex-skill`. Evidence: including practice with feedback raised posttest scores in a randomised study of a computer-based lesson ([practice presence](../claims/practice-presence-raises-cbi-posttest-achievement.md) [+M]); active work against lecture in undergraduate STEM ([active learning](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]). The number of practice episodes per outcome is untested.
4. **Practise at or above the assessed level, not below it.** Where the assessment asks for recall or comprehension, practice at a higher level can still serve it: in one course, higher-order clicker questions produced better lower-order exam performance than lower-order ones ([HOCS clickers](../claims/hocs-clickers-improve-locs-exam-performance.md) [~M]). Read "same verb" as "the assessed performance must be practised", not "practice must be restricted to the assessed level".
5. **Make the assessed task demand the outcome, and grade against criteria.** Replace items that can be answered by recall where the outcome asks for analysis or design; publish the criteria; grade against them rather than by ranking learners. Untested here; the criteria and threshold questions are modelled on [Competency-Based Assessment](../principles/competency-based-assessment.md), and the argument against ranking is one essay ([competitive grading](../claims/competitive-grading-pits-students-against-each-other.md) [+W]).
6. **Use some assessment during learning, with low stakes and a revision step.** One checkpoint per outcome before the graded task, read against the same criteria. Evidence: [assessment for learning](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]; the policy for reading the responses is on the [formative assessment pattern](formative-assessment.md).
7. **Reobserve and compare with the audit.** After one run, compare assessed performance per outcome, and ask learners how they prepared. If preparation still targets something other than the outcome, the assessment still rewards it: revise the task, not the learners. Untested proposal.

When a stated condition is not met: if the assessment cannot be changed (an external examination), do steps 1–4 and 6 against the outcomes you hold, add explicit practice on the examination's format as a separate, labelled activity, and record the mismatch as a known limit rather than rewriting outcomes to match the examination. If only one unit can be redesigned, do the audit for the whole course anyway, so the redesigned unit's outcomes are not contradicted by the final assessment; the earlier page held that a single aligned lesson inside a misaligned course "changes little" (untested).

These rows are proposals for interpreting a course's state, untested with learners or teachers. One probe can shift confidence between explanations; it does not establish a cause.

## Fitting the design to a situation

The three facts that most change the decision:

1. **Whether the designer controls the assessment.** If the graded task is fixed by someone else (an external examination, an accreditation rubric), alignment can only be worked from the activity side, and the mismatch must be recorded. If unstated, ask: who sets and marks the final assessment, and can its format change this year?
2. **The kind and level of the goal.** A recall goal is easy to align and easy to over-align downward; a `complex-skill` needs whole-task practice and a performance assessment, which cost time and assessor agreement. If unstated, ask: what should a learner be able to do at the end that they cannot do now, and in what situation?
3. **The learners' expertise and how they prepare now.** `novice` learners need more practice with feedback per outcome and visible criteria; experienced learners may already study strategically to the assessment. If unstated, ask: have they done tasks like the assessed one before, and how did they prepare for the last assessment in this subject?

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- [In one experiment with 256 undergraduates, computer-based lesson versions that included practice produced significantly higher posttest scores than versions without practice](../claims/practice-presence-raises-cbi-posttest-achievement.md) [+M]: 256 undergraduate computer-literacy students were randomly assigned within pretest blocks to six versions of one lesson; the four versions that included practice scored above 17 on the posttest and the two without practice below 15 (`randomized`, immediate posttest, no effect size reported). It supports giving each outcome practice before it is assessed. The claim page does not report how closely the practice items matched the posttest, so it does not test alignment of practice to assessment as such. Not yet checked against its source.
- [HOCS clicker questions during learning improve performance on LOCS exam questions more than LOCS clicker questions do](../claims/hocs-clickers-improve-locs-exam-performance.md) [~M]: in a counterbalanced quasi-experiment over two semesters of a nonmajors Human Biology course, units taught with higher-order clicker questions produced better lower-order exam performance than units taught with lower-order ones (76% against 72%, d = 0.47). This qualifies a strict reading of the pattern: practice above the assessed level served the lower-level assessment better than practice matched to it. A companion claim from the same study reports that a semester difference, not clicker type, separated performance on higher-order exam questions ([semester cohort](../claims/semester-cohort-difference-hocs-exams.md) [~M]), so the study does not show that higher-order practice improved higher-order exam performance. One course, sample size not reported on the claim page, not yet checked.
- [Active learning improves exam performance](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of undergraduate STEM courses compared with traditional lecturing reports exam and concept-inventory scores 0.47 SD higher (158 studies), largest in classes of 50 or fewer and on concept inventories; a second synthesis found exam gaps for underrepresented and low-income students narrowed only under high-intensity active learning. It supports activities in which learners perform rather than listen. The claim page lists alignment of active elements with assessment as an expected moderator still to be established, so it does not show that aligned active learning beats unaligned active learning. Partly checked against its sources.
- [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]: syntheses of K–12 formative assessment studies with control conditions report small positive effects on achievement (d = .29; about .20), with larger feature estimates for supported self-assessment (d = .61) and a medium cycle within or between units (d = .52); the claim page says these feature estimates are not randomised comparisons, not additive and not a forecast. It supports step 6, assessment during learning; it does not test whether the formative tasks were aligned with the summative ones. Partly checked against its sources.
- [Individually removing objectives, examples, or review from a well-designed computer-based lesson did not significantly reduce achievement](../claims/single-event-removal-no-achievement-effect.md) [~M]: in the same six-version experiment as the practice claim, the three versions that each removed one event, one of them the stated objectives, scored between 17.16 and 17.36 against 17.61 for the full lesson, a nonsignificant difference; equivalence was not tested. It limits what step 2 may promise: writing outcomes well is a design discipline for the designer, and showing them to learners did not by itself raise achievement in this lesson. Not yet checked.
- [Instructional design models are rarely tested against outcomes; their credibility comes from practitioners finding them useful](../claims/instructional-design-models-are-validated-by-adoption-not-testing.md) [~W]: an expert account in a textbook chapter, from a survey of 34 design models, that models are rarely evaluated against predetermined criteria and gain credibility from adoption. It applies to this pattern: its standing in higher education is not evidence that it raises learning, and the evidence for it lives in the claims above, about its parts.

Do not combine these into one strength or rank them: two are single lessons or courses, two are syntheses of different populations, one is a nonsignificant difference and one is an argument. None measured learners' approach to study (surface or deep), which is the mechanism the earlier page put at the centre of the pattern; that link is a hypothesis here.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented for illustration; not a tested design.* A logistics company runs a self-paced online induction for new shift supervisors (`adult-workplace`, `online-self-paced`, two weeks, no trainer present). The stated outcomes are a topic list ("understand health and safety", "know the escalation policy"), and the only assessment is a 20-item multiple-choice quiz the compliance team owns and will not change this year.

How the situation table changed the design. The fixed-assessment row applies: the quiz stays, its items are audited, and most turn out to ask for recall of policy wording. The outcomes are rewritten as performances the company actually wants ("decide whether an incident on the floor must be escalated, and to whom, and say why"). The self-paced row adds, in each module, three short incident scenarios answered with immediate feedback before the quiz (the number is an untested choice). The recall-goal row adds scenario questions above the quiz's level rather than drilling quiz wording. Because the quiz cannot show the outcome, an ungraded scenario task at the end of week two is added and its result is reported to the shift manager, labelled as not part of the compliance record. A supervisor's valued goal ("not to look out of my depth on my first night shift") is recorded beside the company's objective (a pass on the quiz and correct escalations in the first month); the scenarios serve the first, the quiz only the second.

Record: **outcome as written → rewritten outcome → activity and practice attempts with feedback → assessed item(s) and who controls them → learner response on the graded task and on an ungraded representative task → how the learner prepared → candidate interpretations → next change to outcome, activity or assessment → next observation at the stated horizon**. An agent may summarise this record but must not invent missing inputs, and must not report a course as "aligned" from the grid alone without the learner observations.

## Description
Constructive alignment, developed by John Biggs, holds that effective teaching requires three components to point in the same direction: intended learning outcomes (ILOs) stating what learners should be able to *do*, teaching and learning activities (TLAs) that require learners to enact exactly those verbs, and assessment tasks (ATs) that measure the same performance. Because learners construct their understanding from what they actually do during instruction, misalignment — teaching one thing, assessing another — produces learning that follows the assessment rather than the stated outcomes. The pattern treats the course as a system to be designed, not a set of topics to be covered.

## Implications

The pattern rests on a constructivist premise: learners cannot acquire an outcome they never practice. If an outcome says "analyze," but lectures only transmit content and the exam only asks for recall, learners rationally adopt a surface approach — doing the minimum the system actually rewards. Alignment works by making the rewarded behavior and the intended behavior identical, harnessing the powerful "backwash" effect of assessment on study behavior [Assessment practices that provide feedback during learning improve achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]. When activities require learners to actively construct and apply knowledge rather than receive it, outcomes improve [Active learning outperforms lecture on exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M], and giving learners visible purpose and autonomy within aligned tasks supports intrinsic motivation [Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M].

### Context
#### Requirements
- Intended learning outcomes stated with explicit action verbs (analyze, design, critique), not topic lists ("know about," "understand")
- Teaching activities that give learners repeated practice enacting those verbs ([application](../elements/application.md), [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [case studies](../elements/case-studies.md))
- Assessment tasks that elicit the same verbs under valid conditions ([assess performance](../elements/assess-performance.md))
- Criterion-referenced grading: standards defined by the outcome, not by rank ordering students against each other
- A course-level (not lesson-level) design perspective, since alignment is a property of the whole system

#### Constraints
- Outcomes written at low cognitive levels ("list," "define") align the whole system to surface learning — alignment amplifies whatever level the outcomes specify, good or bad [-M]
- Long checklists of granular outcomes fragment learning and encourage strategic compliance rather than integrated understanding [~M]
- Even well-aligned high-stakes summative grading can pull learners back toward surface strategies when grade stakes dominate [~M]
- Difficult to sustain across program-level curricula: individual course redesigns drift apart without program-wide coordination
- Redesign is time-intensive and often provokes resistance when it removes predictable, reproducible assessment formats

#### Grain Size
Course or program — alignment is a system property; a single aligned lesson inside a misaligned course changes little.

### Target Goals
- Deep approaches to learning: learners engaging with meaning rather than reproducing content
- Higher-order outcomes: analysis, evaluation, design, argumentation — outcomes that cannot be assessed by recall alone
- Transfer and application of knowledge to novel, authentic problems
- Transparent expectations that support learners in self-monitoring progress toward stated standards

### Target Learners
- Higher education and professional education, where Biggs developed and validated the framework
- Learners prone to surface strategies under assessment pressure — alignment redirects strategic effort toward intended outcomes
- Competency-based and accreditation-driven programs that must demonstrate outcome attainment

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — learners construct understanding from what they do; alignment ensures the "doing" matches the intended outcome
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) (Zimmerman) — explicit outcomes and criterion-referenced standards give learners the information needed to monitor and direct their own effort
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) (Sweller) — a coherent, aligned structure reduces extraneous load created by guessing what the teacher really wants

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Behaviorism](../theories/behaviorism.md) — alignment's reliance on assessment "backwash" can shade into reward-driven compliance; behaviorist framing predicts learners optimize for the measure, which is exactly the surface-learning risk when outcomes are poorly chosen
