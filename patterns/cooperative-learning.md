# Cooperative Learning

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 72 claims (43 for, 28 mixed, 1 against) · 59 studies (21 causal, 19 quant-synthesis, 7 review, 6 design, 3 theoretical, 2 associational, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 20 of 59 report an effect size · 64 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable policy for organising small-group work so that each member's learning, not only the group's product, is the target and is checked. It instantiates the [conditional principle](../principles/cooperative-learning.md). The study configurations below are evidence; the response-dependent policy is an **untested design proposal**. Use it to gather better evidence about each member, not to certify that a group "worked". Named structures such as jigsaw, Student Teams-Achievement Divisions or reciprocal peer tutoring are configurations of this policy, each with its own evidence; none defines it.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Task-specific prior knowledge for each member, from an individual response before grouping; age band; language and access needs; known status or friendship patterns. Preserve unknowns rather than labelling members "high" or "low". |
| Context and activities | Setting (classroom, online, workplace), group size and how groups are formed, how long groups stay together, time available, the task and whether one person could do it alone, roles, talk supports, teacher monitoring, and exactly how any group reward or grade is computed. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask each learner what they want from working in a group and why. Record disagreement with the designer objective, such as valuing the group grade or an even workload over individual mastery. |
| Designer objective | The capability each member should have afterwards, on what kind of knowledge (concept, principle, complex skill, collaboration itself), and what each member must be able to do without the group. |
| Outcome | An individual instrument aligned with the objective, a locally chosen criterion, immediate and delayed horizons, and any separate outcome (collaboration skill, persistence, belonging) with its own measure. A group product score is not this outcome. |

A request for "more group work" or "more engagement" does not specify these inputs. Ask for them before choosing a structure, a reward scheme or a predicted gain.

## Sequence and conditional policy

1. **Elicit individually first.** Take a short individual response on the target before grouping. It is both the starting observation and the comparison for later individual checks.
2. **Choose a task that needs the group.** Prefer a task with something to explain, compare or reconcile. If one member could complete it alone, expect parallel or divided work and either change the task or treat the session as individual practice.
3. **Set interdependence and individual accountability together.** Make the group's recognition depend on each member's individual learning (for example, the sum of members' individual quiz improvements), and say in advance how each member will be checked. Record the exact scheme delivered.
4. **Support the interaction.** Assign or rotate roles and give talk supports where members are young, new to group work or uncertain; monitor talk and give process-level feedback.
5. **Check each member individually.** Use a new task aligned with the objective, under the agreed aids, then use the table below to choose the next activity provisionally.
6. **Reobserve at the stated horizon**, and have groups reflect briefly on how they worked. Retain unresolved explanations; if individual results disagree with the expectation, revise the configuration rather than the definition.

| Observation | Discriminating probe | Proposed action, with its uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Group product is strong but one or more members fail the individual check | Ask each member to explain or redo a part of the product they did not author; compare with a changed task. | If members cannot explain others' parts, make the task less divisible or require each member to present any part chosen at random. If they can explain but not transfer, give individual practice. Neither branch is a validated diagnosis. |
| One member dominates talk | Ask each member privately for their answer before group discussion; rotate the reporter role. | If others had sound answers, adjust roles or status supports; if they had none, the gap is knowledge, so give targeted support before the next group task. |
| Talk is off task | Check whether the task needs discussion and whether members can start it individually. | Redesign the task or supply prerequisite instruction first. Do not read off-task talk alone as a motivation problem. |
| Members converge on a shared misconception | Compare individual answers before and after discussion; present a contrasting case. | Give the group the contrast and ask for a revised, individually justified answer; monitor whether a confident member is carrying the group. |
| A member stays silent but passes the individual check | Ask privately how they experienced the group and what they want from it. | Silence alone is not non-learning; adjust participation demands only if collaboration itself is a stated objective or the learner wants it. |

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Reward on individual learning, not a single product.** For school-age learners in programmes of four weeks or more, [group rewards based on each member's individual learning](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+M] are associated with larger achievement effects than single-product or no-reward methods (median +.32 against +.07, vote counts from a best-evidence review), and a randomised component study of at-risk 4th and 5th graders found reward plus structured tutoring ahead of either alone. Not settled against its sources (1 of 3 entries confirmed, abstracts). The same review reports structured interaction working without rewards, so a reward scheme is one candidate configuration, not a requirement; effects on intrinsic motivation over time are unresolved on the claim page.
- **Structure the interaction.** In peer tutoring, [structured programmes produced larger achievement gains than unstructured ones](../claims/structured-peer-tutoring-outperforms-unstructured.md) [+M] in a meta-analysis of school tutoring, though unstructured tutoring still helped and no magnitude for the difference is reported; in elementary peer-assisted learning, structured roles were related to larger social, self-concept and behavioural effects. Not settled (abstracts). It does not say which component (training, scripts, monitoring) matters.
- **Expect a programme-level gain in undergraduate STEM, not a forecast.** [Small-group against non-group instruction](../claims/small-group-learning-improves-stem-achievement.md) [+M] gave weighted d = 0.51 on achievement across 39 studies, larger when the investigator taught and smaller (non-significant) at two-year colleges. Not settled (abstract). It does not isolate which structural features were present.
- **Plan for failure in a given implementation.** [Science-classroom comparisons](../claims/cooperative-learning-science-studies-mixed.md) [~W] include null results and a chemistry course in which control classes won all 60 comparisons. Second-hand and unchecked, with no effect sizes; it is a reason to check individual outcomes, not an estimate of failure rates.

Do not convert vote counts or median effects into pooled estimates, rank these syntheses against each other, or read a group product score as evidence about any member. A precise abstention names the missing input (individual starting response, outcome, horizon) and how to get it.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested lesson.* In an undergraduate chemistry lab, students want a finished report and a fair workload; the instructor wants each student to predict and justify a reaction outcome on an individual test next week. Before grouping, each student answers one prediction item individually. Groups of three get a lab task with rotating roles (procedure, prediction, data check); the team's recognition is the sum of members' individual improvements on a short prediction quiz, announced in advance. At the end, any member may be asked to explain any part of the report. Agree a reasoning rubric and repeat an unaided prediction item a week later.

Record: **individual starting response → group, task, roles and reward scheme as delivered → observed talk and division of work → each member's individual response → candidate interpretations and confidence basis → discriminating probe → chosen next activity and justification → individual response at the stated horizon**. Preserve each learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise this record, but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical state probabilities without a calibrated model.

## Elements and limits

[Group work](../elements/group-work.md), [group roles](../elements/group-roles.md), [group processing](../elements/group-processing.md), [peer explanation](../elements/peer-explanation.md), [class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md), [assessment](../elements/assessment.md), [quizzes](../elements/quizzes.md) and [feedback](../elements/feedback.md).

This pattern is scoped to small-group work where each member's individual learning is the target. Collaboration skill as an outcome, intergroup relations, belonging, online or adult workplace teams, and long-running project teams need their own outcome measures and evidence. The policy supports observation and design reasoning; its predictive accuracy and the role of learners' valued goals remain to be tested with actual learners.

## Examples

- [The science unit 'How does alcohol consumption affect our body?'](../designs/alcohol-consumption-multidisciplinary-unit.md) — disciplinary groups regrouped into multidisciplinary groups of three to build a shared model

**Jigsaw (Aronson):** Each member of a home team learns one segment of material in an expert group, then teaches it to teammates. Every member is essential by design — the canonical positive-interdependence structure. See [https://www.jigsaw.org](https://www.jigsaw.org).

**Kagan Structures:** Named protocols (Numbered Heads Together, RoundRobin, Pairs Check) that build accountability and equal participation into the mechanics of the activity. See [https://www.kaganonline.com](https://www.kaganonline.com).

**Peer Instruction (Mazur, Harvard physics):** Students answer individually, convince a neighbor, then answer again — individual accountability plus peer explanation in a large lecture. See [https://peerinstruction.mit.edu](https://peerinstruction.mit.edu).

**PALS (Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies):** Structured reciprocal peer tutoring pairs in reading and math, with role switching and point systems; extensively validated in elementary classrooms.

## Description
Cooperative learning organizes students into small groups with a shared goal, structured so that each member's success depends on the group's success (positive interdependence) while each member is individually accountable for their own learning. Unlike unstructured group work, cooperative learning deliberately teaches and enforces collaborative skills — turn-taking, explaining, challenging ideas respectfully — and includes processing time in which groups reflect on how well they worked together. The problem it solves is the chronic failure mode of group work: free-riding, status hierarchies, and division of labor that lets some students disengage.

## Implications

The pattern rests on social interdependence theory: achievement is enhanced when students perceive that they sink or swim together, and degraded when goals are competitive or individualistic [Group rewards plus individual accountability improve cooperative learning outcomes.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]. Individual accountability is the load-bearing component — without it, group discussion can be dominated by a few voices and weaker students can hide. Structured explanation is the key mechanism: the student who explains elaborates and reorganizes their knowledge, while the listener receives peer-calibrated support, producing gains for both [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S].

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuinely group-worthy task — one that cannot be completed as well by an individual working alone
- Explicit positive interdependence (shared goal, shared product, or complementary roles)
- Individual accountability mechanisms: individual quizzes, random reporter selection, individual write-ups, role rotation
- Time for teaching collaborative skills explicitly, not just assuming them
- Group processing: brief structured reflection on how the team functioned

#### Constraints
- Poorly designed tasks produce free-riding and social loafing; accountability structures are not optional [Group rewards plus individual accountability improve cooperative learning outcomes.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [~S]
- Grouping low-ability students together without support tends to produce worse outcomes than heterogeneous grouping
- High-status students dominate unless roles and talk structures are assigned and enforced
- Setup and monitoring are time-intensive; benefits shrink when groups are formed ad hoc for a single activity
- Group grades without individual accountability can penalize strong students and mask weak learning

#### Grain Size
Lesson to unit — a single cooperative activity can run 15–40 minutes; longer structures (jigsaw units, project teams) run weeks.

### Target Goals
- Conceptual understanding through peer explanation and [argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md)
- Acquisition of collaboration and communication skills as outcomes in their own right
- Retention — explaining to peers is a form of [elaboration](../elements/articulation.md) that strengthens memory
- Reduced achievement gaps, since heterogeneous teams give lower-achieving students access to peer explanation

### Target Learners
- Effective across ages from primary school through university and professional education [+S]
- Particularly valuable for students who benefit from low-stakes verbal rehearsal before whole-class participation
- Requires some social skill foundation; very young or socially anxious students need more explicit skill instruction and smaller groups

### Theory
#### Supporting

- [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) (Bandura) — peers model strategies and persistence; observing similar others strengthens self-efficacy
- [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) (Deci & Ryan) — cooperative structures support relatedness and autonomy, sustaining intrinsic motivation
- [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) (Lave & Wenger) — group work situates learning in collaborative practice resembling real communities of practice

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) (Sweller) — collaboration itself imposes coordination costs; for well-structured individual tasks, group work adds load without benefit, and novices may lack the schemas needed to profit from peer explanation
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — radical versions assume peer interaction suffices for knowledge construction; cooperative learning research shows structure and teacher guidance are necessary, qualifying pure discovery assumptions

### Claims
#### Supporting
- [Group rewards plus individual accountability improve cooperative learning outcomes.](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S] — the two structural features that distinguish cooperative learning from mere group work
- [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S] — cooperative structures are a primary vehicle for active learning in STEM
- [Feedback is most effective at task and process levels.](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+M] — peer feedback within teams works best when focused on the task and reasoning, not the person

#### Contradicting

- None identified — though the accountability claim is conditional: benefits disappear when either component is missing [~S]

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Set interdependence** — Define a shared goal and assign complementary roles (recorder, skeptic, reporter) so each member is necessary
2. **Establish accountability** — Announce individual checks: random reporter, individual exit tickets, or individual components of the product
3. **Teach collaborative skills** — Briefly model and name the target skill (e.g., "disagree with the idea, not the person") before work begins
4. **Structured group work** — Students work with [discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md) protocols and talk moves that ensure every voice is heard
5. **Monitor and intervene** — Instructor circulates, observing group process and giving [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) at the process level
6. **Individual demonstration** — Each student independently shows their learning (quiz, write-up, explanation)
7. **Group processing** — Teams briefly reflect on what helped and what to change next time

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md) — cooperative learning is the structured, accountability-bearing form of this principle; it specifies the conditions under which collaboration actually produces learning rather than mere activity
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — group explanation, debate, and joint problem-solving give every student generative cognitive work, not just the fastest hand-raisers
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — peer explanation and feedback within teams function as continuous formative assessment, surfacing misconceptions to peers before they reach the instructor

### Personalization

**Novices:** Provide discussion prompts and role scripts; assign roles rather than letting groups self-organize. Keep groups small (2–3) so no one can hide.

**Learners with anxiety or low confidence:** Use think-pair-share sequencing — private writing before pair talk — so students enter discussion with something prepared. Pairs are lower-stakes than groups of four.

**Learners with language or learning differences:** Provide sentence frames for academic talk ("My evidence is…", "I disagree because…"), allow written contributions to count fully, and extend processing time.

**Heterogeneous cohorts:** Form teams deliberately — mixed ability, but avoid isolating a single student by any salient characteristic. Use jigsaw structures so each member holds unique expertise, making status equal by design.

**High-achieving students:** Assign the role of questioner rather than answer-giver; explaining through questioning produces deeper elaboration than simply supplying solutions.
