AI Tutoring¶

Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 219 claims (113 for, 63 mixed, 43 against) · 160 studies (52 causal, 34 review, 20 design, 19 theoretical, 16 quant-synthesis, 10 qualitative, 9 associational), q1–q4 · 30 of 160 report an effect size · 206 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

Tutoring delivered by software rather than a person: step-based intelligent tutoring systems that track a learner's work on each step and give hints and feedback, and conversational tutors built on large language models that answer, question and explain in dialogue. The decisions it covers are how much the tutor gives away (answers, hints, Socratic questions), how it keeps the learner doing the thinking, how it checks understanding rather than accepting a correct-looking answer, how it guards against over-reliance and wrong answers, and when it refers the learner to a human tutor, mentor or instructor.

It draws on three older literatures that conversational AI tutors inherit: pedagogical agents (animated or embodied on-screen characters that explain, model, give feedback and motivate), teachable agents (learning by teaching a computer agent that learns from the learner, as in Betty's Brain, and the protégé effect), and computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL), where software scripts, scaffolds or takes part in a learning conversation as a peer or partner.

The study configurations below are evidence; the response-dependent policy is an untested design proposal. Use it to gather better evidence about a learner group and a tutor configuration, not to certify that a tutor "works" or that a learner has mastered something because the tutor says so.

What the evidence does and does not test. The wiki's strongest evidence is on step-based intelligent tutoring systems against conventional instruction: three meta-analyses (Ma et al. 2014, k = 107; Kulik & Fletcher 2016, 50 evaluations; VanLehn 2011) agree on a moderate advantage over classroom instruction, textbooks and other computer-based instruction and no detected difference from one-to-one human tutoring, with much smaller effects on standardized than on locally developed tests. For LLM tutors the wiki records a handful of randomized studies, mostly single-session and immediate: an unguarded GPT-4 interface raised practice scores and lowered later unaided exam scores, while a guardrailed tutor removed the harm without a detected gain (Bastani et al. 2025); a research-designed physics tutor beat an in-class active-learning lesson on an immediate post-test (Kestin et al. 2025). No study recorded here follows an LLM tutor across a whole course to a delayed or transfer outcome. Pedagogical-agent evidence rests largely on reviews of older multimedia experiments; teachable-agent evidence on one meta-analysis of learning by teaching and a few small studies; CSCL evidence here is the moderator analyses of one meta-analysis of collaboration scripts (its main effect is not recorded as a claim in the wiki). So the configuration this page defends is narrow: a tutor that works step by step, withholds final answers by default, escalates help contingently, is judged on unaided and delayed performance, and has a named human route.

Mastery learning decides when a learner moves on; self-regulated learning concerns the learner's own planning and monitoring; practice with simulated people covers AI role-play partners and simulated patients or clients; mentor support in self-paced online learning covers the human side of the handoff. This pattern is about the tutor's moves inside a task.

Learner-valued goal Ask what the learner wants from the tutor (finish the assignment, pass the assessment, be able to do this at work) and why. Record disagreement with the designer objective: a learner who wants answers and a designer who wants independent skill want different tutors, and the evidence below says which one each will get.
Designer objective The capability (procedure, concept, complex-skill, judgement), whether the domain is well structured (steps can be checked) or ill structured, and which aids are permitted when the capability is assessed.
Outcome Separate measures for assisted performance during practice, unaided performance at the end, a delayed unaided measure and, where it matters, transfer to a changed task. Say whether the test is developed locally (close to the tutored problems) or standardized. Add a reliance or verification measure if over-reliance is a risk.

A request to "add an AI tutor" does not specify these inputs. Ask for them before choosing a tutor configuration or predicting a gain. Check first that the tutor's answers on the course's own problems are correct: in the wiki, LLM-generated hints were wrong, too general or gave the answer away about a third of the time in one coding study, and expert reviewers discarded a quarter to nearly half of generated hints in another.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

  1. Elicit an unaided attempt first. Before the tutor opens, ask for the learner's own attempt and reasoning on a representative task. Keep it short and record it as a learning exposure. This is the baseline against which tutored practice is later read.
  2. Configure the tutor for the objective, not for helpfulness. For a structured task, set the tutor to work step by step, to withhold final answers by default, and to give problem-specific hints (teacher- or designer-written content in the prompt, or a validated hint bank). Start contingent: the least help that moves the learner, more only after a failed attempt, less after success. Test the tutor on the course's own problems before learners see it.
  3. Tutored practice, dose by session. Early sessions (the first one or two in a unit): 20–40 minutes of tutored step-based practice with hints available and an explanation request at each step. Middle sessions: raise the threshold for help (a hint only after an attempt; no worked step without a learner explanation) and add prompts to check the tutor's claims. Late sessions: tutor off for at least one unaided practice set before the assessment. Move a learner on when unaided work on a changed problem meets the agreed criterion, not when tutored practice does. These doses are a proposal; no claim here tests a dose or a fading schedule for an AI tutor.
  4. Read the log, not only the score. Record what was asked (answer, hint, explanation, check), how many hints, whether the full answer was given, and how often the learner verified the tutor. Use the table below.
  5. Hand off to a human on named triggers. Repeated impasse at the same step, a learner asking for the answer repeatedly, a tutor error the learner accepted, distress, or a question the tutor cannot answer. Name who takes over and how fast. Two studies recorded here support human involvement alongside software; none tests a specific trigger.
  6. Reobserve unaided and later. Score an unaided, changed task at the end of the unit and again after a delay, separately from tutored practice. If tutored practice is high and unaided performance is not, treat it as a configuration problem, not as learning.
Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
Tutored practice scores high, unaided scores flat or lower Compare the log: share of turns asking for the answer or receiving it; give one matched unaided item mid-unit. If answer-taking dominates, tighten answer-withholding and require an attempt before a hint. This is the pattern Bastani et al. observed with an unguarded interface; the guarded configuration removed the harm but did not show a gain, so expect no more than parity until an unaided measure shows otherwise.
Learner asks for the answer repeatedly, or skips Ask what they are trying to get done and by when; check whether the task is beyond reach with the current hints. If the task is out of reach, step back (a worked example or a simpler step) rather than release the answer. If the goal is completion under time pressure, negotiate the goal; refer to a human mentor if it persists. Untested branch.
Learner accepts a wrong tutor answer Ask them to check the step against a source or a second method. Add a short reflection or verification prompt; warning labels alone did not reduce reliance in one study recorded here. Log the error and fix the tutor's content.
Learner rarely asks for help but fails unaided Compare hint use with errors; ask whether they avoided help. A warning that the tutor can be wrong raised hint requests in one classroom study; a metacognitive help-seeking tutor changed help-seeking but not domain learning in another. Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Whether to expect a step-based tutor to beat ordinary instruction. Intelligent tutoring systems improve achievement over non-individualised instruction [+S]: Ma et al. (107 effect sizes, 14,321 participants) and Kulik & Fletcher (50 controlled evaluations, median 0.66 SD; random-effects g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.40, 0.59]) both find a moderate advantage. The overall weighted effect was g = .41 under random effects [+M]. By comparator [~M], tutoring systems beat large-group human instruction (g = .44), individual computer-based instruction (g = .57) and textbooks or workbooks (g = .36), but showed no significant difference from small-group (k = 4, g = .05) or individual human tutoring (k = 5, g = −.11); equivalence was not tested.
  • Human tutoring is the ceiling to compare against, and it is lower than often said. Human tutoring had a medium effect over no tutoring [+S]: VanLehn's review found d = 0.79 for human tutoring and d = 0.76 for step-based tutoring systems against no tutoring (abstract only). In a small study in the same claim (11 tutor–student pairs), students learned as well when tutors only prompted them to construct explanations as when tutors also explained and gave feedback, which is one reason this page defaults to eliciting rather than telling. AutoTutor's gains were virtually equivalent to experienced human tutors [+M] in typed or spoken physics tutoring, with no effect size printed, and AutoTutor gains of 0.3 to 0.8 sigma over reading text for the same time [+M].
  • The test decides the size of the effect. Effects were large on locally developed tests and small on standardized ones [~M]: average ES 0.73 against 0.13 (g 0.62 against 0.09) across Kulik & Fletcher's evaluations. Plan an outcome that is not written by the tutor's designers, or expect a smaller effect on it. The largest effects in that set [+M] (ES 1.97 and 3.18) came from one system for Navy technicians compared with longer classroom courses on locally developed tests; they are outliers, not a forecast.
  • An unguarded LLM tutor can raise practice and lower learning. In a randomized trial with nearly a thousand high-school mathematics students (2,848 observations), a standard GPT-4 interface raised assisted practice scores by 0.137 (out of 1) over a textbook-only control mean of 0.28 [~M], and lowered the later unaided exam by 0.054, about 17% of the control mean [-M]. A guardrailed tutor with teacher-designed, problem-specific hints raised practice more (0.361) [+M] and removed the exam harm (−0.004, not significant) without a detected exam gain [~M]. In a separate experiment, costs concentrated among participants who used the AI for direct solutions [-M] (61% of the AI group; d = −0.36 against controls on later solving, cross-sectional within the AI arm), while hint users resembled controls.
  • A research-designed LLM tutor against active learning. In a crossover randomized trial in an undergraduate physics course, students learned more from an AI tutor than from an in-class active-learning lesson on the same content [+M] (median post-test 4.5 against 3.5; N = 142 and 174), a regression effect of 0.63 after controls, which the authors call an underestimate because of a ceiling [+M], in a median of 49 minutes against a 60-minute class [+M], with the advantage holding above and below 40% on the pre-test concept inventory [+M]. The outcome was an immediate post-test of one lesson; the trial says nothing about retention or a whole course.
  • Answer-giving is not always worse on an immediate test, which is why the horizon matters. In a 50-person experiment, an unrestricted chatbot produced larger immediate learning deltas than a Socratic chatbot (d = 0.80) or an adaptive exercise system (d = 0.85) [~M]; in a proctored experiment with 211 undergraduates, any generative AI during a 35-minute learning phase raised an immediate unaided test by 7.2 points (0.28 SD) [+M]. In writing, open AI collaboration gave the best supported writing and bounded support the best independent Week 8 writing [~M]; in creativity, free ChatGPT use raised originality on the assisted task (d = 0.79) and the advantage vanished unaided (d = 0.04) [~M]. Read immediate gains as provisional until a delayed unaided measure agrees.
  • Contingent help, and how much to give away. Computer-based scaffolding showed g = 0.46 across 144 STEM studies [+S], and contingent scaffolding beat fixed or absent support [+M] in small tutoring studies. In an LLM tutor comparison, a tutor that escalated and faded on what the learner did gave away the full answer in one session in sixteen, against one in six for a question-only tutor and three in five for an answer-giving one [+M]. Answer-revealing turns were followed by less independent student work on every tutor model tested [-M]. In a logic tutor with 113 undergraduates, adaptive scaffolding policies beat a non-adaptive control on the post-test [+M].
  • Feedback content. Feedback averages d = 0.48 (435 studies) but more than a third of feedback interventions in an older meta-analysis reduced performance [~S]; information-rich feedback is the moderator. Praise for intelligence after success undermined later persistence [-S], so a tutor's encouragement should name the process, not the person. ChatGPT-generated hints produced significant gains over no hints [+M] in an online algebra experiment (N = 274, crowd workers), but GPT-4 feedback was targeted for 66% of errors and too general, wrong or answer-revealing for about 35% [~M].
  • Over-reliance is not fixed by a warning. A brief metacognitive reflection prompt cut acceptance of incorrect AI advice (62.4% to 39.7%; OR = 0.40) [+M], whereas warning labels alone did not reduce reliance [-M] in the medical-education studies a review reports. Learners can spot an error and follow the system anyway: 80.7% detected flawed recommendations yet deferred [-M]; 7 of 15 students failed a task with plausible falsehoods despite checking [-M]. Where verification was measured, checking the AI's claims against sources predicted gains [+M].
  • Guided prompting of the learner's AI use. Theory-guided five-step prompting improved open-ended explanations over unprompted AI access and slides [+M] (95 undergraduates, construction engineering; 12.41 against 10.45 and 9.68).
  • Human in the loop. In a preregistered randomized trial with about 900 tutors, students whose human tutors had real-time AI suggestions were 4 percentage points more likely to master the session's topic [+S] (9 points for students of lower-rated tutors, per a review). In a quasi-experiment with 635 middle-school students, adding human tutors to AI tutoring raised relative MAP growth by 61% within the analysis bandwidth [+M] (the full-sample 26% was not significant) and time on task by 25% [+M].
  • Pedagogical agents: the content and the voice matter more than the face. A review reports that a meta-analysis of 43 studies found agents enhance learning against no agent [+M] (no effect size printed). AutoTutor's talking head added little beyond the content of the dialogue [~M]; effects are attributed mainly to the agent's voice [~M], with transfer rather than recall improved; iconic and realistic appearance did not differ [~M]. In one 2×2 experiment with 72 undergraduates, a high-competency learning companion improved application (d = 0.46) [+M], a low-competency one raised self-efficacy (d = 0.49) [~M], and a proactive companion improved recall over a responsive one (d = 0.78) [+M].
  • Teachable agents and learning by teaching. Preparing to teach and then teaching gave g = 0.56 across 28 studies, larger when the teaching was interactive [+S]. Adding a teachable agent to a fourth-grade science kit improved gains on curriculum and reasoning tests (d = 1.1 and 0.93) [+M] in a two-year quasi-experiment with seven classrooms. With an LLM, students who taught a ChatGPT agent outscored a video group on knowledge [+M] (n = 20 and 21; the printed η² = 0.74 overstates an effect the paper's own figures put nearer d ≈ 0.7) but not on code correctness [~M].
  • Collaboration scripts and AI in groups. In the collaboration-scripts meta-analysis, prompting transactive activities [~M], the script level [~M] and extra content support [~M] did not significantly moderate effects, but each test had low power (8–16%), so "no detected difference" is not "no difference". In a preregistered online experiment (315 participants), a tutor plus LLM peers produced the highest unaided accuracy (about 65% against 42% for control) [+M].

Do not rank these results against one another: comparators (no tutoring, textbooks, classroom instruction, active learning, an unguarded chatbot), outcomes (practice, immediate unaided, standardized, transfer) and learners differ. A precise abstention names the missing comparison, outcome or observation and the next way to resolve it.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for four facts when the brief omits them: is the task structured enough for the tutor to check each step?, what is assessed, with what aids, and when (immediately, at the end of the course, later at work)?, is a human tutor, mentor or instructor reachable, and how quickly?, and what will the tutor actually say, tested on the course's own problems?

The evidence by question¶

  • Does a step-based tutoring system improve learning over ordinary instruction? Intelligent tutoring systems improve achievement over non-individualised instruction [+S]
  • How does it compare with a human tutor? ITS beat large-group instruction and textbooks but not small-group or individual human tutoring [~M]; Human tutoring d = 0.79, step-based tutoring d = 0.76, against no tutoring [+S]
  • Does an LLM tutor help or harm learning? Unguarded GPT-4 access harmed the unaided exam [-M]; guardrails removed the harm [~M]; a research-designed tutor beat active learning on an immediate test [+M]
  • How much should the tutor give away? Answer-revealing turns reduce next-turn independent work [-M]; a contingent tutor gave away the answer least [+M]
  • Does an on-screen agent help? Pedagogical agents enhance learning compared with no agent [+M]; the talking head contributes little [~M]
  • Does teaching an agent help the learner? Learning by teaching improves tutor learning [+S]
  • When should a human be involved? Real-time AI support for human tutors raised mastery [+S]

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Illustration only, invented, not a tested course. An online, self-paced introductory statistics module for working adults, with a mentor who answers within one business day. The designer's objective is that each learner can choose and run the right test on a new data set and explain the result; one learner says she wants to pass the module quickly while working full time. Agree that the module assessment is unaided and proctored, and that a delayed check a month later matters to the programme. The first activity asks each learner to choose a test for a short scenario without help. The tutor is configured to work step by step, never to give the final test choice or computed value, to give problem-specific hints written by the course team, and to ask the learner to check its claims against the course notes. Sessions one and two: about 30 minutes of tutored practice; sessions three and four: hints only after an attempt; session five: an unaided practice set with the tutor off. The log flags a learner who asks for the answer three times on one step, or accepts a tutor statement later marked wrong, and sends a message to the mentor.

Record: unaided first attempt → tutor configuration and content actually used → per-step requests (answer, hint, explanation, check), hints given, answer released or not → tutor errors and whether the learner accepted them → handoffs to a human and their response time → unaided score on a changed task at the end → delayed unaided score. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record, but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical mastery probabilities without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Hints, feedback, immediate feedback, scaffolding, fading scaffolding, Socratic questioning, self-explanation, worked examples, pedagogical agent signaling, peer teaching, collaborative problem solving and metacognitive reflection.

This pattern is scoped to the tutor's moves within tasks and the handoff to people. It does not cover AI grading, AI-generated course content, proctoring or AI role-play as a practice partner (see practice with simulated people). The wiki records no randomized comparison of an LLM tutor across a whole course with a delayed or transfer outcome, no test of a handoff trigger, and no test of fading a conversational tutor's help across sessions; the diagnostic branches above remain to be tested with actual learners.
