Problem-Based Learning¶

Pattern · All patterns
Evidence · 62 claims (16 for, 42 mixed, 4 against) · 47 studies (16 causal, 8 review, 7 theoretical, 6 quant-synthesis, 5 qualitative, 3 design, 2 associational), q1–q4 · 9 of 47 report an effect size · 57 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable policy for organizing a unit around a problem that learners must investigate, deciding how much guidance to give and when, and reading what learners do with the problem. It instantiates the conditional principle. The study configurations below are evidence; the response-dependent policy is an untested design proposal. Use it to gather better evidence about a learner group, not to certify that a learner is "ready for open problems".

The pattern is general across domains. A course for a particular setting (a medical curriculum, a primary science unit) is a design that may use it; the longer description of the tradition, from the former problem-based-learning-pbl page, is kept in the merged block at the end of this page.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Age band and task-specific starting knowledge (novice, intermediate, advanced, or ?); a brief independent attempt at framing the problem or a comparable one; what the learner says they would need to know. Preserve unknowns.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-instructor-led, workplace-clinical, ...), duration, group size and roles, available resources, who facilitates, and exactly what guidance, instruction and feedback are planned and when.
Learner-valued goal Ask what the learner wants from working on this problem and why. Record disagreement with the designer objective; negotiate rather than assume agreement.
Designer objective The concept, principle or complex skill the problem is meant to carry (principle, complex-skill, ...), and whether a correct solution to the problem as posed actually requires it.
Outcome Separate instruments for conceptual understanding, transfer, procedural fluency and product quality; individual as well as group measures; immediate and delayed horizons.

A request for "more engaging, real-world learning" does not specify these inputs. Ask for them before choosing a problem, a guidance level or a predicted gain. Check first that the problem, the resources and any model solution are correct and that the target concept is needed to solve it.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

  1. Elicit briefly. Present the problem and ask each learner (not only the group) for a first framing: what is the question, what do they already know, what do they need to learn. Keep this short; record it as a learning exposure.
  2. Choose the guidance level provisionally. Default for task-specific novices on the target content: make the content explicit (a short explanation, a worked or modelled example, a short text read before the group discusses the problem, resources that carry it) before or alongside the problem, and use the problem to apply and connect it. A problem-first phase is a narrower candidate, for learners who can generate several partial solutions from what they already know and make real headway on the problem (pilot it), in mathematics and science rather than other domains where it has not replicated, always followed by consolidating instruction. Do not adopt PBL on its label: the average curriculum-level effect is small and varies widely, so the configuration decides.
  3. Run the problem phase with contingent facilitation. Increase support when a learner or group is stuck, reduce it when they progress; record each intervention. Ask learners to make their solutions visible, including flawed ones.
  4. Consolidate. Teach the canonical idea explicitly, building on and contrasting with the solutions learners produced. Do not treat the consolidation as optional: the problem-first evidence concerns problem solving followed by instruction.
  5. Reobserve individually. Use a new problem or a changed condition, scored separately for concept, transfer and procedure, at the agreed horizon. If the response disagrees with the expectation, revise the interpretation or the configuration.
Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
Learners cannot frame the problem or list learning issues Re-present the problem in a familiar representation or with a model of a finished product; ask for a first decision only. If a familiar framing releases a start, the barrier may be access or representation: adjust the problem. If not, supply the missing content explicitly before continuing: in one experiment, students without relevant prior knowledge who elaborated in a problem-based discussion recalled less than those who did not, immediately and after a delay. Neither branch is a validated diagnosis.
Group is active, but solutions do not use the target concept Ask what idea the solution depends on; check whether the problem as written requires the concept. If the concept is not required, revise the problem. If it is required and absent, move consolidation earlier or add a targeted resource. Activity alone does not show learning. In one design experiment, a student asked twelve times in 53 minutes what the group's work meant in disciplinary terms and nobody answered adequately [+W]: a repeated conceptual question is a cue for the facilitator to explain, not to wait.
Discussion is all explanation-building, little checking against the problem's data Ask which facts in the problem the group's explanation does not yet account for; ask each member privately what they are unsure of. Prompt data exploration and reflection on the group's reasoning. In one tutorial group, discussion during problem analysis was mainly theory building, with less data exploration and meta-reasoning [~W], and stimulated recall showed that the talk revealed only part of members' thinking [~W]: a quiet member may be reasoning, and a fluent group may be hiding gaps. Both from one qualitative study; the prompt is an untested proposal.
Several varied, partly wrong solutions generated Ask learners to compare two of their solutions and say what each assumes. Proceed to consolidating instruction that contrasts these solutions with the canonical one. The claim page places the effect mostly in well-structured domains such as mathematics, and its synthesis reports a reversal for second to fifth graders.
Strong group product, weak individual explanation Individual explanation or changed-condition task, matched in demand; ask each member to describe in writing how the group negotiated and built its solution. Add individual accountability and individual practice; record who contributed what. Do not read the group score as each member's. In one qualitative study of teacher education students, only about half of their written accounts of PBL group work showed co-regulation and co-construction rather than divided group work.
Conceptual probe passed, routine procedure failed Score procedure separately under the same conditions. Add targeted procedural practice; the problem-first evidence records no procedural advantage, so this is expected rather than a failure of the policy.

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Whether to expect anything from the PBL label. The overall effect in a 353-outcome re-analysis is modest (g = 0.27) [~M], ranges from g = −1.26 to 1.91 [~M] and may be inflated by publication bias (g = 0.103 after trim-and-fill) [~M]. Plan and evaluate the specific configuration; do not promise a gain from the format. An essay citing an older review reports equivocal results on knowledge acquisition and clinical reasoning, attributed to how faithfully programmes implement PBL [~W] (second-hand; the review itself is not read on the claim page), and a meta-synthesis of meta-analyses reports PBL ahead of conventional instruction on skill development and on student and teacher satisfaction [+W] (no effect sizes in the text available). Both point the same way: which outcome, and which implementation, decide the result.
  • Who facilitates. Tutor background did not predict learning [~M] in the same data. In a field experiment across 98 health-sciences PBL courses, staff-tutored groups scored slightly higher than peer-tutored groups [~M] (6.79 against 6.64 on a ten-point scale): a real but small difference, so peer tutors are a workable option where staff are scarce, provided they get the preparation below. Prepare facilitators in the guidance and consolidation moves below rather than relying on content expertise or its absence; whether facilitation training itself changes outcomes is not recorded in the wiki.
  • Amount of guidance. For novices, unassisted discovery underperforms explicit instruction, while enhanced discovery outperforms other instruction [-S]. This rules out a configuration in which essential content is left for novices to find unsupported; it does not rule out guided problem work. The meta-analysis is not broken down by prior knowledge on the claim page.
  • Prior knowledge before a problem-based discussion. In one randomized 2×2 experiment (66 students), elaboration during a problem-based discussion interacted with relevant prior knowledge on immediate recall [~M] (η² = .06, p = .06), and students without relevant prior knowledge who elaborated recalled less than those who did not [-M], on the immediate and the delayed test. This is the most direct evidence in the wiki for the default at step 2: give learners some relevant prior knowledge (a short text, a pre-reading) before they discuss the problem. It is one study, measuring text recall rather than transfer. An earlier randomized trial with first-year medical students points the same way for a relevant problem: discussing it before studying a text raised accurate recall of both explanatory and descriptive content [+M], without changing the number of inaccurate propositions [~W], but did not increase the inferences learners made in recall [~W]. So the evidence in the wiki supports problem discussion as preparation for reading, measured as memory for the text, not as a source of deeper inference. In an example-and-problem sequencing study, pre-test knowledge strongly predicted application and transfer scores whatever the order [~W]: measure prior knowledge at step 1 and read any outcome against it.
  • Who sets the learning issues. In an experiment reviewed by Loyens et al., groups who generated their own learning issues studied more materials and spent more time on self-study and reporting [+W] than groups handed the issues with the same problem. That supports step 1's "what do they need to learn" coming from learners; the review prints no achievement outcome, so it is evidence about study behaviour, not learning.
  • Whole problems or parts first. A training-design argument holds that practising whole complex tasks favours transfer [+W], which is the rationale for a whole problem; no study comparing whole-task with part-task practice is recorded on that claim. For learners with very little prior knowledge facing a task with many interacting elements, presenting isolated parts before the integrated task helped [~M] in one experiment. So for absolute novices on a complex problem, isolate the key parts first and then return to the whole problem.
  • Order of problem and instruction. For conceptual and transfer outcomes, a problem-first phase followed by instruction [~S] has meta-analytic support over the same instruction taught first, with no procedural difference and a reversal for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills. A randomized experiment with ninth-graders learning standard deviation, reported in Kapur (2016), found the same pattern (better conceptual understanding and transfer, no procedural difference) when students generated solutions individually, and the small-group version with ninth-graders learning variance [+W] reported the same. Choose it only with a consolidation phase, for learners old enough, and judge it on conceptual and transfer measures. The claim page reports no delayed-horizon breakdown. Two qualifications: across studies, PS-I benefits appeared where most learners solved the initial problems (80–90%) and not where few did (14–28%) [~W] (a cross-study comparison in a theoretical paper), so learners need enough knowledge to make real headway, not merely to fail; and in social-science research methods, problem-first did not outperform instruction-first in two quasi-experiments with tenth-graders [-M], even though pilot tests had shown the problem drew several solution ideas containing the targeted errors [~W]. A problem pitched at the right complexity is necessary and was not sufficient there. A secondary account holds that groups given harder, ill-structured problems solved fewer but did better on later problems [~W]; it is reported second-hand in an editorial, and it is a reason to judge the phase on a later problem (step 5) rather than on the problem phase itself.
  • Facilitation. Contingent scaffolding [+M] (support raised after failure, lowered after success) outperformed fixed or absent support in small one-to-one tutoring studies, with better transfer in one. It is a candidate rule for a facilitator, not a tested result in PBL groups.
  • Case-based formats. Case-based learning [~M] raised exam scores in one non-randomized biology cohort, while a systematic review in health professional education found the evidence on learning inconclusive and noted that gains may come from group work. Do not expect a case or problem format alone to carry the effect.
  • Lectures alongside the problem sessions. In a qualitative comparison across curricula, where more content was covered in lectures, students in PBL sessions repeated lecture facts rather than questioning or challenging, though the prior knowledge made them less afraid to speak [~W]. This is the cost side of step 2's default: content given first eases participation and can close down the discussion. Pose a problem the lectured content informs but does not answer.

Do not rank these results against one another: their comparators (instruction-first, explicit instruction, fixed support, lecture and reading) and outcomes differ. A precise abstention names the missing comparison, outcome or observation and the next way to resolve it.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for four facts when the brief omits them: can learners generate partial solutions to the problem from what they already know?, how much time is there (one session or several)?, who facilitates, and how many groups each?, and which outcomes will be assessed (knowledge, procedure, transfer)?

The evidence by question¶

  • Does PBL as a curriculum format raise achievement over conventional instruction, and how reliably? The overall effect of PBL in the tutor-background meta-analysis is modest (g = 0.27) with large, statistically significant heterogeneity [~M]
  • Should learners attempt a problem before the idea is taught, and what must the teaching afterwards do? Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning [~S]
  • Do learners need relevant prior knowledge before they discuss the problem? Elaboration interacts with relevant prior knowledge on immediate recall [~M]
  • Does PBL group work produce collaborative learning, or only divided work? About half of students' analysed texts showed co-regulation and co-construction [~W]
  • For which outcomes: basic knowledge, application and transfer, or professional skills? Problem-based learning shows null or negative effects on basic knowledge acquisition but positive effects on conceptual understanding and transfer relative to direct instruction [~M]; a meta-synthesis adds skill development and student and teacher satisfaction [+W]
  • Where is the research on PBL thin? A review found considerable research on flexible knowledge, problem solving and self-directed learning, little on collaboration and motivation, and minimal outside medical and gifted education [~W]

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Illustration only, invented, not a tested lesson. An adult evening class in community health is given a problem: a neighbourhood's clinic reports rising missed appointments. The designer's objective is that learners can explain and apply a model of barriers to access; one learner says she wants to be able to argue for a change at her own workplace. Agree that both an individual explanation of the model and a defensible proposal matter. Ask each learner for a first framing; note that most list causes from experience but none names a structural barrier. Give a short resource on the model, let groups generate competing explanations, then teach the model explicitly against their explanations. Assess individually with a changed case a week later, separately for explanation and proposal.

Record: problem and its framing → each learner's first response → resources, guidance and facilitation actually given, with timing → solutions generated → candidate interpretations and confidence basis → distinguishing probe → consolidation delivered → individual next observation at the stated horizon. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical state probabilities without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Problem-based learning, problem scenario, solution development, invention, scaffolding, case-based learning, group roles, feedback and reflection.

This pattern is scoped to problem-centred units in which an explicit consolidation of target content is planned. Fully self-directed inquiry with no consolidation, assessment of whole PBL curricula, and learner groups below the ages in the evidence need distinct configurations and evidence. The wiki records no direct comparison of whole PBL curricula with conventional instruction, and the diagnostic branches above remain to be tested with actual learners.

Claims¶

  • Prior knowledge correlates negatively with delayed ICL and ECL and positively with delayed GCL, consistent with meta-analytic findings [~W]
  • Creating products as part of problem-driven learning does not change its effect on motivation [~W]
  • Computer-based scaffolding improves cognitive outcomes in problem-based learning in STEM education with an overall effect of g = 0.385 [+W]
  • Scaffolding effects in PBL are higher in Engineering, Technology, and Mathematics than in Science, and relatively low in integrated STEM education [~W]
  • PS-DI learners consulted the reference book on supplier selection procedure far more often than DI-PS learners (d = .83) [~W]
  • PS-DI learners reported higher intrinsic in-scenario cognitive load during problem solving (d = .53) [~W]
