Peer Teaching¶

Pattern · All patterns
Evidence · 30 claims (14 for, 14 mixed, 2 against) · 17 studies (10 quant-synthesis, 5 causal, 1 review, 1 design), q2–q4 · 10 of 17 report an effect size · 27 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable session routine for learners teaching one another material they have already met: each learner studies a part → prepares to teach it → teaches it to a partner who must respond (restate, answer, ask, attempt) → the partner corrects with a scripted move when the answer is wrong → roles swap within the session → each learner is checked alone. Run across weeks, the scripts thin out and learners take over the questioning. The intended change is in what each learner can do alone afterwards (immediate-performance on the taught concept, procedure or reading skill), for the learner who teaches and the learner taught.

It sits inside the Peer Learning principle, which owns the general relationship (a structured exchange of explanation and response between learners of similar standing raises individually measured achievement over studying alone) and its evidence. This page owns one configuration of it: a reciprocal, scripted, class-wide teaching session, of which Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies (PALS) is the most tested branded form in the wiki. Neighbouring configurations have their own pages: the Peer Instruction pattern (commit to an answer, discuss, revote), the Structured Peer Review pattern (comment and revise on drafts), and jigsaw-style expert groups in Fostering Communities of Learning and Cooperative Learning.

Two kinds of claim test parts of this pattern, and none tests the whole of it as specified here. Meta-analyses test peer tutoring against no tutoring (Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement [+S]), teaching against studying without expecting to teach (Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+S]), and structured against unstructured tutoring (Structured Peer Tutoring Outperforms Unstructured [+M]). What Works Clearinghouse and evidence-rating reports test the scripted PALS routine against usual instruction, mostly in elementary reading, with small or nonsignificant effects that vary by outcome and population, and one null in mathematics. No claim tests the session lengths, the number of weeks, the point at which scripts are thinned, or the rule for when a learner moves on; those are labelled proposals below.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Before pairing, give each learner one short task on the material alone (read a passage aloud, solve two items, explain one idea in a sentence). Record what each can do unaided, whether they can read the material, and who has met it before. Do not label a learner "strong" or "weak" from grades alone; preserve unknowns.
What is to be taught A concept, a procedure, a reading skill (fluency, decoding, comprehension), or an interpretation. Whether a wrong explanation can be spotted by the partner with a key or a criterion, or only by the teacher. Whether the material splits into parts each learner can own.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-instructor-led, online-self-paced, workplace-clinical), single-session or days-weeks, class size, how many adults are present to listen, whether pairs are same-age or cross-age, and whether a scripted programme (such as PALS) is already adopted.
Learner-valued goal Ask what learners want from teaching each other: to get faster help than waiting for the teacher, to practise explaining before a real audience, to help a friend, not to look slow. Record disagreement with the designer objective.
Designer objective Which capability the routine is for (decoding, fluency, comprehension, a calculation, a concept explanation), and whether the teacher's own learning (the learner who teaches) is a target as well as the taught learner's.
Outcome An individual check on new items or a new passage, scored for each learner apart from the pair's work; immediate or delayed; and whether a standardised measure or a test written for the material will be used, since effects differ between the two.

A request to "add peer teaching" does not specify these. Ask what each learner must do alone afterwards, and who will hear a wrong explanation, before choosing the pairs, the scripts or the dose.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

The sequence below makes the earlier page's one-line description concrete. Each step names its evidence status. The doses are untested proposals: no claim in the wiki sets a session length, a number of sessions per week or a programme length for this routine.

  1. Check the starting point and form pairs (one session before the routine starts, 10 minutes). Use the starting task to pair learners so that each pair has one learner a little further along, and plan to swap who leads. Untested proposal; the tested programmes the claims describe pair learners for turn-taking, and peer-assisted learning [+M] showed larger effects with more student autonomy and self-referenced evaluation, which the pairing does not settle.
  2. Teach the roles once before relying on them (one 20–30 minute session, plus a 5-minute reminder in the next two). Give each role a card: the learner teaching states the step, gives an example and asks the partner to do it; the partner restates, attempts and asks one "why". Model one exchange in front of the class with a deliberate error and the correction move (one fixed phrase that stops the error and asks for a retry, or the material's own correction). Basis: Structured Peer Tutoring Outperforms Unstructured [+M] (structured programmes, with roles and trained tutors, produced larger achievement effects than unstructured ones; unstructured tutoring still helped). The session length is an untested proposal.
  3. Prepare to teach (5 minutes). Each learner studies their part and writes or marks what they will teach: the main idea or step, one example, and the question they expect. Basis: Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+S]: preparing to teach alone gave a smaller benefit than preparing and then teaching. Minutes are an untested proposal.
  4. Teach with a partner who must respond (8–10 minutes per role). The learner teaching does not read from notes; the partner attempts the task or answers, and the learner teaching corrects with the scripted move. For reading, the old page's tested session shape is partner reading with retelling, then "paragraph shrinking" (stating the main idea of each paragraph in a few words), then "prediction relay" (predict, read, check). For mathematics it is the coach modelling self-talk questions, then practice. Basis: the meta-analysis in Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+S] found interactive teaching (responding to a learner) better than non-interactive; the PALS session shapes are those tested in the reading and mathematics trials under step evidence below. Minutes are an untested proposal.
  5. Swap roles inside the session. The learner who was taught now teaches the same or the next part. Untested proposal in itself: the tutoring claims discuss reciprocal roles but no claim compares swapping with fixed roles. The PALS routines the claims test use turn-taking.
  6. Listen to a few pairs each round and correct for everyone. The teacher hears two or three pairs per round, notes any wrong explanation, and ends the round by giving the correct model to the whole class. Untested proposal for how many pairs; structured programmes in the meta-analysis included monitoring among the features that define "structured".
  7. Check each learner alone (5 minutes). A new passage, two new items, or a one-sentence explanation in writing, without the partner. The pair's answer shows what the pair can do together, not what either can do alone. Basis: the Peer Learning principle owns the individual check; In the meeting-standards study, PALS classrooms used more peer-mediated and less teacher-led instruction [+W] is a fidelity check showing the routine displaced teacher-led time, which is why the individual check matters.
  8. Across sessions (untested proposal for an 8–12 week arc, three 30–35 minute sessions a week): weeks 1–2 with full role cards, the modelled exchange revisited, and the teacher sampling pairs every round; weeks 3–6 with the partner's prompts cut to one card ("restate, try it, ask why"), learners choosing which part to teach, and harder material; weeks 7 onward with learners running the session unprompted and the teacher reading only the individual checks and sampling one pair. In the mathematics peer-tutoring meta-analysis, programme duration and time of day moderated the effect while session length and frequency did not (Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement [+S]; the direction of the duration effect is not recorded on the claim page). The one tested dose recorded on a claim page is daily 20-minute sessions for three months (37 sessions, 12.3 hours), in a cross-age trial that found no effect on oral language proficiency (cross-age tutoring and oral proficiency [~M]).
  9. When a learner moves on (untested proposal). A learner who teaches their part correctly, answers the partner's question and passes the individual check in two consecutive sessions moves to harder material or becomes the one who teaches a learner meeting it first. A learner who fails the individual check twice in a row is taken out of the pair for that part and taught by an adult before rejoining. For first-grade readers who had not responded to class-wide PALS, one-to-one adult tutoring did as well or better (below).
Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
Pair answers correctly; one learner's individual check is wrong Ask that learner to teach the step back to the teacher; give a matched item with and then without the partner present. If they can do it with the partner only, the partner carried the reasoning: swap roles so this learner teaches next. If they cannot do it either way, the material is not yet learned: reteach before pairing again. Untested.
Both learners give the same wrong explanation Check each learner's starting task: did both start with the error? If both started with it, it came from earlier instruction: teach it to the whole class. If only the learner teaching had it, they passed it on: check the teaching learner's part before the next session. Untested.
The learner teaching reads from the card or text; the partner only agrees Ask the partner a "what if" question the text does not answer. If the partner cannot answer, the exchange is recitation: require the partner's attempt before the next step, or give harder material to the learner teaching. Untested.
Gains on the taught passages or items but not on a new passage or a standardised test Compare the individual check on taught and untaught material. Effects on tests close to the taught material can be much larger than on standardised tests (reciprocal teaching [~M]); widen the material before claiming a general gain.
A learner keeps failing the individual check after several weeks of class-wide sessions Is the failure on the same skill each time, and does the learner read the material accurately? Treat as non-response to the routine: move to adult small-group or one-to-one teaching for that skill (nonresponders with PALS against adult tutoring [-M]).

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Whether the learner teaching knows the part well enough to correct the partner. Teaching material one has studied helps the one teaching; teaching what one has not met passes on errors, and the correction move needs a key. If unstated, ask: has each learner studied this part, and is there an answer key or criterion the partner can check against?
  • What outcome must change, and how it will be measured. The PALS evidence is positive for early reading skills (letter sounds, first-grade literacy), mixed for comprehension and fluency, and null for mathematics in learners with learning disabilities. If unstated, ask: which skill, measured how (a test of the taught material or a standardised test), and how long afterwards?
  • Which learners, and whether they have already not responded to class-wide teaching. Effects in the claims differ for kindergarten, first grade, learners with learning disabilities, English learners and older learners, and for learners who had not responded, adult tutoring did as well. If unstated, ask: who are the learners, and has a class-wide routine already been tried with them?

Adults in work settings (staff teaching each other a procedure or case) Each colleague prepares one procedure or case, teaches it to another who must perform it or decide a variant, then they swap; the check is a short case decision made alone. Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Teaching after preparing, and teaching that responds to a learner. Learning By Teaching Improves Tutor Learning [+S]: across 28 studies, preparing and then teaching improved the domain knowledge of the learner who taught over studying without expecting to teach (g = 0.56), preparing alone less (g = 0.35), interactive teaching more than non-interactive; a 65-evaluation tutoring meta-analysis found tutors gained in understanding alongside tutees. Both read from abstracts. Its ## Studies bearing on this claim table counts 5 distinct studies. It does not compare teaching with an equal-time alternative such as self-explaining. Carry it to "have the learner prepare, then teach someone who responds", which is steps 3–4.
  • Peer tutoring against no tutoring, and structure. Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement [+S]: tutored learners outperformed controls, and tutors showed the same pattern, in 65 evaluations; in 50 mathematics studies the pooled effect was small (g = 0.333), moderated by stage, design, programme duration, tutor knowledge level, time of day and sample size, not by session length or frequency. Its table counts 4 distinct studies. Structured Peer Tutoring Outperforms Unstructured [+M]: structured programmes produced larger effects than unstructured ones in the first meta-analysis (overall d = .40); structured roles related to social and behavioural effects in a second. Marked M, below its cap, because only one synthesis bears on achievement.
  • Peer-assisted instruction for struggling elementary learners. Peer-assisted instruction and small-group instruction by adults have large, robust short-term effect sizes for K-6 struggling students [+M]: a Campbell systematic review; both methods had average effects around 0.35–0.45 in the short term, robust across specifications. It sets peer-assisted instruction beside adult small-group teaching, not above it.
  • The scripted PALS routine. In early reading, K-PALS [+M] raised kindergarten letter-sound fluency (effect 0.86, one large trial) and first-grade PALS [+M] averaged +0.23 across three studies. Beyond that, effects were positive but not significant: comprehension for learners with learning disabilities [~M] (0.75 average), fluency for the same learners [~M], reading for English learners [~M] (0.31 combined; improvement index +12 percentile points, range +5 to +25 [~M]), comprehension in Fuchs et al. 1997 [~M] (0.48, p = 0.12 after clustering), and passage comprehension in first grade [~M] (0.44, p = 0.07). For beginning readers the WWC rating was potentially positive on alphabetics, no discernible effect on fluency and mixed on comprehension (three domains [~M]).
  • Limits. PALS showed no discernible effects on mathematics for learners with learning disabilities [~M]. For first-grade nonresponders, PALS against one-to-one adult tutoring gave a negative, nonsignificant comprehension effect [-M] (−0.31) and no difference on alphabetics [~M] or fluency [~M]. Cross-age tutoring did not move oral language proficiency [~M]. The Adolescent Literacy evidence rests on one trial with reservations [~M]: of 97 studies reviewed, one met standards.

Do not rank peer teaching above adult small-group or one-to-one teaching from these claims: the one direct comparison (nonresponders) did not favour the peers, and the review that ranks methods puts the two side by side. Do not read a pair's correct answer, or gains on the taught passages, as each learner's learning. Many of the PALS claims draw on the same few trials (Fuchs et al. 1997 and Saenz et al. 2005 appear under more than one claim), so count trials, not claims. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (a scripted peer-teaching routine against an equal-time individual or adult-led alternative, at a stated horizon) and the local observation that stands in for it: each learner's individual check, on new material, before and after.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does structured, reciprocal peer tutoring such as PALS raise reading and mathematics for elementary learners, including those who struggle, over usual instruction? Peer-assisted instruction and small-group instruction by adults have large, robust short-term effect sizes for K-6 struggling students [+M]

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested lesson. A second-grade teacher with 26 pupils and no classroom assistant wants pupils to read more fluently and retell what they read; four pupils have learning disabilities in reading and three are English learners. The table changed the default: pairs are formed from a one-minute read-aloud each pupil does alone, the roles are taught in one 30-minute session with the teacher modelling a misread word and the correction move, and sessions run three times a week for 35 minutes over ten weeks (partner reading with retelling, then naming the main idea of each paragraph, then predict-and-check). The teacher samples three pairs per session and reads a one-minute individual read-aloud with retell from each pupil every second Friday. The pupils with learning disabilities also get a 20-minute adult-led small group twice a week, because the routine's effects for them were not significant; English learners may retell in their first language. A pupil says she wants to read to her younger brother; the designer's objective is fluency and retell on a new passage. They agree she will take a book home in week 6 and report back. The claims cover elementary readers in US trials, not this class; the doses and the extra small group are local choices to observe.

Record: material and parts → starting task per learner and pairing → roles taught and how → sessions held (date, minutes, activities) → pairs sampled, errors heard and corrections given → each learner's individual check on new material, by date → candidate interpretations (partner carried it, error passed on, recitation, not yet readable, non-response) and their basis → changes made (swap, harder material, adult small group) → reobservation at the stated horizon. Preserve each learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of mastery without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Peer teaching, peer discussion, peer explanation and reciprocal teaching. Strategies that run parts of the routine: Peer Tutoring, Reciprocal Peer Tutoring, Learning by Teaching, Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies and training students before peer-tutoring roles. Principle: Peer Learning.

This pattern is scoped to learners teaching one another material they have met, in a scripted, reciprocal session with an individual check. It does not cover commit-and-discuss on conceptual questions (Peer Instruction), peer review of drafts (Structured Peer Review) or group projects. Whether it does better than an equal-time individual or adult-led alternative, for which learners, with what dose and at what horizon, is not shown in the wiki; in early reading it has the most support, in mathematics for learners with learning disabilities none, and for learners who have not responded to class-wide teaching an adult did as well.
