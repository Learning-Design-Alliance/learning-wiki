PAIR-C Framework (Pattern, Agents, Interactions, Relations, Causality)¶

Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 11 claims (4 for, 7 mixed) · 19 studies (7 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 3 review, 2 qualitative, 1 design, 1 theoretical), q2–q4 · 3 of 19 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable policy for teaching an emergent phenomenon: a system-wide pattern (a population's coloring changing over generations, an ant colony's foraging trail, a traffic jam) that arises from many decentralized, uncoordinated interactions with no agent in control. Learners commonly explain such phenomena with a sequential, individualistic causal schema (one agent causes the change, step by step, on purpose), which produces teleological ("the moths chose to change color") and anthropomorphic explanations. PAIR-C names five dimensions on which the two kinds of causal structure differ and has learners use them while they work with an agent-based simulation:

  • Pattern: the macro-level, system-wide change;
  • Agents: the individual entities, and whether control is centralized or decentralized;
  • Interactions: whether interactions are uniform or random, simultaneous or serial, independent or dependent;
  • Relations: how the interaction features combine and compare;
  • Causality: how micro-level behavior sums to the macro-level pattern.

The policy is: elicit each learner's explanation → introduce the five dimensions on an intuitive case → set emergent against sequential causal structure side by side → learners run an agent-based simulation with prompts framed on the dimensions → learners explain what they saw in the dimensions' terms → each stated misconception is answered by naming the dimension it violates → a second case, then a new phenomenon compared explicitly with the first. The knowledge type is a principle (a causal model of how a kind of system works); the outcomes are conceptual-understanding of the inter-level mechanism, fewer misconceptions, near-transfer (the same concept in a new context) and far-transfer (an unrelated emergent phenomenon).

One claim tests this pattern, and only partly. In a single quasi-experiment with 50 pre-service teachers learning natural selection with NetLogo simulations, PAIR-C scaffolding beat traditional instruction using the same simulations on near-transfer gains; deep-understanding gains and the reduction in misconceptions favored PAIR-C but did not reach conventional significance; and neither group transferred to an unrelated emergent phenomenon (PAIR-C scaffolding shows mixed evidence [~M]). Persistence beyond the immediate posttest was not measured, and the claim page does not record how long the instruction ran. Everything else in the policy below (the session arc, the fading of prompts, the cross-domain comparison step, the responses to particular learner answers) is carried from neighbouring claims or is an untested proposal, and each step says which.

How the converted siblings sit beside this one. Cognitive Disequilibrium owns confronting a confident but inaccurate explanation and supporting its resolution; PAIR-C is one configuration of that, with the five dimensions as the resolving vocabulary. Simulations and Immersive Virtual Environments owns practicing inside a simulation with guidance and debrief; here the simulation is used to make an invisible mechanism visible, not to rehearse a performance. Inquiry-based Learning and Scaffolding own guided inquiry and contingent support in general. Transfer of Learning and Analogical Reasoning own comparing cases to transfer a structure, which is the step this pattern's own study suggests is missing for far transfer. The concept attainment pattern is the nearest converted pattern for teaching a category from contrasted instances. This page does not repeat their models.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Before any instruction, ask each learner to explain one emergent phenomenon in writing or aloud ("why are most moths in this sooty forest dark?"). Record the causal structure the explanation uses: an agent that wants or chooses, a single cause acting step by step, need-driven change, use and disuse, or decentralized variation and selection. Record prior knowledge of the domain and of simulations. Keep the answer rather than a label such as novice.
The phenomenon Whether the target really is emergent (many agents, no controller, the pattern arising from their interactions) or a sequential process (digestion, a production line), which PAIR-C does not fit. Which simulation, video or physical model shows agent-level behavior. Which second phenomenon in another domain shares the structure.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-self-paced, online-instructor-led, workplace-clinical), single-session or days-weeks, devices available (computers, phones only, none), group size, whether a teacher is present to answer misconceptions as they arise, minutes per session.
Learner-valued goal Ask what the learner wants: to explain evolution to their own pupils, to pass a biology course, to understand a system at work (epidemics, markets, crowds). Record disagreement with the designer objective, and any belief the topic touches (natural selection can).
Designer objective Which of these the assessment will weigh: explaining the target phenomenon at both levels, removing named misconceptions, applying the explanation to the same concept in a new context, or recognizing the same causal structure in a different phenomenon.
Outcome Shallow items, deep (inter-level causal) items, near-transfer items, far-transfer items on an unrelated emergent phenomenon, and misconceptions coded from open explanations against a named set of categories, each scored apart; immediate or delayed-retention. The tested study used all five.

A request to "use PAIR-C for evolution" does not specify these. Ask what phenomenon, which simulation, what learners currently say, and which transfer the assessment will weigh before choosing the arc below.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

The default arc is the earlier page's six steps, spread over four sessions, with a seventh step proposed for far transfer. Minutes and the number of sessions are proposals; the claim page does not report the study's dose.

Session 1 (about 50 minutes): from the learner's explanation to the dimensions.

  1. Elicit (5–10 min). Each learner writes an explanation of the target phenomenon before any teaching, and the teacher sorts them by causal structure (above). Evidence status: untested here; surfacing the prior explanation is the precondition conflict-based instruction [+S] works from (science conceptual learning in 218 studies, g = 1.10 for cognitive-conflict strategies, k = 150, with a small experiment finding that staged contradictions helped only learners who reported being confused).
  2. Introduce the five dimensions on an intuitive case (10 min), such as ant pheromone trails: no ant directs the trail; it forms from many ants each following local cues. Evidence status: from the earlier page; part of the tested PAIR-C package, not isolated.
  3. Set emergent against sequential causal structure side by side (10 min): a two-column table, one row per dimension (decentralized versus a single controlling agent; random, simultaneous interactions versus serial steps; converging, collective change versus incremental, cumulative change). Fill the sequential column from the learners' own explanations in step 1 where possible. Evidence status: part of the tested package; comparing cases [~M] found larger gains when the principle comes after a prompted comparison, though in one algebra study comparison raised procedural knowledge, not conceptual knowledge.
  4. Run the simulation with dimension-framed prompts (20–25 min). Learners manipulate an agent-based model of the target (Peppered Moths, Rock Pocket Mice in NetLogo) with an annotated diagram or short video beside it marking agent-level events, and a prompt for each dimension ("Is any moth controlling the change? Point to where the color shift happens across the population"). Evidence status: tested as a package in the PAIR-C study [~M] against the same simulations without the framing; guidance added to simulation-based inquiry [+M] improved learning outcomes (d = 0.49) and inquiry skills (d = 0.41) independent of guidance type, as reported second-hand in a review.

Session 2 (about 40 minutes): explaining and correcting.

  1. Structured reflection in the dimensions' terms (15 min). Each learner rewrites their step 1 explanation, one sentence per dimension, then compares it with the original. Evidence status: from the earlier page; untested as a separate step.
  2. Answer each stated misconception by naming the dimension it violates (throughout, and 15–20 min of targeted cases). "The moths chose to change color" → Agents: no moth controls the population's coloring; Interactions: variation is random, survival depends on each moth's match to the bark. Name the misconception, say why it is wrong, give the correct account. Evidence status: instruction that confronts misconceptions [+S] (a meta-analysis and a two-decade review of refutation text, both read from abstracts) produced conceptual change more often than standard exposition; contingent scaffolding [+M] outperformed fixed or absent support, mostly in one-to-one tutoring.

Session 3 (about 40 minutes): a second case in the same domain, prompts faded. A second simulation (Rock Pocket Mice after Peppered Moths). Prompts shrink from one per dimension to a single "which dimension explains what you just saw?" and then to none; learners write the five-dimension explanation unaided. Evidence status: fading is an untested proposal here. The study's significant result was on near transfer, which is what this session practises.

Session 4 (about 40 minutes): a new phenomenon, compared explicitly.

  1. Cross-domain comparison (proposed addition). Give an emergent phenomenon from another domain (geese flight formation, a traffic jam, a forest-fire spread model) and have learners fill the same two-column table for it beside the natural-selection table, then state what the two share. Evidence status: untested for emergent phenomena. Neither group in the PAIR-C study transferred to an unrelated emergent phenomenon, and the claim page reports the authors' suggestion that explicit cross-domain analogical comparison may be needed; comparing analogous cases [+M] improved transfer of a principle with novice undergraduates (48% against 19% in a negotiation experiment), carried here to a different domain and a different kind of principle.

When a learner moves on (proposed, untested): from session 1–2 to session 3 when their rewritten explanation of the target names decentralized control and random variation without teleological or need-driven language; from session 3 to session 4 when they do so on the second case without prompts. A learner who still explains with a controlling agent after session 3 gets the one-to-one response in the table below, not a fourth simulation.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Whether the phenomenon is genuinely emergent. The five dimensions exist to contrast decentralized, collective causation with sequential, individual causation; a sequential process gives the framework nothing to contrast. If unstated, ask: is there any agent controlling the outcome, or does it arise from many agents' local interactions?
  • Which transfer the assessment weighs. The one study found a significant effect on near transfer only, and no far transfer in either group. If unstated, ask: will learners be tested on the same concept in a new context, or on a different phenomenon altogether?
  • What learners currently say, and who they are. The tested learners were pre-service teachers; the misconceptions coded were teleological reasoning, anthropomorphism, directed variation and use-and-disuse. If unstated, ask: what does a learner write when asked to explain the phenomenon now, and how old are they?

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • The pattern itself. PAIR-C scaffolding shows mixed evidence for deep understanding and reduced misconceptions in emergent-phenomena instruction [~M]: a quasi-experiment (no random individual assignment) with 50 pre-service teachers (24 PAIR-C, 26 control), both groups using the same NetLogo simulations, assessed pre and post. Near-transfer gains favored PAIR-C (Condition × Time interaction F = 4.64, p = .036); deep-understanding gains (p = .059) and misconception reduction (p = .060 for categories, p = .095 for frequency) favored it without reaching p < .05; neither group achieved far transfer to geese flight formation. Coded q2 · i1 · causal · r2. A nonsignificant trend does not establish an effect, and nothing here tells which feature of the package (annotations, prompts, feedback) did the work.
  • Guidance with simulations and inquiry. Supplementing simulation-based inquiry with learner guidance modestly improves learning outcomes [+M] (d = 0.49 on learning outcomes, d = 0.41 on inquiry skills, independent of guidance type, reported from D'Angelo et al. through a review) and Guidance during inquiry learning improves post-inquiry learning outcomes [+M] (a meta-analysis, d = 0.50; its table gathers 4 distinct studies, including the PAIR-C study as a qualifying one). These bear on whether to guide the simulation at all, not on PAIR-C's dimensions as the guidance.
  • Confronting the misconception. Instruction that confronts science misconceptions produces conceptual change more often than standard exposition [+S]: a meta-analysis and a review of refutation text, both read from abstracts, neither with a recorded pooled effect; its table gathers 4 distinct studies. Conflict-based instruction improves science conceptual learning [+S]: 218 studies, g = 1.10 (adjusted 0.93), with wide heterogeneity; its table gathers 5 distinct studies. The qualification on the same page: in one experiment contradictions helped only learners who reported being confused, and no study isolates disequilibrium as the mechanism. So name the misconception and resolve it; do not expect a surprising simulation run alone to do it.
  • Contingent correction. Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support [+M]: five studies, mostly one-to-one tutoring; its table gathers 20 distinct studies across narrower claims, including the PAIR-C study. Carried to a teacher answering misconceptions during simulation work.
  • Limits on transfer. Comparing analogous cases improves transfer [+M] (its table gathers 5 distinct studies) is the basis for the proposed cross-domain step and is untested on emergent phenomena. Simulation games support near transfer, but evidence for long-term or far transfer is less robust [~W] (a review citing meta-analyses) and comparing cases raised procedural knowledge, not conceptual knowledge, in one algebra study [~M] limit what to expect. Presenting multiple cases from different perspectives supports transfer in ill-structured domains [~M] adds that the approach presupposes prior knowledge to compare across cases, a reason to fix the target domain first and add the second phenomenon later.

Do not report PAIR-C as significantly improving deep understanding or reducing misconceptions: the claim page records trends. Do not rank it against other conceptual-change designs (refutation text, predict-observe-explain): no claim compares them. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (PAIR-C with and without a cross-domain comparison step, on far transfer and at a delay, with school-age learners) and the local observation that would stand in for it.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does adding guidance to inquiry and simulation work improve what learners learn? Guidance during inquiry learning improves post-inquiry learning outcomes (d = 0.50) [+M]
  • Does instruction that names and confronts a science misconception change learners' conceptions more than standard exposition? Instruction that confronts science misconceptions (conceptual-conflict interventions, refutation text) produces conceptual change more often than standard exposition [+S]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Every claim the earlier page cited is used in the model above: the PAIR-C study [~M] (the earlier page marked it [~W]) and contingent scaffolding [+M]. Claims found in the search and read, but not used as core evidence:

  • Newell's complex systems theory of interdisciplinarity fails to explain integration [~W]: a theoretical claim about interdisciplinary studies that shares the words "complex systems"; it does not bear on teaching emergent phenomena.
  • A demonstration-plus-guided-inquiry-plus-computational-model sequence helped most students predict increasing gravitational-wave frequency, but only about half predicted increasing amplitude [~W]: one classroom design report in high-school physics with no comparison; a reminder that a guided model sequence can leave one misconception in place while fixing another, not evidence for PAIR-C.

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested lesson. A secondary biology department (adolescent, classroom, days-weeks, one teacher for 28 students, computers shared in pairs) wants students to explain antibiotic resistance and to recognize the same mechanism elsewhere. The teacher brings the students' own pre-unit answers, most saying "the bacteria got used to the antibiotic". The table changed the default: the plain-words version of each dimension; the students' sentences as the sequential column and as the misconception cards; a demonstration run before pairs use the simulation; individual rewrites after paired work; and a session 4 comparing resistance with peppered moths. A student says she wants to understand why her doctor told her to finish the course of tablets; the department's objective is the inter-level explanation. They agree to include one item on finishing a course of antibiotics in the far-transfer test, scored separately. The PAIR-C claim covers adults learning natural selection at an immediate posttest, not this age, phenomenon or horizon; these are local choices to observe, not settled ones.

Record: phenomenon and why it is emergent → the second phenomenon used for comparison → each learner's starting explanation and its causal structure → simulation runs, settings changed, predictions made → prompts given and which dimension each named → misconceptions stated and the dimension used to answer each → rewritten explanation per session → prompts withdrawn and when → near- and far-transfer explanations, scored per dimension → candidate interpretations (model changed, wording changed only, recitation without application, partner's account copied) and their basis → reobservation at the stated delay. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of conceptual change without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Simulation, prediction, prompts, misconception probes, comparing cases, analogies, feedback, reflection activities and visual imagery. Principles the pattern draws on: Cognitive Disequilibrium, Simulations and Immersive Virtual Environments, Inquiry-based Learning, Scaffolding, Analogical Reasoning and Transfer of Learning. Theory: Constructivism.

This pattern is scoped to explaining one emergent phenomenon with an agent-based model and the five dimensions, at lesson and short-unit grain. Sequential processes and phenomena with a real controlling agent need other configurations. Whether PAIR-C produces deeper understanding or fewer misconceptions than other conceptual-change designs, whether it transfers across domains with an explicit comparison step, whether it works with school-age learners, and whether its gains last remain to be tested.
