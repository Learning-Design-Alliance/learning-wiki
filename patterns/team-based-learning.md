# Team Based Learning

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 25 claims (11 for, 12 mixed, 2 against) · 24 studies (8 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 2 associational, 2 design, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 8 of 24 report an effect size · 18 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

Team-Based Learning is a unit-level cycle in which learners study material before class, take a closed-book individual readiness test, retake the same test in a stable team with immediate feedback, appeal items in writing, receive clarification targeted at what the tests showed, and then spend most of class time on team application exercises. Its readiness-assurance sequence is a response-dependent policy: what the instructor does next depends on the individual and team responses. It instantiates part of the [retrieval practice principle](../principles/retrieval-practice.md).

**Which parts the retrieval evidence bears on, and which it does not.** The individual test is a retrieval attempt after study, and the team retest supplies corrective feedback; the retrieval and feedback claims bear on those two steps, and on reusing readiness items later. They say nothing about team formation, team size or permanence, appeals, application exercises, simultaneous public reporting, peer evaluation or the grading weights. Those parts rest on other evidence (cooperative learning and active learning, below), on argument, or on nothing recorded in this wiki. **No claim page in this wiki reports a study of Team-Based Learning itself.** The study configurations below are evidence about components; the policy and its branches are an untested design proposal.

## Sequence and conditional policy

1. **Set the course frame.** Form teams and agree the grading scheme and peer evaluation. *No retrieval evidence bears on this step.*
2. **Pre-class study.** Learners study assigned material. Retrieval evidence assumes material has been studied; it does not say that reading alone is enough first instruction for every learner or topic.
3. **Individual readiness test.** Closed-book, individual, usually multiple choice. *Retrieval evidence bears here*: testing against restudy, item format, initial success. Multiple choice without feedback carries a recorded risk of learning the lures, which the next step is placed to correct.
4. **Team readiness test with immediate feedback.** The same items, retaken by the team until correct. *Feedback evidence bears here*, though team discussion and feedback arrive together and the studies separate neither from the other in this form.
5. **Appeals.** Teams argue in writing that an item was flawed or their answer defensible. *No retrieval evidence bears on this step.*
6. **Targeted clarification.** The instructor explains only what the individual and team responses show was not understood. Evidence on combining peer discussion with instructor explanation bears by analogy.
7. **Application exercises, public reporting, peer evaluation.** Teams work a shared problem and report simultaneously. *Retrieval evidence does not bear on these.* Active-learning evidence bears on lecture replacement in general, not on this exercise design.
8. **Reobserve.** Probe individual recall and application of earlier units' content at a delay, with new items. This step is a proposal added here; it is not part of the canonical sequence.

| Observation | Discriminating probe | Proposed action, with its uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Individual score low, team score high on the same items | Was the material studied, or studied and not retained, or were wrong lures chosen? Give an individual short-answer probe on the missed items at the next session and ask what was read. | If the individual probe is now correct, the team feedback may have corrected it; keep the cycle and recheck later. If it is still wrong, the team result was carried by others: offer individual follow-up retrieval with feedback. Neither branch is validated. |
| Individual and team both wrong on an item | A shared misconception, a flawed item, or reading that did not cover it. Read the appeals, check the item against the reading, and ask a team to explain its choice. | Revise or drop a flawed item. Otherwise give targeted clarification on that concept and a new retrieval item on it later; do not reteach the whole reading. |
| Team reaches the correct option only after several scratches | Elimination by guessing, or discussion that changed members' understanding. Give an individual isomorphic item later with no discussion. | If the isomorphic item fails, treat the team result as not yet understood and plan another retrieval opportunity with feedback. |
| Readiness scores high, application exercise weak | Recall of facts without their use; an ambiguous problem; team process. Give an individual application item with the needed facts supplied. | If it succeeds with facts supplied, the gap is in use, not recall: work the application with modelling. If it fails, revisit the concept. Retrieval evidence does not predict this case. |
| One member dominates, others are silent | Team dynamics, uneven preparation or language demand. Compare members' individual test results and private explanations; read peer evaluations. | Adjust norms or roles, or team composition. This is a team-process judgement; the retrieval evidence offers nothing here. |

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Keep the individual test closed-book and low-stakes enough to be a learning event.** [Classroom quizzing](../claims/classroom-quizzing-improves-exam-performance-across-grades-and-content.md) [+M] improved classroom exam performance in eighth-grade science, college statistics and introductory psychology, including on completely new questions in one comparison of two sections; these are reported second-hand in one review chapter and not yet checked against their sources. None was a team-based course.
- **Follow multiple-choice readiness items with feedback.** [Multiple-choice tests without feedback](../claims/multiple-choice-lures-can-be-learned-as-false-knowledge.md) [-M] led students to produce the lures they had selected on a later cued-recall or short-answer test, even with an overall benefit; second-hand from the same chapter, one of two entries passes the judge. This is the case for the team retest's immediate feedback, and for feedback on the individual test too if the team retest is ever dropped.
- **Expect feedback to matter most for errors.** [Feedback after retrieval](../claims/feedback-enhances-retrieval-practice.md) [+M] raised retention after incorrect responses and added little after correct ones in a word-pair experiment, and classroom quizzes with feedback showed larger effects than those without, in a between-study comparison another meta-analysis did not reproduce. Team feedback in this pattern comes with discussion, so the studies do not isolate it.
- **Combine team discussion with targeted instructor explanation.** In genetics courses using clicker questions, [peer discussion](../claims/peer-discussion-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+W] improved performance on a later isomorphic question, and peer discussion followed by instructor explanation gave larger gains than either alone. These are classroom studies of peer instruction (one with no control group, one quasi-experimental), not of team readiness tests. One entry passes the judge on its abstract; the other could not be confirmed from its abstract.
- **Treat the application phase as active learning, not as retrieval.** [Active learning](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [~M] beat traditional lecturing on undergraduate STEM exams and concept inventories (0.47 SD across 158 studies), and only high-intensity active learning narrowed achievement gaps. Team-Based Learning is one form among many in those syntheses, which do not estimate it separately. Not settled on the abstracts.
- **Base team rewards on individual learning.** [Group rewards with individual accountability](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+W] were associated with larger achievement effects (median +.32 against +.07) in a best-evidence synthesis of school studies, which also reports structured interaction working without rewards. These are school-age cooperative-learning studies, not this pattern; they bear on its grading, not its retrieval steps.

Do not rank these against one another, and do not read an effect from a laboratory word-pair or a lecture course as a forecast for a team-based course. A precise abstention names the missing comparison: no recorded study compares this sequence with the same content taught otherwise.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented for illustration; not a tested course.* A second-year pharmacology unit: students read a chapter on one drug class, take a ten-item closed-book individual test, retake it as teams of six with scratch cards, and may appeal. The designer's objective is one-week recall of mechanisms and their use in choosing a drug for a case; several students say they value being able to explain a choice to a patient. The instructor sees most teams scratch twice on one interaction item while the individual test shows a common wrong option, clarifies that interaction for ten minutes, and runs a case exercise. A week later each student answers two new short-answer items on the unit, one recall and one application, with no notes.

Record: **item and format → time since study, notes allowed → individual response and confidence → team response and number of attempts → appeal, if any → candidate interpretations → clarification given → individual response to a new item at the stated horizon**. Keep individual and team records separate; a team score is not evidence about any one member. Preserve the learners' stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise the record, but must not fill missing fields or assign probabilities to states without a calibrated model.

## Related Patterns
- [Cooperative Learning](cooperative-learning.md) — shares positive interdependence and individual accountability; TBL adds the readiness assurance cycle and simultaneous reporting
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — TBL's application phase is typically case-based; the Harvard method variant pairs naturally with TBL team structure

## Examples

**Medical education — pharmacology:** Many medical schools (e.g., Vanderbilt, Duke-NUS) use TBL for pre-clerkship sciences: students complete assigned readings, take iRATs on drug mechanisms, then teams work patient-case application exercises choosing and defending treatment decisions.

**Business education — management courses:** Michaelsen's original context: teams of 5–7 in large organizational-behavior courses work through group-dynamics cases, with peer evaluation feeding into grades each unit.

**Large undergraduate STEM courses:** Introductory biology and chemistry courses at research universities have adopted TBL to convert lecture halls into team-application sessions, using digital tRAT tools such as [ InteDashboard](https://intedashboard.com) or [Top Hat](https://tophat.com) for immediate team feedback.

## Description
Team-Based Learning is a complete instructional strategy, not an occasional group activity. It resembles a [flipped classroom](flipped-classroom.md) in that students first meet the fundamental material outside class (Demetry, 2010), but differs in two ways: in-class application runs through structured team interaction around specific problems, and every unit opens with a formal assessment of how well the pre-class material landed before any application work begins. Students are assigned to permanent, instructor-formed teams of 5–7 that stay together for the entire term. Each instructional unit follows a fixed cycle: individual pre-reading, an individual readiness test (iRAT), the same test taken as a team (tRAT), written appeals, and then the majority of class time spent on team application exercises in which all teams work simultaneously on the same significant problem and report their choices publicly. The design solves the chronic failure modes of group work — free-riding, unequal preparation, and shallow discussion — by making individual accountability and team accountability mutually reinforcing.

## Implications

TBL operationalizes the finding that active, collaborative learning improves exam performance [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S] while addressing its central risk: collaboration without individual accountability degrades into social loafing. The readiness assurance process forces every student to prepare before class, and the team test converts individual preparation into immediate, high-quality peer discussion — students must defend answers to teammates, which produces argumentation and feedback at a scale no instructor could provide alone. Public team reporting creates simultaneous, whole-class engagement rather than the sequential, disengaged reporting typical of traditional group presentations.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Permanent, instructor-formed teams balanced for prior achievement and diverse enough to generate genuine disagreement
- A graded individual readiness assessment that students can pass only by completing pre-class preparation
- Application exercises with a *specific* defensible answer that all teams work on simultaneously
- Enough class time per unit for the full readiness cycle plus application (typically 75+ minutes)
- A grading scheme that weights both individual and team performance

#### Constraints
- Requires substantial front-loaded design work: readiness tests, appeals procedures, and well-crafted application problems for every unit
- Fails when application problems have no single defensible answer or are trivially easy — teams disengage or converge without discussion
- Poor fit for content that cannot be front-loaded as pre-class reading (e.g., highly sequential skill-building where novices cannot self-teach the next step) [~M]
- Fixed teams can entrench dysfunctional dynamics if the instructor does not monitor and intervene; poorly formed teams suppress minority viewpoints [~M]
- High-stakes team tests can produce coercive conformity rather than genuine discussion if grading is misconfigured

#### Grain Size
Unit and course — the readiness assurance cycle repeats per unit, and team formation and grading design are course-level decisions. A single lesson can use the cycle, but the accountability and team-cohesion benefits accrue only across repeated cycles.

### Target Goals
- Deep conceptual understanding and application of course content to novel problems [Active learning improves exam performance.](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+S]
- Teamwork, communication, and peer-feedback skills valued in professional practice
- Individual accountability for pre-class preparation, freeing class time for higher-order work
- Development of evaluative judgment through peer discussion and appeals [Assessment for learning improves achievement.](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]

### Target Learners
- Large-enrollment undergraduate courses where discussion is otherwise impractical
- Professional programs (medicine, nursing, business, law) where teamwork is a licensure-relevant competency
- Students who under-prepare for class without graded accountability; TBL's structure particularly benefits these learners [~M]

### Theory
#### Supporting

- [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) — team discussion exposes students to peers' reasoning, making diverse problem-solving approaches observable and imitable
- [Self-Determination Theory](../theories/self-determination-theory.md) — permanent teams build relatedness; appeals and team autonomy support competence and autonomy needs [Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation.](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) — moving first exposure to pre-class reading reserves limited class time for application, where collaborative processing distributes load across team members

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Constructivism](../theories/constructivism.md) — TBL is highly structured and instructor-scripted; pure discovery-oriented constructivists would critique its fixed sequence, though the structure is precisely what prevents unproductive group work

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Pre-class preparation** — Students complete assigned readings or media independently ([assigned readings](../elements/assigned-readings.md))
2. **Individual Readiness Test (iRAT)** — 10–20 closed-book multiple-choice questions on the pre-class material ([assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
3. **Team Readiness Test (tRAT)** — Teams retake the same test with immediate feedback (scratch cards or digital), discussing until they reach consensus ([feedback](../elements/feedback.md))
4. **Appeals** — Teams may challenge test items in writing, requiring them to re-examine the source material and construct an argument ([argumentation](../elements/argumentation.md))
5. **Mini-lecture on demand** — Instructor addresses only the concepts the readiness data show teams actually struggled with
6. **Application exercise** — All teams simultaneously work the same significant problem with a specific defensible answer ([application](../elements/application.md))
7. **Public reporting and discussion** — Teams commit to answers simultaneously (e.g., colored cards), then defend choices in whole-class discussion ([class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))

### Affordances
- [Collaborative Learning](collaborative-learning.md) — TBL is a fully specified implementation of this principle, adding the accountability structures (iRAT, appeals, grading) that generic group work lacks
- [Case-Based Learning](case-based-learning.md) — application exercises typically take the form of authentic cases; TBL supplies the team and accountability machinery that case discussion needs to work at scale
- [Constructive Alignment](constructive-alignment.md) — the grading scheme (individual preparation + team performance + peer evaluation) must align with the collaborative outcomes the sequence targets

### Personalization

**Novices with no prior knowledge:** Keep readiness tests focused on foundational terminology and concepts; reserve application exercises for guided in-class work with more instructor scaffolding in early units.

**Learners with some background knowledge:** Raise the difficulty of readiness tests toward application-level items so preparation remains challenging; let stronger students take rotating roles (scribe, skeptic) within teams.

**Learners with anxiety or low confidence:** The team test provides a low-risk first venue to voice ideas before whole-class discussion; explicitly establish team norms that all answers must be heard before voting. Avoid cold-calling individuals to defend team answers early in the term.

**Learners with diverse prior knowledge in the same cohort:** Instructor-formed, deliberately heterogeneous teams are the design's answer — the tRAT discussion is where prepared students teach unprepared ones, but only if the iRAT makes preparation non-optional.

**Learners with language or learning differences:** Allow extended time on the iRAT; provide glossaries with pre-reading; ensure application problems are written in plain language so reading load doesn't mask conceptual reasoning.
