# Team-Based Learning (TBL)

## Description
Team-Based Learning (TBL) resembles a [flipped classroom](flipped-learning.md) in that students first encounter fundamental material outside class (Demetry, 2010), but differs in two key ways: in-class application happens through structured team interaction around specific problems, and every unit begins with a formal assessment of how well students absorbed the pre-class material before any application work starts. TBL is explicitly "a model for course instruction, not just a teaching strategy that can be employed on occasion" (Fink, 2004) — adopting it, per creator Larry Michaelsen (2004), requires a genuine paradigm shift: the instructor moves from being the source and distributor of content toward being a designer and manager of learning experiences, and students move from passively receiving content toward being engaged, accountable participants responsible for their own preparation.

Michaelsen (2004) organizes this shift around **four essential principles**: (1) **proper team formation and management** — permanent teams of 5-7 members, formed transparently by the instructor in class specifically to break up pre-existing subgroups (since perceived favoritism toward prior relationships can undermine team cohesion), with student talents, resources, and liabilities deliberately distributed evenly across teams; (2) **student accountability**, both individual and team, established through graded individual and team readiness tests, team performance on application activities and summative assessments, and peer assessment of teammates' contributions; (3) **team activities** designed to promote both learning and team cohesion, following the **"4S" framework** (below); and (4) **frequent, immediate feedback**, needed both for learning/retention and for team development, since teams need ongoing signals about how well they're working together as much as whether their answers are correct.

Each module follows a **three-phase sequence**: a **preparation phase** (readiness assurance process, ~50-70 minutes), an **application phase** (~3-5 hours across sessions), and an **assessment phase** — after which the cycle repeats for the next module.

**Preparation phase (Readiness Assurance Process, RAP).** Students engage with assigned pre-class materials (readings, videos, audio), then on the first in-class day for that module take a two-part **Readiness Assurance Test (RAT)**: first an **individual Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT)** completed alone, then a **team Readiness Assurance Test (tRAT)** — the identical questions, answered as a team — with the two scores averaged (weighting can vary by course). The tRAT uses the **Immediate Feedback Assessment Technique (IF-AT)**: a scratch card where each row corresponds to one question's multiple-choice options; the team discusses, agrees on an answer, and scratches it to immediately learn whether it's correct — with partial credit available for a second, third, or fourth attempt, which keeps the team discussing an item they got wrong rather than simply moving on. Teams may then formally **appeal** any missed question, citing the source material to justify why their answer should be accepted; the instructor reviews appeals after class and can adjust scores. Finally, the instructor reviews tRAT patterns for continued difficulty and delivers a brief, targeted **corrective instruction** before application work begins.

**Application phase.** Teams apply concepts to make predictions, solve problems, and construct explanations for complex problems, with activities starting simple and growing more complex as the module progresses. Design should follow the **4S framework** (Michaelsen, 2004; Michaelsen & Knight, 2004): a **significant problem** (meaningful and complex enough to drive real team discussion); the **same problem** for every team simultaneously (increasing investment in the eventual class-wide comparison and debrief); a problem with a **specific choice** — a clearly-defined answer, not an open-ended essay; and **simultaneous reporting**, where all teams reveal their answer at the same time, which both prevents a team from silently switching its answer after hearing others and creates real engagement through anticipation and mild competition. Feedback during this phase comes from the instructor, but often just as much from teammates and rival teams comparing and defending answers.

**Assessment phase.** The module closes with graded assessment — team or individual problems, traditional exams, peer evaluations, or other formats — before the entire three-phase cycle begins again with the next module.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Genuine instructor buy-in to the paradigm shift TBL requires — designing application exercises and managing team dynamics, rather than delivering content, becomes the primary instructional task
- Permanent teams of 5-7 members, deliberately balanced for talent/resource/liability distribution and formed transparently to avoid perceived favoritism
- A mechanism for immediate team feedback during the tRAT (e.g., IF-AT scratch cards or an equivalent)
#### Constraints
- TBL is designed as a whole-course model, not an occasional activity — adopting only isolated pieces (e.g., team quizzes without the full RAP/application/assessment cycle) forgoes much of the structure's rationale
- The readiness assurance process only functions if students genuinely complete pre-class preparation; a cohort with widespread non-compliance undermines both the iRAT/tRAT comparison and the application phase that depends on baseline competence
#### Grain Size
[course] (Michaelsen suggests organizing a course into roughly 5-7 modules)

### Target Goals
- Genuine comprehension of pre-class material, verified before application work begins, rather than assumed
- Team cohesion and communication skill development alongside content mastery

### Target Learners
- Learners in courses meeting frequently and over enough duration to sustain permanent teams across multiple modules

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Social Interdependence](../principles/social-interdependence.md) [+M] — TBL's permanent, deliberately-balanced teams and shared accountability structure are a direct application of genuine positive interdependence
- [Formative Assessment](../patterns/formative-assessment.md) [+M] — the iRAT/tRAT and IF-AT mechanism are formative assessment delivered with unusually immediate feedback

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Preparation**: Assign pre-class materials for the module; on the first class day, administer the iRAT (individual) followed immediately by the tRAT (team, same questions) using an immediate-feedback mechanism like IF-AT.
2. Allow teams to appeal specific missed questions with cited justification; review and adjust scores after class.
3. Deliver brief corrective instruction targeting the specific gaps the RAT process revealed.
4. **Application**: Run 4S-framework team activities (significant, same, specific-choice, simultaneous-report problems) over the bulk of the module's class time, increasing in complexity.
5. **Assessment**: Administer individual and/or team graded assessment to close the module.
6. Repeat the cycle for the next module.
