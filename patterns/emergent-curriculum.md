# Emergent Curriculum

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 14 claims (10 for, 4 mixed) · 17 studies (5 review, 5 qualitative, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 2 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 3 of 17 report an effect size · 11 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable policy for letting the **topic** of a stretch of teaching come from interests learners express, while the teacher keeps responsibility for what is learned through it: **a planned entry point (a book, an object, a question, a set of materials) → the teacher observes and writes down what learners ask, say and keep returning to → a sustained interest is webbed, with learners or among the adults, to content and activities worth learning → the thread is followed with new materials, visitors and projects, with an adult guiding the activity toward the webbed goals → observation continues and the web is redrawn → the thread closes on a product, a display or a shared account of what was found, and the next entry point is offered**. The planned unit is set aside rather than rejected, and the record of observations doubles as a running assessment of what learners are learning.

The intended change is in the depth of engagement and in learner-initiated questioning and problem-solving (`motivation-affect`, `persistence`), and, where the web names target ideas and an adult guides toward them, in understanding of those ideas (`conceptual-understanding`). The approach's track record, on the earlier page, is concentrated in early childhood and the first years of school (`child`, `pre-k-12`); with older students the same move is made by turning a personal interest into a research question that is then studied formally.

**No claim in this wiki tests emergent curriculum as such**, against a planned curriculum or anything else. What the wiki holds is: two single-classroom or two-student qualitative cases in which departing from or extending beyond a designed task went with deeper self-directed inquiry; one qualitative co-design study on tools for balancing student interests with standards; a meta-analysis of guided play in early childhood; a meta-analysis and two other studies showing that unguided discovery learns less than explicit instruction for novices; one randomised study of personalising problem contexts to students' interests; general reviews on task value and autonomy support; and a single case where an open, partner-led project narrowed to its easiest meaning. Each is carried to this pattern by extrapolation, and the page says how far. The sequence is the earlier page's, with each step's evidence status stated; the response-dependent branches are **untested design proposals**.

How it sits beside converted pages: the [Free-Choice Learning Environment Design principle](../principles/free-choice-learning-environment-design.md) owns settings with no imposed curriculum at all (a museum floor, an ungraded studio block); this pattern is the case where a teacher still owns a curriculum and lets learners' interests choose the route through it. The [Autonomy principle](../principles/autonomy.md) holds bounded choice and the case against controlling incentives; the [Inquiry-based Learning principle](../principles/inquiry-based-learning.md) and the [Problem-Based Learning pattern](problem-based-learning.md) hold how much guidance an investigation needs and when. This page applies those models to a topic that learners chose and does not repeat them.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Age or stage (`child`, `adolescent`, `adult`); what the learners already know of the likely topics and of the moves the thread will need (asking a question, finding a source, making a model); what they have been seen to choose and return to in the last week or two. Preserve unknowns rather than assuming the class shares one interest. |
| What counts as an expressed interest | Who raised it, how many learners took it up, whether they came back to it unprompted on another day, and what exactly they asked. A passing remark and a question several learners pursue for days are different observations. |
| Context and activities | Setting (`classroom`, `online-instructor-led`, `online-self-paced`, `workplace-clinical`), how many adults are present, how much timetable is free to follow a thread (`single-session`, `days-weeks`, `term-plus`), what materials, visitors and outings can be arranged at a few days' notice. |
| Required content | Which standards, outcomes or programme goals must still be covered this term or year, and who checks. This sets how far a thread may wander and what the web must connect to. |
| Learner-valued goal | What the learners want to find out, make or do with the topic (build a model, help the birds, make a game that others play). Keep it apart from the designer objective and record where they diverge. |
| Designer objective | Which capability the thread is meant to build: concepts in the topic's domain (`concept`, `principle`), inquiry or research moves (`complex-skill`), literacy or numeracy practised through the topic, or interest itself (`attitude-motivation`). Name the one that will be observed. |
| Outcome | What will be recorded as evidence of learning: the teacher's dated notes, learners' products and explanations, a closing account, a check on the required content; and when (end of thread, end of term). |

A request to "use an emergent curriculum" does not specify these. Ask who decides when an interest is real, what must still be covered, and how much time a thread may take before choosing the entry point or the web.

## Sequence and conditional policy

The default sequence is the earlier page's five steps, with the closing step and the doses added. Steps 1, 2 and 5 are untested; steps 3 and 4 have neighbouring claims, carried by extrapolation; step 6 is a proposal.

1. **Offer a planned entry point, held loosely.** Start from a planned activity or topic (a read-aloud, a set of materials, an object, a visit) chosen because it has several directions a question could go. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. [IDC's interest cycle](../claims/interest-cycle-triggering-immersing-extending.md) [+W] (a theoretical commentary) argues that interests should be treated as launching points for deeper immersion, not as fixed topics.
2. **Observe and record continuously.** During ordinary activity, write down learners' questions, comments and what they return to, with the date and who said it. Proposed dose: a few lines per session, and a 10–15 minute review of the notes at the end of each day or week by the adults together. An interest is "sustained" when several learners return to it unprompted on at least two separate days (proposal). Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
3. **Web the interest to content worth learning.** Brainstorm connections between the learners' questions and possible content, activities, materials, visitors and outings: with the learners (10–20 minutes for young children, proposal), by the teacher alone, or with co-teachers and assistants. Mark on the web the target ideas the thread is for, and which required standards or outcomes each branch reaches. Before launching a larger thread, try the opening activity on colleagues "in student hat" to see whether it raises questions that lead to the target ideas or into dead ends. Evidence status: [co-design tools for standards analysis, student-interest data and launch rehearsal](../claims/co-design-tools-support-balancing-standards-and-student-interests.md) [+M]: participants in one two-year co-design partnership (teachers designing secondary science units) credited unpacking standards, interest data and rehearsal with balancing the two goals; self-report, no outcome measure, and not an emergent curriculum.
4. **Set the plan aside and follow the thread, with an adult guiding.** Bring in books, materials, a visitor or a project that serves the learners' question; let learners propose solutions and lead discussion; the adult asks questions, models, supplies tools and keeps the activity aimed at the webbed ideas. Evidence status: [Spontaneous responsiveness to real-world events in PBL](../claims/spontaneous-authenticity-in-pbl-deepens-student-directed-inquiry.md) [+M] (one bilingual third-grade classroom: more self-initiated questioning and problem-solving when the teacher left the written unit to respond to an unplanned event than in a designed lesson; a single case) and [choice-rich infrastructure](../claims/choice-rich-infrastructure-supports-productive-deviation-and-learning.md) [+M] (two sixth-graders extending one challenge into a year-long project; an extreme case chosen for its length). On guidance: [guided play](../claims/play-based-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [~M] did better than free play or direct instruction on some early-childhood outcomes and no differently on most; [unassisted discovery](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S] learned less than explicit instruction, while discovery with feedback, examples or scaffolding did better.
5. **Keep observing and redraw the web as the topic evolves**, treating the notes and products as ongoing assessment of what each learner is learning. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
6. **Close the thread** when interest has fallen in the notes for two or three sessions running, or the webbed ideas have been reached, or the product is finished: learners make, show or tell what they found (a model, a mural, a display, a short talk to another class or to families), the teacher records which webbed ideas and standards each learner showed, and a new entry point is offered. Evidence status: untested proposal.

**Dose and progression (proposals; no claim gives them).**

| | Early (first threads, or learners new to the approach) | Later |
|---|---|---|
| Who supplies entry points | The teacher, from materials and events chosen to open several questions | Learners propose topics; the teacher adds materials and checks the web against required content |
| Webbing | Teacher-led, 10–15 minutes, the adults adding most branches | Learners draw most of the web; 15–20 minutes, with the teacher marking the target ideas |
| Daily block | 30–45 minutes of thread work for young children, inside an otherwise ordinary day | 45–60 minutes, or longer project blocks for older learners |
| Thread length | One to two weeks | Two to six weeks, as long as the notes show learners returning to it |
| Adult guidance | Frequent: model, ask, supply tools in each session | Lighter: check in, ask for explanations, bring in experts when learners hit a limit |
| When to move on | Interest falls for two or three sessions, the target ideas are reached, or the product is shared | The same, plus learners can say what they want to find out next |

For older students the earlier page's route is the default: two or three short sessions in which students bring personally relevant problems or experiences, discuss them with classmates and formulate their own research questions, followed by several weeks of formal, systematic study of one question with a stated method, and a seminar or presentation at the end (from the earlier page; untested here).

| Observation | Discriminating probe | Proposed action, with its uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Several learners ask about something unplanned during the entry activity | Put a related book or material out the next day without comment; see who returns to it and what they ask. | If several return and ask further questions, web it. If they do not, record it as a passing interest and continue the plan. Neither branch is tested. |
| The thread is lively but a few vocal learners are driving it; others drift | Look at the notes by learner: who has spoken, made or asked anything in the last few sessions? Ask a quieter learner privately what they would like to try. | Open a second branch of the web, or a parallel role (drawing, building, recording), so each learner has an entry point. Do not read whole-class enthusiasm as each learner's interest. |
| Activity continues, but it has narrowed to making, collecting or a single easy goal (money, decoration) and the webbed ideas are not appearing | Ask learners to explain why something happens or why they chose it; check the notes for any question about the target idea. | Add a conceptual frame: a question that requires the idea, a visitor who brings it, a comparison. [Community projects without conceptual framing](../claims/community-projects-need-conceptual-framing-to-avoid-narrowing-the-learning-object.md) [~W] (one school, one course) describes this narrowing and its partial repair through facilitated reflection. |
| Learners repeat the same play or activity and ask no new questions | Ask what they are trying to find out; offer one tool or model and see whether they use it. | If they lack the knowledge or moves to go further, guide directly (model, show, explain), then hand back. If they are content and the thread has served its purpose, close it. Proposed; the guidance evidence is from discovery and play studies, not emergent curricula. |
| Older students name a topic but no question they could investigate | Ask what they would need to find out, and how they would know. | Support question formulation before any research starts; if the question cannot be studied in the time, narrow it with them. Untested. |

## Fitting the design to a situation

**The three facts that most change the decision:**

- **How much the learners already know of the topic and of the moves the thread needs.** A thread that leaves learners to discover essential content unaided learns less than explicit teaching; guided forms do better. If the brief does not say, ask: given the likely topic, can these learners ask a question about it, find something out and explain it, or will an adult have to show them how?
- **How much required content must be covered, and who checks.** The approach gives up the guarantee that a set list is covered. If the brief does not say, ask: which outcomes must every learner show by the end of the term, and how much of the timetable is free?
- **How many adults are present and how much of their time is free.** Continuous observation and webbing are adult work. If the brief does not say, ask: who will watch, write the notes and review them, and for how long each day?

|---|---|---|
| Learners are `child` (preschool to early primary) | Use the full sequence: teacher-chosen entry points, daily observation notes, webbing with the children, threads of one to four weeks, and an adult in the activity guiding it toward the webbed ideas (asking, modelling, supplying tools) rather than leaving it as free play. | [Guided play](../claims/play-based-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [~M]: early-childhood meta-analysis, guided play ahead of direct instruction on early maths, shape knowledge and task switching and of free play on spatial vocabulary, no reliable difference on most outcomes; guided play, not emergent curriculum |
| Learners are `novice` on the content the thread leads into | Keep the topic theirs but teach the content inside it explicitly: short demonstrations, worked examples or a visitor's explanation at the point learners need them, with feedback on what they make. | [Unassisted discovery](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S]: 5 distinct studies in its table; carried from discovery and inquiry tasks to an interest-led thread |
| Learners are `adolescent` or `adult` in a standards-driven course | Use the research-question route: two or three sessions to turn personal interests into investigable questions, then weeks of formal study with a stated method and a seminar or presentation. Map each question to the required outcomes before it is approved. | Untested; the earlier page's high-school example |
| Required standards or a fixed syllabus must be covered | Draw the web with the standards beside it: mark which branch reaches which outcome, and steer between branches rather than abandoning the thread; cover what no branch reaches in short planned lessons. Collect learners' interests by more than one route (a short survey plus conversation), since question wording can change which learners rate a topic highly. | [Co-design tools](../claims/co-design-tools-support-balancing-standards-and-student-interests.md) [+M]: one teacher-researcher co-design team, self-report; teachers designing units, not learners' outcomes |
| The goal is a fixed skill (algebra expressions, decoding) and the topic cannot move | Keep the content fixed and let interests supply the contexts: write or choose problems and texts set in what learners said they care about. This is interest-based context, not emergent topic. | [Interest-personalized problems](../claims/personalization-improves-hard-kc-accuracy-algebra-its.md) [+M]: one randomised study, 145 ninth-grade Algebra I students in an intelligent tutor, better accuracy on hard expression-writing steps within one unit |
| Mixed modes: some learners join remotely while others are in the room, or learners are phone-only | Keep one shared record of questions and the web (a shared document or photo of the web board) that remote learners add to; give remote learners a branch they can pursue with what they have at home (photographs, interviews, local observation) and a slot to report back. | Untested proposal |
| One teacher for many learners, no assistant | Observe a different small group each day (four or five learners), so each learner is noted at least weekly; run one thread for the whole class with two or three branches rather than many threads; web with the class, not alone after hours. | Untested; the earlier page notes that webbing works best with more than one adult present |
| Scarce facilitator time (volunteers, a busy shift, one tutor checking in briefly) | Cut observation to one question per check-in ("what are you trying to find out?") written on a shared sheet; let learners keep their own question log; review the sheet once a week to choose the next branch. | Untested proposal |
| A single session only, or a fixed short time budget (for example 45 minutes) | Do not run an emergent curriculum; use the moves inside the plan: 5 minutes to collect questions about the entry point, 30 minutes on the activity with the class's chosen question as its focus, 10 minutes to share what was found and record questions for later. | Untested proposal |
| The brief names its own routine for observation, planning or assessment (its own record sheet, planning cycle, rubric or report categories) | Keep it, and record interest and webbing in its categories; change only what the evidence says matters: an adult guiding the thread toward named ideas, and a check that the required content is reached. Use the brief's own rubric to judge the closing product. | Untested; [unassisted discovery](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S] for the guidance change only |
| Learners with low confidence or literacy, or the language of instruction is not their first language | Accept interest shown in talk, drawing, building and pointing, not only in written questions; write learners' questions down for them; use visual and concrete materials and visitors; keep the web pictorial. | Untested proposal |
| Adults in a work setting | Let the thread start from a problem or incident from their own work; keep it to sessions that fit the shift; close it on something usable at work (a revised procedure, a job aid in their own terms). | Untested proposal |
| The thread ends in a product or performance for a real audience (families, another class, the public) | Agree the audience with the learners early; keep the product whole and in the learners' own form; rehearse once; the audience sees the product, not the teacher's notes. | Untested proposal; [choice-rich infrastructure](../claims/choice-rich-infrastructure-supports-productive-deviation-and-learning.md) [+M] describes two students whose games were shared publicly, one extreme case |
| Private or sensitive interests (family events, identity, health) | Let the learner decide whether the interest goes on the shared web; offer a private branch they pursue with the teacher, and keep notes about it out of displays. | Untested proposal |
| Learners or families hold expectations about what matters to learn that differ from the school's | Ask families what they would like the child to learn and add those topics as entry points; record where learners' interests and families' expectations diverge rather than resolving it for them. | Untested; from the earlier page's rationale |
| A low-resourced school under heavy test pressure | Ring-fence a small regular block (for example two 45-minute sessions a week) for the thread rather than abandoning it; use free materials and local visitors. | Untested; [the low-income-schools argument](../claims/low-income-schools-test-prep-crowds-out-interest-activities.md) [~W] is a theoretical commentary that such schools have the fewest resources for interest-driven activity, not a test of any design |
| Rewards, points or grades attached to taking part | Remove expected rewards for joining the thread; give informational feedback on what learners found and made. | [Autonomy support](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+S]: 8 distinct studies in its table, including a meta-analysis of 128 experiments in which expected tangible rewards undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks; carried to emergent threads untested |

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Departing from the plan to follow an unplanned event.** [Spontaneous responsiveness to real-world events in PBL can deepen student-directed inquiry beyond what designed curriculum achieves](../claims/spontaneous-authenticity-in-pbl-deepens-student-directed-inquiry.md) [+M]: one bilingual third-grade classroom of 20 students, studied through 192 hours of observation, interviews and artefacts (`qualitative`, `q2`, no effect size). When the teacher paused a written PBL unit on birds to respond to a spring snowstorm, students generated their own solutions, led the discussion, consulted an outside source on their own initiative and showed concern for the birds; in a designed lesson on the same topic engagement stayed within the script. The authors attribute the move to the teacher's expertise and autonomy, and the first author co-wrote the curriculum. This is the closest the wiki comes to the pattern's own relationship, and it is one case: it shows what a flexible curriculum and a willing, experienced teacher made possible once, not how often.
- **Extending beyond a designed task.** [In one extreme case, two sixth-graders with a choice-rich, ungraded STEAM infrastructure extended a game-design challenge into a year-long self-directed project well beyond its designed scope](../claims/choice-rich-infrastructure-supports-productive-deviation-and-learning.md) [+M]: two sixth-grade students in an ungraded in-school studio spent about 22.5 hours across 34 sessions extending one game-design challenge into two original games, resolving 23 technical problems, with problems becoming less frequent and quicker to resolve after midyear. An extreme case selected for its length; most students in the same studios did not do this, and there is no comparison group.
- **Guidance inside child-led activity.** [Guided play outperforms direct instruction or free play on a few early-childhood outcomes, with no reliable difference on most others, in one meta-analysis](../claims/play-based-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [~M]: a meta-analysis of 39 early-childhood intervention studies (17 pooled, N=3893) found guided play ahead of direct instruction on early maths (g=0.24), shape knowledge (g=0.63) and task switching (g=0.40), and ahead of free play on spatial vocabulary (g=0.93, with high heterogeneity), with no reliable differences on most other outcomes. It tests adult guidance of play, not interest-chosen topics; it is the strongest reason for step 4's adult in the activity.
- **The limit: unguided discovery.** [Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S]: its table gathers 5 distinct studies; in one meta-analysis explicit instruction outperformed unassisted discovery across 580 comparisons (d = 0.38 favouring explicit instruction), and discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations outperformed other instruction across 360 comparisons; with 112 third- and fourth-graders, many more learned the control-of-variables procedure from direct instruction. An emergent thread that leaves the content to be found unaided is in the condition these studies say fails.
- **The limit: a thread that narrows.** [Community projects need conceptual framing to avoid narrowing the learning object](../claims/community-projects-need-conceptual-framing-to-avoid-narrowing-the-learning-object.md) [~W]: one Finnish middle school's entrepreneurship course with outside partners, 11 students in the focal year (`q1`): without a disciplinary frame the activity converged on "money-making" and left its intended critical questions unexamined, until a facilitated cross-group discussion raised another framing. A caution for any open thread, from one site.
- **Balancing interests and standards.** [Co-design tools for standards analysis, student-interest data, and launch rehearsal support balancing standards alignment with student interests](../claims/co-design-tools-support-balancing-standards-and-student-interests.md) [+M]: 27 participants (19 teachers) in a two-year co-design partnership credited unpacking standards, writing target explanations, disaggregated interest surveys and rehearsing launches "in student hat"; the wording of one survey item changed which demographic groups rated a phenomenon highly, and the team concluded that surveys should be supplemented with focus groups or interviews. Self-report about designing units; no learner outcome.
- **Interest as context rather than topic.** [Personalization to student interests improved accuracy on hard knowledge components involving algebraic expression-writing within the tutoring unit](../claims/personalization-improves-hard-kc-accuracy-algebra-its.md) [+M]: a randomised experiment with 145 ninth-grade Algebra I students in an intelligent tutor; students given problems set in their stated interests were more accurate on the hard expression-writing steps within one unit (odds = 1.53). The only causal test in the wiki of adapting instruction to learners' interests, and it keeps the content fixed; it supports interest-based contexts, not emergent topics.
- **Why interest should help.** [Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals](../claims/task-value-increases-motivation-and-engagement.md) [+M]: its table gathers 4 distinct studies, three reviews and one associational, none with an effect size coded; [Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning](../claims/autonomy-supports-intrinsic-motivation.md) [+M]: 8 distinct studies, mainly reviews and theory, with one meta-analysis on rewards. Both are general mechanisms, not tests of letting learners choose the topic.

Do not rank emergent curriculum against a planned curriculum from these claims: none compares them, none measures coverage of required content, and none follows learners past the thread. Do not read a lively class, or a few learners' sustained projects, as the class having learned the webbed ideas. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (an interest-led thread with adult guidance against a planned unit on the same content, on engagement and on the target ideas, at the end of the thread and later) and the local observations that would stand in for it: the dated notes by learner and each learner's explanation of the target ideas at the close.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested lesson.* A kindergarten class of 22 with a teacher and an assistant has a planned autumn unit on leaves. During a walk, several children stop at puddles and ask why some leaves float and others sink; the assistant's notes show five children still talking about it the next morning and three bringing objects from home. The adults web it in a 15-minute after-school meeting: floating and sinking, materials, measuring, counting and recording results, and two early science outcomes and one numeracy outcome the school requires. With the children they draw a picture web the next day ("what floats? what sinks? can we make a sinker float?"). For two weeks, 40 minutes a day, the children test objects in water tubs; an adult sits at the tubs each session, asks children to predict before they drop something, shows them how to sort results on a two-column chart, and introduces the word "material". A parent who builds boats visits in the second week. Interest falls in the notes on days nine and ten; the thread closes with the children showing families their chart and a foil boat that carries coins. One child's family prefers that the class keep to the leaves unit; the teacher records this and finishes the unit's required leaf-sorting activity in two short sessions. The guided-play and guidance claims cover adults guiding young children's activity, not this choice of topic; whether the children learned more than the leaves unit would have taught is not something this design can show.

Record: **entry point → dated notes of questions and returns, by child → the decision to web (who, how many, how many days) → the web, with target ideas and required outcomes marked → materials, visitors and guidance given, by session → each child's predictions, sorting and explanations → candidate interpretations of any child not taking part (no interest, no entry point, cannot yet do the moves) and their basis → the closing product and audience → required outcomes reached, by child → what children said they want to find out next**. Preserve the children's own questions in their words and any change in them. An agent may summarise this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of learning without a calibrated model.

## Elements and limits

[Observation](../elements/observation.md), [learner choice](../elements/learner-choice.md), [question formulation](../elements/question-formulation.md), [public display](../elements/public-display.md), and the strategies [guided play](../strategies/guided-play.md), [free play](../strategies/free-play.md), [interest inventories](../strategies/interest_inventories.md) and [co-design tools for standards and interest balancing](../strategies/co-design-tools-for-standards-and-interest-balancing.md).

Limits: no claim in this wiki compares an emergent curriculum with a planned one, measures coverage of required content under it, or follows learners beyond a thread; the two cases nearest the pattern are single qualitative studies, one chosen as an extreme case; the guidance evidence comes from play and discovery studies; and the only causal test of adapting to interests keeps the content fixed. The record above is how a setting would gather its own evidence.

## Description
Instructional planning is normally described as if goals and objectives are chosen by educators rather than students — but this places a real burden on everyone involved: curriculum writers must be confident their specified goals genuinely matter, teachers must motivate students toward goals the students didn't choose, and students must master objectives regardless of personal interest. Critics argue this can be a serious impediment to learning (Kohn, 2004), especially for the youngest students, who may have little patience for an agenda set entirely by others (Kohn, 1999; Seitz, 2006), and in culturally diverse classrooms, where students and families may hold legitimate but unconventional expectations about what matters to learn (Banks & Banks, 2005).

**Emergent curriculum** responds by building instruction explicitly on interests students themselves express, rather than on goals set by curriculum documents or teachers in advance. Because it unfolds spontaneously, planning for it doesn't mean specifying objectives ahead of time — student interests are somewhat predictable in kind but not usually foreseeable far in advance (Peterson, 2002). A canonical example: a first-grade teacher plans a Halloween unit and reads a Halloween book aloud, but students turn out far more interested in one illustration — a full moon partly hidden by clouds — and begin asking why the moon is sometimes full, why it rises in different places each month, and whether the moon or the clouds are the ones actually moving. Rather than redirecting back to the planned unit, the teacher sets the Halloween plans aside, finds books on the moon and solar system, invites a local amateur astronomer to visit, and lets the class build paper-mâché moon models and a moonscape mural — the original goals aren't rejected so much as set aside in favor of something the children found more immediately compelling.

The defining feature is not that these activities *could* happen (a curriculum document could specify moon astronomy too) but *why* they happen: because the children wanted them, not because a predetermined plan called for them. This reframes the teacher's core challenge from planning activities that match preset goals to responding flexibly and sensitively as student interest becomes visible. Two practices support this responsiveness: **continuous observation** — watching, listening, and often keeping informal written records of student comments and activities, which doubles as an ongoing, authentic assessment of what students are actually learning; and **curriculum webbing** — brainstorming connections among student-suggested and teacher-suggested initiatives, done jointly with students, independently by the teacher, or jointly with co-teachers/assistants (a structure that works especially well in preschool, kindergarten, or special-education classrooms with more than one adult present; Vartuli & Rohs, 2006).

Emergent curriculum can seem, to skeptics, like a formula for curricular and management chaos, but it has proven successful particularly in early childhood and the earliest elementary grades (Seitz, 2006; Wurm, 2005). A structurally similar approach is possible even with older students: a documented high school program had students begin with personally relevant problems and experiences, discuss them with classmates, and formulate their own research questions to study more formally and systematically (Hawkins, 2006; see [Socratic Seminar](../elements/socratic-seminar.md)) — an emergent curriculum in substance, since what students studied emerged from their own expressed interest rather than being predetermined.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Continuous, attentive observation of students' expressed interests, informal enough to happen alongside ordinary classroom activity rather than as a separate assessment event
- Genuine willingness to set aside a planned unit when student interest points somewhere else, rather than redirecting students back to the original plan
- Curriculum webbing or an equivalent process for connecting emerging student interest to genuine learning content and activities
#### Constraints
- Because goals are not specified in advance, the approach offers none of the accountability guarantees of a predetermined curriculum (e.g. ensuring specific standards are covered) — a real tradeoff, not a flaw to be engineered away
- Best-documented success is concentrated in early childhood and early elementary settings; applying the same degree of open-endedness with older students in standards-driven contexts requires more structure (as in the Hawkins 2006 example, where student-generated interests were still channeled into formal, systematic study)
#### Grain Size
[lesson] (though a single emergent thread, as in the moon example, can extend across days or weeks)

### Target Goals
- Motivation and depth of engagement that a predetermined curriculum's topic selection cannot reliably produce, since the topic is by construction one students already care about

### Target Learners
- Young children (early childhood through early elementary), where the approach has its strongest track record
- Older students in contexts flexible enough to formalize student-generated interests into structured inquiry (e.g., research projects, seminar discussion)

## Design

### Sequence
1. Begin with a planned activity or topic as an entry point, without treating it as fixed.
2. Continuously observe student engagement, interest, and questions during the activity, keeping informal notes where useful.
3. When genuine, sustained student interest emerges (even in an unplanned direction), use curriculum webbing to connect that interest to viable learning content and activities.
4. Set aside the original plan as needed and follow the emergent thread with new materials, experts, or projects.
5. Continue observing and webbing as the topic evolves, treating the process itself as ongoing assessment of student learning.

### Affordances

### Personalization
- The pattern is inherently responsive to whichever specific interests a given group of students expresses, rather than needing separate personalization for individual students
