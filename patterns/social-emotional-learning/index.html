    Social Emotional Learning¶

    Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 16 claims (10 for, 6 mixed) · 29 studies (12 causal, 11 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 1 design, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 12 of 29 report an effect size · 6 claims rest on one study

    Description and scope¶

    A reusable configuration for teaching named social and emotional skills (CASEL's five competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills and responsible decision-making) as part of school or programme life: a predictable, relationally safe climate → explicit teaching of one named skill at a time → the adult models it aloud → learners rehearse it in low-stakes structured practice with feedback → they use it inside real academic and social work, coached in the moment → they reflect on what they did → the skill is cued across settings until it is used unprompted. The intended change is in the skill itself (observed or rated social and emotional competence, a skill-performance and attitude-motivation target), and, more weakly, in conduct, emotional distress and academic achievement (general-achievement), at horizons from the end of a programme to years later.

    What the evidence tests, and what it does not. Unlike most patterns in this wiki, this one is tested as a whole, at programme grain: meta-analyses of universal, school-based SEL programmes for kindergarten to grade 12 compare participating students with controls (SEL programs improve social-emotional skills [+S], benefits persist at follow-up [+S], and achievement, by a smaller and contested amount [~S]). Those programmes differ in content, and the syntheses report that programmes using four practices (sequenced, active, focused, explicit: "SAFE") and those without implementation problems did better. No claim tests this page's seven steps, their order, or any one step against its absence; no claim tests SEL with adults; and none compares integrating the skills into academic lessons with teaching them as stand-alone lessons. So the sequence below is the earlier page's, kept as the default, each step labelled with its evidence status, and the response-dependent branches are untested proposals.

    How the converted neighbours sit beside this page. Self-Regulated Learning (and its pattern) owns planning, monitoring and acting on a learning task; this page's self-management strand overlaps it but targets emotion and conduct as well as study. Building Empathy owns the perspective-taking model behind the social-awareness strand, including its limits on dose and horizon; this page does not repeat it. Engagement owns the claim that engagement is a mediator, not an outcome, which applies here too: a calmer, more engaged class is a route to learning only if the work it engages in is aligned. Check-ins owns the recurring self-report routine used in step 1, and Autonomy the controlling side of rewards and praise. This page owns the programme-level configuration that combines them, and the evidence about SEL programmes as such.

    Inputs: establish the design brief¶

    Field Record or elicit
    Learner and starting observation Age or stage (child, adolescent, adult; the evidence is pre-k-12, with one synthesis of ages 2–6). Before teaching, observe the target skill in an ordinary situation: what a learner does when frustrated by a task, when a peer disagrees, when asked to join a group. Record the situation and who observed it (teacher, the learner, a peer, a parent), since ratings differ by informant. Note adversity, trauma or chronic stress where known, language and literacy, and neurodivergence, without reading an atypical emotional expression as a deficit.
    The skill Which competency and which named skill within it ("identify the emotion, then choose a strategy"; asking for help; disagreeing without insult). One skill per unit of teaching. Whether learners already have the words for it in the language of instruction.
    Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-instructor-led, online-self-paced, workplace-clinical), mode (all in the room, some remote, phone-only), days-weeks or term-plus, minutes a week available, group size and number of adults, who will deliver it (the learners' own teacher, other school staff or an outside provider), and whether the school's discipline and reward practice agrees or conflicts with what is taught.
    Learner-valued goal Ask what the learner wants from it: fewer fights with a particular friend, to stop panicking in tests, to be picked for a team, to handle patients or customers. Adolescents and adults especially may not value "SEL" as named. Record disagreement with the designer objective.
    Designer objective Which outcome is the target: the skill itself, conduct (referrals, suspensions, disruption), emotional distress, belonging, or academic achievement. Name the one the evaluation will weigh: the evidence says they move by very different amounts.
    Outcome An observed or performance measure of the skill in a new situation, beside any self-report; conduct and achievement measured separately; immediate and delayed (a term or a year later). Say whether the measure was written by the programme (aligned and closer to the intervention) or is independent.

    A request to "add SEL" or "build students' resilience" does not specify these. Ask which skill, for which learners, delivered by whom, how many minutes a week for how long, and measured how, before choosing a curriculum, a lesson order or a routine.

    Sequence and conditional policy¶

    The default sequence is the earlier page's. The SAFE practices reported as moderators in the programme syntheses bear on it as a whole (steps 2 and 4 make it explicit and active; one skill at a time makes it focused; the order makes it sequenced); no step is tested on its own. Durations and progressions are untested proposals unless a claim is cited.

    Dose across a programme (untested proposal). One explicit lesson of 20–30 minutes a week for each named skill, for at least a school term per competency, with 2–5 minutes of daily cueing and use inside ordinary lessons; plan for a full school year, and repeat with new skills in following years. In the first two or three weeks of a skill, the adult supplies the scenarios, scripts and sentence starters and models every step; in the middle weeks, learners rehearse with scenarios drawn from their own class life and the adult coaches; in the last weeks, learners choose the situations, prompts are withdrawn one at a time, and the adult only cues. A learner moves on from a skill when they use it unprompted in two different settings (for example in group work and at break) on two separate occasions, observed by an adult or reported by a peer; a learner who still needs the prompt keeps the script while the class moves on.

    1. Climate-setting: predictable routines and relational safety. Greet learners individually; run a short, regular check-in; co-author a few class norms. Proposal: daily, 2–5 minutes, from the first week and throughout. Evidence status: positive greetings at the door [+M] raised academic engaged time and reduced disruption in a randomized evaluation with middle-school teachers against an attention control; it is a climate step tested on engagement, not on social and emotional skill. The check-in is untested here (see the Check-ins principle).
    2. Explicit skill instruction. Name and teach one skill in steps ("notice the feeling, name it, choose a strategy, try it") through direct instruction and demonstration. Proposal: 20–30 minutes once a week. Evidence status: carried from the programme syntheses' SAFE moderator (SEL skills [+S]); for the naming step, emotion labelling [~M] reduced physiological fear a week later, but not self-reported fear, in spider-fearful adults under exposure, a clinical laboratory study, not a classroom one.
    3. Modelling. The adult narrates their own regulation and social decisions aloud during real moments ("I'm getting frustrated with this projector, so I'm going to take a breath and try the other cable"). Proposal: whenever a genuine moment arises, at least once a lesson in the first weeks. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
    4. Guided practice with feedback. Learners rehearse the skill in low-stakes structured activities: role-play, class discussion of scenarios, and structured cooperative learning. Give feedback on what the learner did and how (the task and the strategy), not praise of the person. Proposal: 10–15 minutes of the weekly lesson, scenarios supplied early and drawn from learners' own situations later. Evidence status: the "active" SAFE practice in the syntheses (SEL skills [+S]); for group practice, group rewards resting on each member's learning [+M] raised achievement in school cooperative methods, carried here to social practice it did not test; for the feedback, task- and process-level feedback [+M], carried from feedback on learning tasks to feedback on social behaviour.
    5. Embedded application. Learners use the skill inside real academic work (peer feedback, collaborative problem-solving, a group project), with the adult coaching in the moment. Proposal: in at least two ordinary lessons a week, the skill named in the lesson plan. Evidence status: untested as a step. In the syntheses, programmes taught by learners' own classroom teachers showed significant skill and academic effects where programmes run by non-school personnel did not (SEL and achievement [~S]); that is a delivery finding, not a test of embedding.
    6. Reflection. Learners name what they did and what they would do differently, through short structured reflection prompts or journals, privately as well as aloud. Proposal: 3–5 minutes at the end of the weekly lesson and after any coached incident. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
    7. Reinforcement across settings, then fading. Cue and acknowledge the skill in corridors, group work, assessment situations and at home, and remove prompts one at a time once a learner uses the skill unprompted (the moving-on rule above). Evidence status: untested as a step; the follow-up synthesis [+S] shows programme gains outlasting the programme (6 months to 18 years after) but does not say which programmes reinforced across settings.
    Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
    Learner names the skill and its steps in the lesson, but does not use it when upset in class In a calm moment, ask them to walk through a recent incident; separately, watch whether they use it with a prompt card in the next incident. If the prompt card works, the skill is known but not yet cued: keep the card and add adult cueing (step 7). If it does not, the moment may be too intense for a newly learned skill: teach a shorter first step (breathing, asking to step out) before the full routine. Neither branch is tested.
    Learners comply in role-play but call it "babyish" or perform it ironically Ask privately what situations they actually find hard; offer a choice of scenarios. If they engage with their own scenarios, switch to learner-chosen situations and frame the skill around fairness, friendship or future goals. Carried from the autonomy claim [~M] (intrinsic motivation, not SEL); untested here.
    Self-reported skill rises, observed conduct unchanged Compare the self-report with a teacher's or peer's rating and with an observed behaviour count over the same weeks. If only the self-report moved, it may reflect knowing the right answer rather than doing it: weight the observed measure and lengthen practice in real situations. Informants disagreeing is expected (early-childhood synthesis [~S]: effects varied by informant and assessment type).
    A learner withdraws or becomes distressed during sharing or emotion talk Ask privately whether sharing in the group feels safe; check what is known about adversity or trauma. Offer a private channel (written check-in, one-to-one) and focus on regulation and predictability before social sharing. Untested proposal from the earlier page.
    Skills improve in the SEL lessons, achievement flat Check whether the skills were used inside academic lessons and whether the academic work itself is aligned and demanding. Do not expect large achievement gains from SEL alone: in rigorous recent trials the average academic effect was negligible (SEL and achievement [~S]). If skills were not used in lessons, embed them (step 5); if they were, look at the academic instruction, not the SEL.
    Behaviour incidents cluster around particular adults or routines Count incidents and exclusions by setting and by adult over a few weeks. If concentrated, address the adults' responses, not only the learners' skills: a brief online exercise on an empathic rather than punitive mindset for teachers is a candidate (empathic discipline [+M]).

    Fitting the design to a situation¶

    The three facts that most change the decision:

    • Who will deliver it, and how often. The syntheses found skill and academic effects for programmes run by learners' own teachers and not, or not significantly, for programmes run by outside personnel or as multicomponent programmes, and implementation problems lowered effects. If unstated, ask: will the learners' own teacher teach and cue this every week, and for how long?
    • Which outcome the brief is buying. Social and emotional skills moved most; conduct, distress and attitudes less; academic achievement least, and in rigorous recent trials by a negligible amount. If unstated, ask: what result would count as success, and how will it be measured?
    • Age or stage, and whether the learners chose to be there. The evidence is from school children and adolescents (and one synthesis of ages 2–6); nothing here tests adults. If unstated, ask: how old are the learners, and will they see this as theirs or as something done to them?
    If the brief says… Then change… Basis
    Learners are aged 2–6, in an early-childhood centre Teach through play, picture emotion cards and puppets; lessons of 10–15 minutes; adults name emotions as they happen; measure social and emotional competence by observation and more than one informant. Early-childhood SEL synthesis [+S]: 63 studies of universal curricula in centres; the play-based format and lesson length are untested proposals
    Learners are adolescent Frame skills around fairness, identity, friendship and real stakes; let learners choose scenarios and lead discussion; avoid scripts that feel young; offer mindfulness or breathing practice as one strategy among several. Autonomy support [~M], carried from intrinsic motivation to SEL; mindfulness programmes [+W] and self-regulation interventions with high schoolers and young adults [+W], one practice brief's review, no effect sizes
    adult learners in work settings (health care, teaching, management, customer service) Present the skills as professional competence; use cases and structured reflective discussion of real encounters (Balint-style groups) instead of scripted lessons; assess on observed practice. Untested: no claim here tests SEL with adults; the examples are the earlier page's
    The goal is academic achievement Keep SEL, but embed the skills in academic lessons, delivered by the subject teacher, and promise only a small achievement effect; measure achievement on an independent test, not the programme's own. SEL and achievement [~S]: small at post-test in the older synthesis (significant only with school-staff delivery), negligible in randomised trials of a recent one, larger for GPA
    An outside provider or a pull-out session will deliver it Train and involve the class teacher, who cues the skill daily; or, failing that, expect smaller effects and measure them. SEL and achievement [~S] and SEL skills [~S]: delivery by non-school personnel and multicomponent programmes showed weaker or non-significant effects
    A single session, assembly or workshop only Do not promise durable change. Teach one skill and leave a routine (a check-in card, a breathing step) the teacher will run daily; or spend the session on the adults' discipline mindset instead. Empathy-building claim [~M]: most prejudice-reduction studies test light-touch interventions with unclear long-term effects; empathic discipline [+M]: a brief teacher exercise reduced suspensions for high-risk groups, with a marginal overall effect in the preregistered replication
    Exclusions, suspensions or disruption are the presenting problem Add a short empathic-discipline exercise for teachers and a daily greeting routine before or alongside the student programme; track exclusions by group. Empathic discipline [+M] (middle-school mathematics teachers); positive greetings [+M] (middle school, engagement and disruption)
    Many learners live with adversity, poverty or chronic stress Start with regulation and predictability before social sharing; keep emotional disclosure private and optional; plan for multiple years. Follow-up evidence [+S]: a preschool SEL and language programme for low-income children showed young-adult advantages 17 years later and buffered adverse childhood experiences; the privacy rule is untested
    Learners use a language other than the language of instruction, or have low literacy Use visual emotion scales and choice cards; teach the emotion words directly; allow pointing or drawing in check-ins; extend practice cycles. Emotion labelling [~M], carried from fearful adults in a clinical laboratory; the visual supports are untested
    The programme was designed in another country or culture Adapt scenarios, emotion vocabulary and norms for expression with families and learners; keep the explicit, active, sequenced structure. Follow-up synthesis [~S]: benefits similar across race, socioeconomic status and school location (abstract); adaptation itself is untested
    One teacher with a large class (30+) and little time Run the skill lesson whole-class with paired rehearsal and cue cards; use a 2-minute written check-in instead of a sharing circle; coach a few learners each day in rotation. Untested proposal
    Mixed modes: some learners remote, some in the room, or phone-only Run check-ins as a private poll or chat message so remote learners are seen equally; pair each remote learner with one in the room for rehearsal (breakout or phone call); send the week's skill as a short text with one practice task. Untested proposal
    online-self-paced, no adult present Use short video models and private reflection prompts; ask learners to log one real use of the skill a week; add a human contact (tutor message, peer partner) where possible, since coaching and modelling in the moment cannot happen. Untested proposal
    Learners will run something for a real audience (peer mediation, a family evening, mentoring younger pupils) Rehearse the exact interaction first with role-play and feedback; give each learner an individual role they are accountable for; debrief privately after. Group rewards on individual learning [~M], carried from achievement in cooperative methods; the audience format is untested
    The evaluation relies on student self-report Add an observed or teacher-rated measure and a conduct record; report each separately. Early-childhood synthesis [~S]: effects varied by informant and assessment type; emotion labelling [~M]: the physiological measure moved when self-report did not

    Choosing configurations from evidence¶

    • The programme as a whole, on the skills. SEL programs improve social-emotional skills [+S]: across 213 universal school-based programmes for K–12 students, social and emotional skills improved most at post-test (mean ES = 0.57 over 68 interventions, against 0.22–0.27 for attitudes, positive social behaviour, conduct problems, emotional distress and academic performance); skill effects were significant for classroom programmes run by teachers (0.62) and not for multicomponent programmes; the SAFE practices and implementation problems moderated outcomes; heterogeneity was high (I² = 91%). In centre-based early childhood (ages 2–6, 63 studies), universal curricula improved social competence (d = 0.30) and emotional competence (d = 0.54), and early learning skills by less (d = 0.18). Both are syntheses against controls, mostly at post-test; skill measures include programme-aligned ones.
    • Behaviour and achievement. Universal school-based SEL programs improve students' behaviour, with a small and, in recent randomised trials, negligible effect on academic achievement [+S] and SEL Programs Improve Academic Achievement [~S] carry the same three syntheses. The older one reports academic performance ES 0.27 (35 interventions; the authors translate it as 11 percentile points), significant only when school staff delivered the programme (teacher delivery 0.34; non-school personnel 0.12, not significant). A contemporary synthesis of 424 studies reports improved behaviours, school functioning and achievement (abstract only, no effect size read). A synthesis restricted to randomised or rigorous quasi-experimental studies with elementary and middle-school students found overall achievement g = 0.08, g = 0.063 in randomised trials, larger for GPA (g = 0.33). This is the main limit on the pattern: plan SEL for its social and emotional outcomes, and treat an achievement gain as possible and small.
    • Durability. Social-emotional learning benefits persist at follow-up [+S]: a synthesis of 82 universal school-based programmes found participants ahead of controls on skills, attitudes and well-being 6 months to 18 years later, similar across race, socioeconomic status and school location (abstract only, no pooled effect read); a randomized trial of an enriched Head Start curriculum with 356 low-income preschoolers found advantages on 7 of 11 well-being measures at age 21–22 (d = .21 to .33). The older synthesis's follow-up studies still showed academic effects (ES 0.32, k = 8).
    • The adults' side. Brief intervention empathic discipline cuts suspensions [+M]: a brief online exercise for middle-school mathematics teachers halved year-long suspensions in one randomized field experiment (9.6% to 4.8%); a preregistered, active-placebo replication found a small, marginal overall effect and narrowed the Black/Hispanic disparity from 10.6 to 5.9 percentage points. It targets teachers' responses to students, not students' skills, and its outcome is an administrative record.
    • One step's mechanism. Emotion Labeling Improves Regulation [~M]: 88 spider-fearful adults randomly assigned to affect labelling, reappraisal, distraction or exposure alone; labelling gave a larger drop in skin conductance a week later (d = 0.64–0.85) and no difference in self-reported fear. It supports naming emotions as one part of a routine, not as a classroom effect.

    Do not rank SEL against academic instruction from these claims: none compares the two for time spent, and the achievement effects are small and contested. Do not read rising self-report, or a calm SEL lesson, as the skill attained. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (this sequence, or one step of it, against the same programme without it, on an observed skill measure a term later) and the local observation that would stand in for it.

    Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

    Claims this page cited before the 2026-10-05 rewrite, or that bear on it, which are not core evidence above. Each marker is capped by the claim's recorded evidence.

    • Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M] — a review, a meta-analysis of extrinsic rewards (tangible expected rewards undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks) and a conceptual framework; none is about SEL. Used above only for framing with adolescents, by extrapolation. The earlier page said SEL programmes "emphasizing choice and voice outperform compliance-oriented versions"; no claim here tests that.
    • Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [~M] — laboratory experiments, a classroom diversity programme for grades 4–6 and a review that flags publication bias and light-touch interventions. Bears on the social-awareness strand; its model is on the Building Empathy principle.
    • Reading fiction gives at most a very small gain on theory-of-mind and social-cognition tasks, and the single-passage literary-fiction effect did not replicate [~W] — the earlier page cited it [+W] as "Fiction reading improves empathy". The claim has since been narrowed: the average gain is on social-cognition tasks, below a small effect, and no recorded study measures empathy. Do not use fiction reading as an SEL component on this evidence.
    • Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+M] — two meta-analyses of feedback on learning and performance, supporting the task/process distinction indirectly. Used above for feedback in rehearsal, carried from academic tasks to social behaviour.
    • Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves outcomes. [+M] — school cooperative methods and achievement. Used above for structuring group practice, by extrapolation; it measures no social or emotional outcome.
    • Co-regulation strategies were reported to create a safe, positive classroom climate and to help youth manage emotions [+W] — facilitators' and youths' accounts from one national programme evaluation's focus groups; bears on the climate and modelling steps, as reported experience, not a comparison.
    • In Guglielmo and Tryon (2001), social skills training plus reinforcement increased sharing behaviors significantly more than reinforcement alone, while effects on being in a group were positive but not significant [~W] — attached 2026-10-07 from Social Skills Training (2013), which proposed "Behavioral social skills lesson pattern: instruct, model, practice, reinforce".
    • Social and emotional competence can be enhanced through explicit instruction, teaching practices, and curriculum integration [+W] — attached 2026-10-09 from Collaborative for Academic et al. (2020), which proposed "SAFE: four elements of high-quality SEL instruction".

    Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

    Invented illustration, not a tested design. A Year 7 form tutor (adolescent learners, 28 in the class, classroom, one 25-minute tutor period a week plus 10 minutes each morning) is asked to "do SEL" after a term of arguments in group work. The table changed the default: she chooses one skill, disagreeing without insult, and frames it around fairness rather than "managing feelings"; the morning slot carries a greeting and a written one-word check-in; the weekly period carries the explicit lesson and paired role-play, with scenarios she writes for the first three weeks and pupils write after that; the science and English teachers name the skill in one group task each a week and note who uses it unprompted. One pupil says he wants to stop being sent out of maths; the tutor's objective is fewer group-work conflicts. They agree to track both. After six weeks she compares her incident log, the subject teachers' notes and the pupils' own ratings, and plans to look again at the end of the year. Nothing in the claims tests this configuration with this age group at this dose; these are local choices to observe.

    Record: skill and competency → learners, age and starting observation, with informant → who delivers, minutes a week and for how long → scenarios used and who chose them → each learner's observed use of the skill, prompted or unprompted, by setting and date → feedback and coaching given → self-report beside observed or rated measures → conduct and achievement records, kept separate → candidate interpretations (skill not known, known but not cued, situation too intense, framing rejected, adults' responses driving incidents) and their basis → reobservation at the stated horizon. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs, diagnose a learner, or assign numerical probabilities of social or emotional competence without a calibrated measure.

    Elements and limits¶

    Check-ins, coaching, class discussion, reflection, belonging and role-play; the strategies SEL curricula, SEL routines, SEL integration and the 'What's my emotion?' check-in. Principles the pattern draws on: Collaborative Learning, Check-ins, Building Empathy, Clear Structure, Assessment for Learning, Self-Regulated Learning, Engagement and Autonomy. Learner variables: belonging and self-regulation.

    This pattern is scoped to sustained, explicit teaching and reinforcement of named social and emotional skills, mostly in schools. A one-off workshop, a whole-school behaviour policy, clinical treatment of anxiety or trauma, and professional formation for adults need other configurations or additional evidence. The policy supports observation and design reasoning; which of its steps matter, at what weekly dose, for adults, and against what achievement cost in teaching time, remains to be tested.

    Related Patterns¶

    • Cooperative Learning — depends on the relationship skills SEL develops; the two patterns are mutually reinforcing when group work carries individual accountability
    • Case-Based Learning — cases with interpersonal and ethical dimensions provide authentic contexts for practicing decision-making and perspective-taking
    • Formative Assessment — its feedback culture overlaps with SEL's self-management goal; both depend on students interpreting feedback as information, not judgment
    • Self-Regulated Learning — owns planning, monitoring and acting on a learning task; SEL's self-management strand overlaps it and extends to emotion and conduct
    • Direct Instruction — the format of the explicit skill lesson in step 2

    Examples¶

    PATHS (Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies): A widely evaluated K–6 curriculum teaching emotion labeling, self-control, and problem-solving through scripted lessons plus "Feelings Fair" classroom integration; central to the Durlak et al. evidence base.

    Second Step: A K–8 SEL curriculum (Committee for Children) using weekly lessons, puppet-based modeling in early grades, and peer-narrated video scenarios in middle school, with skills reinforced through teacher cueing during academic work.

    RULER approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence): Teaches Recognizing, Understanding, Labeling, Expressing, and Regulating emotion through tools like the Mood Meter; implemented school-wide so adults and students use a shared emotional vocabulary.

    Medical education — reflective practice seminars: Residency programs use structured case discussions and Balint-style groups to develop self-awareness, empathy, and relationship skills with patients — SEL competencies reframed as professional formation.
