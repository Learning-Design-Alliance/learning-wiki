Social Emotional Learning¶

Pattern · All patterns
Evidence · 41 claims (30 for, 11 mixed) · 56 studies (16 quant-synthesis, 16 review, 15 causal, 4 design, 3 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 theoretical), q1–q4 · 18 of 56 report an effect size · 27 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable configuration for teaching named social and emotional skills (CASEL's five competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills and responsible decision-making) as part of school or programme life: a predictable, relationally safe climate → explicit teaching of one named skill at a time → the adult models it aloud → learners rehearse it in low-stakes structured practice with feedback → they use it inside real academic and social work, coached in the moment → they reflect on what they did → the skill is cued across settings until it is used unprompted. The intended change is in the skill itself (observed or rated social and emotional competence, a skill-performance and attitude-motivation target), and, more weakly, in conduct, emotional distress and academic achievement (general-achievement), at horizons from the end of a programme to years later.

What the evidence tests, and what it does not. Unlike most patterns in this wiki, this one is tested as a whole, at programme grain: meta-analyses of universal, school-based SEL programmes for kindergarten to grade 12 compare participating students with controls (SEL programs improve social-emotional skills [+S], benefits persist at follow-up [+S], and achievement, by a smaller and contested amount [~S]). Those programmes differ in content, and the syntheses report that programmes using four practices (sequenced, active, focused, explicit: "SAFE") and those without implementation problems did better. No claim tests this page's seven steps, their order, or any one step against its absence; no claim tests SEL with adults; and none compares integrating the skills into academic lessons with teaching them as stand-alone lessons. So the sequence below is the earlier page's, kept as the default, each step labelled with its evidence status, and the response-dependent branches are untested proposals.

How the converted neighbours sit beside this page. Self-Regulated Learning (and its pattern) owns planning, monitoring and acting on a learning task; this page's self-management strand overlaps it but targets emotion and conduct as well as study. Building Empathy owns the perspective-taking model behind the social-awareness strand, including its limits on dose and horizon; this page does not repeat it. Engagement owns the claim that engagement is a mediator, not an outcome, which applies here too: a calmer, more engaged class is a route to learning only if the work it engages in is aligned. Check-ins owns the recurring self-report routine used in step 1, and Autonomy the controlling side of rewards and praise. This page owns the programme-level configuration that combines them, and the evidence about SEL programmes as such.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Age or stage (child, adolescent, adult; the evidence is pre-k-12, with one synthesis of ages 2–6). Before teaching, observe the target skill in an ordinary situation: what a learner does when frustrated by a task, when a peer disagrees, when asked to join a group. Record the situation and who observed it (teacher, the learner, a peer, a parent), since ratings differ by informant. Note adversity, trauma or chronic stress where known, language and literacy, and neurodivergence, without reading an atypical emotional expression as a deficit.
The skill Which competency and which named skill within it ("identify the emotion, then choose a strategy"; asking for help; disagreeing without insult). One skill per unit of teaching. Whether learners already have the words for it in the language of instruction.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-instructor-led, online-self-paced, workplace-clinical), mode (all in the room, some remote, phone-only), days-weeks or term-plus, minutes a week available, group size and number of adults, who will deliver it (the learners' own teacher, other school staff or an outside provider), and whether the school's discipline and reward practice agrees or conflicts with what is taught.
Learner-valued goal Ask what the learner wants from it: fewer fights with a particular friend, to stop panicking in tests, to be picked for a team, to handle patients or customers. Adolescents and adults especially may not value "SEL" as named. Record disagreement with the designer objective.
Designer objective Which outcome is the target: the skill itself, conduct (referrals, suspensions, disruption), emotional distress, belonging, or academic achievement. Name the one the evaluation will weigh: the evidence says they move by very different amounts.
Outcome An observed or performance measure of the skill in a new situation, beside any self-report; conduct and achievement measured separately; immediate and delayed (a term or a year later). Say whether the measure was written by the programme (aligned and closer to the intervention) or is independent.

A request to "add SEL" or "build students' resilience" does not specify these. Ask which skill, for which learners, delivered by whom, how many minutes a week for how long, and measured how, before choosing a curriculum, a lesson order or a routine.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

The default sequence is the earlier page's. The SAFE practices reported as moderators in the programme syntheses bear on it as a whole (steps 2 and 4 make it explicit and active; one skill at a time makes it focused; the order makes it sequenced); no step is tested on its own. Durations and progressions are untested proposals unless a claim is cited.

Dose across a programme (untested proposal). One explicit lesson of 20–30 minutes a week for each named skill, for at least a school term per competency, with 2–5 minutes of daily cueing and use inside ordinary lessons; plan for a full school year, and repeat with new skills in following years. In the first two or three weeks of a skill, the adult supplies the scenarios, scripts and sentence starters and models every step; in the middle weeks, learners rehearse with scenarios drawn from their own class life and the adult coaches; in the last weeks, learners choose the situations, prompts are withdrawn one at a time, and the adult only cues. A learner moves on from a skill when they use it unprompted in two different settings (for example in group work and at break) on two separate occasions, observed by an adult or reported by a peer; a learner who still needs the prompt keeps the script while the class moves on. From the first week of each skill, send the other adults who see the learners (and, in early childhood, families) a short note naming the skill, its steps and one way to cue it, so the cueing in step 7 is shared rather than left to one teacher.

  1. Climate-setting: predictable routines and relational safety. Greet learners individually; run a short, regular check-in; co-author a few class norms. Proposal: daily, 2–5 minutes, from the first week and throughout. Evidence status: positive greetings at the door [+M] raised academic engaged time and reduced disruption in a randomized evaluation with middle-school teachers against an attention control; it is a climate step tested on engagement, not on social and emotional skill. In one national youth-programme evaluation, facilitators and youth described adult co-regulation [+W] (a short shared breathing routine at the start of class, calm adult responses) as making the room feel safe for questions and helping youth manage high emotions: focus-group accounts, not a comparison. The check-in is untested here (see the Check-ins principle).
  2. Explicit skill instruction. Name and teach one skill in steps ("notice the feeling, name it, choose a strategy, try it") through direct instruction and demonstration. Proposal: 20–30 minutes once a week. Evidence status: carried from the programme syntheses' SAFE moderator (SEL skills [+S]). The one recorded test of explicit teaching against its absence is small: in a randomized comparison of six preschool classrooms of children with developmental delays (38 children), a social-skills curriculum (instruct, model, practise) plus reinforcement raised observed sharing well above reinforcement alone (ES 1.12), while time spent in a group rose by a non-significant amount (ES 0.28) (Taking Part training [+W]). For the naming step, emotion labelling [~M] reduced physiological fear a week later, but not self-reported fear, in spider-fearful adults under exposure, a clinical laboratory study, not a classroom one.
  3. Modelling. The adult narrates their own regulation and social decisions aloud during real moments ("I'm getting frustrated with this projector, so I'm going to take a breath and try the other cable"). Proposal: whenever a genuine moment arises, at least once a lesson in the first weeks. Evidence status: untested as a step; the co-regulation accounts [+W] above describe adults regulating with youth, reported experience only.
  4. Guided practice with feedback. Learners rehearse the skill in low-stakes structured activities: role-play, class discussion of scenarios, and structured cooperative learning. Give feedback on what the learner did and how (the task and the strategy), not praise of the person. Proposal: 10–15 minutes of the weekly lesson, scenarios supplied early and drawn from learners' own situations later. Evidence status: the "active" SAFE practice in the syntheses (SEL skills [+S]); in a meta-analysis of 104 secondary-school programmes, as summarised in a policy review, programmes giving students practice of the skill during the sessions were among the more effective (SEL moderators [+M]); for group practice, group rewards resting on each member's learning [+M] raised achievement in school cooperative methods, carried here to social practice it did not test; for the feedback, task- and process-level feedback [+M], carried from feedback on learning tasks to feedback on social behaviour.
  5. Embedded application. Learners use the skill inside real academic work (peer feedback, collaborative problem-solving, a group project), with the adult coaching in the moment. Proposal: in at least two ordinary lessons a week, the skill named in the lesson plan. Evidence status: untested as a step. In the syntheses, programmes taught by learners' own classroom teachers showed significant skill and academic effects where programmes run by non-school personnel did not (SEL and achievement [~S]); that is a delivery finding, not a test of embedding. A design-principles report argues for teaching social and emotional skills alongside subject content (integrated skills [+W]), from a neuroscience rationale with no comparison.
  6. Reflection. Learners name what they did and what they would do differently, through short structured reflection prompts or journals, privately as well as aloud. Proposal: 3–5 minutes at the end of the weekly lesson and after any coached incident. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
  7. Reinforcement across settings, with the whole staff and families, then fading. Cue and acknowledge the skill in corridors, group work, assessment situations and at home; tell the other adults who see the learners (subject teachers, support staff, families) which skill is being taught this term and the words used for it, so they cue it the same way; remove prompts one at a time once a learner uses the skill unprompted (the moving-on rule above). Evidence status: untested as a step. The follow-up synthesis [+S] shows programme gains outlasting the programme (6 months to 18 years after) but does not say which programmes reinforced across settings. In the syntheses summarised by Greenberg (SEL moderators) [~M], secondary programmes involving the entire school staff, and preschool programmes combining family and school components, did better than those without; a classroom teacher alone was weaker in the preschool synthesis. A brief reports one study in which students used mindfulness-based conflict-resolution strategies with siblings and friends [+W] outside school, a second-hand account with no design details.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Who will deliver it, who else will cue it, and how often. The older school-age synthesis found skill and academic effects for programmes run by learners' own teachers and not, or not significantly, for programmes run by outside personnel or as multicomponent programmes, and implementation problems lowered effects. Later syntheses, summarised in a policy review, qualify "the teacher alone": in preschool, family-and-school programmes did better and a classroom teacher alone did worse, and in secondary school the more effective programmes were longer and involved the entire staff (SEL moderators [~M]). So the default is the learners' own teacher teaching it, with the other adults around them cueing it. If unstated, ask: will the learners' own teacher teach and cue this every week, for how long, and who else (staff, families) will know the skill?
  • Which outcome the brief is buying. Social and emotional skills moved most; conduct, distress and attitudes less (across twelve meta-analyses, conduct problems ES 0.13–0.33 and emotional distress 0.10–0.31: behaviour and distress [+M]); academic achievement least, and in rigorous recent trials by a negligible amount. If unstated, ask: what result would count as success, and how will it be measured?
  • Age or stage, and whether the learners chose to be there. The evidence is from school children and adolescents (and one synthesis of ages 2–6); nothing here tests adults. If unstated, ask: how old are the learners, and will they see this as theirs or as something done to them?

Teach one skill and leave a routine (a check-in card, a breathing step) the teacher will run daily; or spend the session on the adults' discipline mindset instead. Empathy-building claim [~M]: most prejudice-reduction studies test light-touch interventions with unclear long-term effects; empathic discipline [+M]: a brief teacher exercise reduced suspensions for high-risk groups, with a marginal overall effect in the preregistered replication
Exclusions, suspensions or disruption are the presenting problem Add a short empathic-discipline exercise for teachers and a daily greeting routine before or alongside the student programme; track exclusions by group. Learners use a language other than the language of instruction, have low literacy, or are deaf or hard of hearing Use visual emotion scales and choice cards; teach the emotion words directly, in the learners' own language (a signed language for deaf learners); allow pointing or drawing in check-ins; extend practice cycles. Where learners have had little deep communication at home (many deaf children have hearing parents who do not sign fluently), teach the skills more directly and explicitly rather than expecting them to be picked up. Follow-up synthesis [~S]: benefits similar across race, socioeconomic status and school location (abstract); adaptation itself is untested
One teacher with a large class (30+) and little time Run the skill lesson whole-class with paired rehearsal and cue cards; use a 2-minute written check-in instead of a sharing circle; coach a few learners each day in rotation. Untested proposal
Mixed modes: some learners remote, some in the room, or phone-only Run check-ins as a private poll or chat message so remote learners are seen equally; pair each remote learner with one in the room for rehearsal (breakout or phone call); send the week's skill as a short text with one practice task. Untested proposal
online-self-paced, no adult present Use short video models and private reflection prompts; ask learners to log one real use of the skill a week; add a human contact (tutor message, peer partner) where possible, since coaching and modelling in the moment cannot happen. Untested proposal

Group rewards on individual learning [~M], carried from achievement in cooperative methods; the audience format is untested
The evaluation relies on student self-report Add an observed or teacher-rated measure and a conduct record; report each separately. Early-childhood synthesis [~S]: effects varied by informant and assessment type; emotion labelling [~M]: the physiological measure moved when self-report did not

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • The programme as a whole, on the skills. SEL programs improve social-emotional skills [+S]: across 213 universal school-based programmes for K–12 students, social and emotional skills improved most at post-test (mean ES = 0.57 over 68 interventions, against 0.22–0.27 for attitudes, positive social behaviour, conduct problems, emotional distress and academic performance); skill effects were significant for classroom programmes run by teachers (0.62) and not for multicomponent programmes; the SAFE practices and implementation problems moderated outcomes; heterogeneity was high (I² = 91%). In centre-based early childhood (ages 2–6, 63 studies), universal curricula improved social competence (d = 0.30) and emotional competence (d = 0.54), and early learning skills by less (d = 0.18). Both are syntheses against controls, mostly at post-test; skill measures include programme-aligned ones.
  • Behaviour and achievement. Universal school-based SEL programs improve students' behaviour, with a small and, in recent randomised trials, negligible effect on academic achievement [+S] and SEL Programs Improve Academic Achievement [~S] carry the same three syntheses. The older one reports academic performance ES 0.27 (35 interventions; the authors translate it as 11 percentile points), significant only when school staff delivered the programme (teacher delivery 0.34; non-school personnel 0.12, not significant). A contemporary synthesis of 424 studies reports improved behaviours, school functioning and achievement (abstract only, no effect size read). A synthesis restricted to randomised or rigorous quasi-experimental studies with elementary and middle-school students found overall achievement g = 0.08, g = 0.063 in randomised trials, larger for GPA (g = 0.33). A policy review of twelve SEL meta-analyses gives the same ordering: academic performance ES 0.18–0.28 (engagement and academic performance [+M]), conduct problems 0.13–0.33 and emotional distress 0.10–0.31 (behaviour and distress [+M]); the "11 percentile points" repeated in that review, in another report and in a charter-school brief (schoolwide SEL and achievement [+W]) is Durlak et al.'s figure retold, not further studies. This is the main limit on the pattern: plan SEL for its social and emotional outcomes, and treat an achievement gain as possible and small.
  • Durability. Social-emotional learning benefits persist at follow-up [+S]: a synthesis of 82 universal school-based programmes found participants ahead of controls on skills, attitudes and well-being 6 months to 18 years later, similar across race, socioeconomic status and school location (abstract only, no pooled effect read); a randomized trial of an enriched Head Start curriculum with 356 low-income preschoolers found advantages on 7 of 11 well-being measures at age 21–22 (d = .21 to .33). The older synthesis's follow-up studies still showed academic effects (ES 0.32, k = 8).
  • The adults' side. Brief intervention empathic discipline cuts suspensions [+M]: a brief online exercise for middle-school mathematics teachers halved year-long suspensions in one randomized field experiment (9.6% to 4.8%); a preregistered, active-placebo replication found a small, marginal overall effect and narrowed the Black/Hispanic disparity from 10.6 to 5.9 percentage points. It targets teachers' responses to students, not students' skills, and its outcome is an administrative record.
  • Programme and delivery features. Program and delivery characteristics moderate SEL effects [~M]: a policy review's summary of two meta-analyses. In 104 secondary-school studies, the more effective programmes were longer, included insight building and problem-solving, gave practice during sessions and involved the whole staff; in 33 preschool studies, family-and-school programmes did better and programmes delivered by a classroom teacher alone did worse. Higher implementation quality went with stronger outcomes, and stronger designs gave somewhat smaller effects. These are moderator analyses across studies, not tests of adding one feature to one programme; they qualify, rather than contradict, the older synthesis's finding for own-teacher delivery over outside personnel.
  • Explicit teaching against reinforcement alone. Taking Part training [+W]: the only recorded comparison of explicit social-skills teaching with its absence, a randomized trial in six preschool classrooms (38 children with developmental delays): sharing ES 1.12, being in a group ES 0.28, not significant. Small, early-childhood and disability-specific.
  • The surrounding climate. SEL programs cannot produce long-term growth in punitive or exclusionary environments [~W] is a policy report's assertion, with no study. The nearest tested evidence is from belonging interventions at college entry (belonging interventions [~M]), where a brief message raised first-year completion only at institutions that afforded students' groups opportunities to belong. No claim in the wiki tests an SEL programme in a punitive against a supportive school climate.
  • One step's mechanism. Emotion Labeling Improves Regulation [~M]: 88 spider-fearful adults randomly assigned to affect labelling, reappraisal, distraction or exposure alone; labelling gave a larger drop in skin conductance a week later (d = 0.64–0.85) and no difference in self-reported fear. It supports naming emotions as one part of a routine, not as a classroom effect.

Do not rank SEL against academic instruction from these claims: none compares the two for time spent, and the achievement effects are small and contested. Do not read rising self-report, or a calm SEL lesson, as the skill attained. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (this sequence, or one step of it, against the same programme without it, on an observed skill measure a term later) and the local observation that would stand in for it.

The evidence by question¶

  • Do sustained, structured programmes produce social-emotional gains, and do they last? SEL programs improve social emotional skills [+S]
  • Do SEL programmes also improve conduct, distress and academic achievement, and by how much? Universal school-based SEL programs improve students' behaviour, with a small and, in recent randomised trials, negligible effect on academic achievement [~S]; SEL programs reduce behaviour problems and distress [+M]; SEL programs improve engagement and academic performance [+M]
  • Which programme and delivery features go with larger effects, and who besides the teacher should be involved? Program and delivery characteristics moderate SEL effects [~M]
  • Does explicit teaching of a social skill add to reinforcing it? Taking Part training [+W]
  • Does the school's climate limit what SEL lessons can achieve? SEL requires educative, inclusive environments [~W]; belonging interventions [~M], carried from college entry

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims this page cited before the 2026-10-05 rewrite, or that bear on it, which are not core evidence above. Each marker is capped by the claim's recorded evidence.

  • Autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. [+M] — a review, a meta-analysis of extrinsic rewards (tangible expected rewards undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks) and a conceptual framework; none is about SEL. Used above only for framing with adolescents, by extrapolation. The earlier page said SEL programmes "emphasizing choice and voice outperform compliance-oriented versions"; no claim here tests that.
  • Building empathy improves intergroup attitudes. [~M] — laboratory experiments, a classroom diversity programme for grades 4–6 and a review that flags publication bias and light-touch interventions. Bears on the social-awareness strand; its model is on the Building Empathy principle.
  • Reading fiction gives at most a very small gain on theory-of-mind and social-cognition tasks, and the single-passage literary-fiction effect did not replicate [~W] — the earlier page cited it [+W] as "Fiction reading improves empathy". The claim has since been narrowed: the average gain is on social-cognition tasks, below a small effect, and no recorded study measures empathy. Do not use fiction reading as an SEL component on this evidence.
  • Feedback is most effective at task and process levels. [+M] — two meta-analyses of feedback on learning and performance, supporting the task/process distinction indirectly. Used above for feedback in rehearsal, carried from academic tasks to social behaviour.
  • Cooperative learning with group rewards and individual accountability improves outcomes. [+M] — school cooperative methods and achievement. Used above for structuring group practice, by extrapolation; it measures no social or emotional outcome.
  • Social and emotional competence can be enhanced through explicit instruction, teaching practices, and curriculum integration [+W] — read 2026-10-19: CASEL's web page names the three approach types and cites no study or effect size. It does not compare them, so it does not settle stand-alone lessons against integration; it leaves the model unchanged.
  • Students' success in school and beyond is inextricably linked to healthy social and emotional development [+W] — read 2026-10-19: a consensus statement that social and emotional development predicts success, with no study printed. It is a rationale for teaching the skills, not evidence that a programme changes them; no change to the model.
  • Assessment for learning improves achievement. [+M] — read 2026-10-19 (not about this page): K–12 formative-assessment meta-analyses on achievement (d ≈ .20–.29, larger with student self-assessment). The merged Responsive Classroom fragment linked it to teacher language; it measures no social or emotional outcome.
  • Digital Intensity Attenuates Ef Reappraisal Link [+M]
  • Ef Associated With Reappraisal And Lower Suppression Adolescents [+M]
  • Emotion regulation is concurrently associated with executive performance in the reverse direction, with habitual suppression linked to diminished EF [+W]

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested design. A Year 7 form tutor (adolescent learners, 28 in the class, classroom, one 25-minute tutor period a week plus 10 minutes each morning) is asked to "do SEL" after a term of arguments in group work. The table changed the default: she chooses one skill, disagreeing without insult, and frames it around fairness rather than "managing feelings"; the morning slot carries a greeting and a written one-word check-in; the weekly period carries the explicit lesson and paired role-play, with scenarios she writes for the first three weeks and pupils write after that; the science and English teachers name the skill in one group task each a week and note who uses it unprompted. One pupil says he wants to stop being sent out of maths; the tutor's objective is fewer group-work conflicts. They agree to track both. Elements and limits¶

Check-ins, coaching, class discussion, reflection, belonging and role-play; the strategies SEL curricula, SEL routines, SEL integration and the 'What's my emotion?' check-in. Principles the pattern draws on: Collaborative Learning, Check-ins, Building Empathy, Clear Structure, Assessment for Learning, Self-Regulated Learning, Engagement and Autonomy. Learner variables: belonging and self-regulation.

This pattern is scoped to sustained, explicit teaching and reinforcement of named social and emotional skills, mostly in schools. A one-off workshop, a whole-school behaviour policy, clinical treatment of anxiety or trauma, and professional formation for adults need other configurations or additional evidence. The policy supports observation and design reasoning; which of its steps matter, at what weekly dose, for adults, and against what achievement cost in teaching time, remains to be tested.

Claims¶

  • SEL program effects are sustained over long-term follow-up periods [+W]
  • Utah Schools for the Deaf and Blind adapted the Resilience, Rights & Respectful Relationships curriculum for intentional, direct SEL instruction with deaf students [~W]
  • Cognitive, emotional, and social skills should be taught alongside content because learning is integrated in the brain [+W]
  • Schoolwide SEL programs are associated with long-term achievement gains, with multiple meta-analyses estimating an 11-percentile-point gain on average for participating students [+W]
  • Students who learned mindfulness-based conflict resolution strategies used them outside school with siblings and friends [+W]
  • SEL programs reduce disruptive behavior problems and emotional distress and foster prosocial behavior [+W]
  • SEL programs are associated with an 11 percentile-point improvement in academic performance [+W]
  • SEL programs cannot produce meaningful long-term growth in authoritarian, punitive, or exclusionary environments; restorative practices are needed [~W]
  • Curriculum add-ons PATHS and CSRP improve children's EFs and related outcomes in regular classrooms, with CSRP's academic gains mediated largely via EF improvement [+W]
  • There was no clear pattern to the relationship between student, teacher, and school characteristics and the effectiveness of the interventions. [~W]
  • SCOE supported 3,000 educators in almost 200 schools in receiving SEL training through RULER since 2019 [+W]
  • No SEL construct status or growth significantly predicted chronic absenteeism in 9th grade after controlling for background characteristics [~W]
  • A 2017 meta-analysis found SEL program participation associated with a 6% increase in high school and an 11% increase in college graduation rates [+M]
  • Zoo U subscales are intercorrelated in patterns the authors attribute to real-world overlap among social skills competencies [~W]
  • SEL interventions improve attitudes, self-regulation, behaviour, and academic performance across reported effect-size ranges [+W]
  • Programme characteristics — longer duration, higher frequency, teacher delivery and older students — were tentatively associated with more significant well-being effects [+W]
  • Most school-based mental health programs reported in Australian schools were social and emotional learning programs, some without an evidence base [~W]
  • Conflict resolution, peace education and peer mediation programs have a large overall positive effect on students' conflict resolution skills (Hedge's g = 1.256) [+W]
  • A prior meta-analysis suggested primary school classroom management programs had a small, statistically significant effect on most outcomes, with stronger effects for interventions focused on social-emotional development and teacher development [+M]

Related Patterns¶

  • Cooperative Learning — depends on the relationship skills SEL develops; the two patterns are mutually reinforcing when group work carries individual accountability
  • Case-Based Learning — cases with interpersonal and ethical dimensions provide authentic contexts for practicing decision-making and perspective-taking
  • Formative Assessment — its feedback culture overlaps with SEL's self-management goal; both depend on students interpreting feedback as information, not judgment
  • Self-Regulated Learning — owns planning, monitoring and acting on a learning task; SEL's self-management strand overlaps it and extends to emotion and conduct
  • Direct Instruction — the format of the explicit skill lesson in step 2
  • Community of Inquiry — both treat social presence as a precondition for deep engagement, though RC operates at classroom-routine grain rather than discourse grain

Examples¶

  • Bronxdale High School whole-school design — inquiry instruction integrated with social-emotional supports and restorative discipline
  • Gateway guiding principles and structures — inclusivity, relationships and continuous improvement carried by advisories, inclusion and project-based instruction

PATHS (Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies): A widely evaluated K–6 curriculum teaching emotion labeling, self-control, and problem-solving through scripted lessons plus "Feelings Fair" classroom integration; central to the Durlak et al. evidence base.

Second Step: A K–8 SEL curriculum (Committee for Children) using weekly lessons, puppet-based modeling in early grades, and peer-narrated video scenarios in middle school, with skills reinforced through teacher cueing during academic work.

RULER approach (Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence): Teaches Recognizing, Understanding, Labeling, Expressing, and Regulating emotion through tools like the Mood Meter; implemented school-wide so adults and students use a shared emotional vocabulary.

Medical education — reflective practice seminars: Residency programs use structured case discussions and Balint-style groups to develop self-awareness, empathy, and relationship skills with patients — SEL competencies reframed as professional formation. - Center for Responsive Schools — the developer organization; publishes RC professional development, the Responsive Classroom course sequence, and implementation fidelity tools used in the evaluation studies below. - Fairfield, Connecticut elementary schools (i3-scale RCT): A large randomized controlled trial across 24 elementary schools found significant gains in math and reading achievement and improved teacher efficacy in schools implementing RC with fidelity (Ottmar et al., 2017). - Morning Meeting in a 3rd-grade classroom: 25-minute daily meeting — greeting around the circle, two student shares with peer questions, a quick cooperative game, and preview of the day's learning goals — followed by interactive modeling of the new math-station routine. - Tailor SEL programming to developmental level, from preschool skill practice through adolescent status-and-respect opportunities
