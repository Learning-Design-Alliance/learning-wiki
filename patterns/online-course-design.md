# Online Course Design (Community of Inquiry)

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 42 claims (20 for, 21 mixed, 1 against) · 29 studies (8 associational, 5 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 4 qualitative, 4 design, 3 review, 1 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 5 of 29 report an effect size · 40 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable course-level configuration for a course taught mostly or wholly online (`online-instructor-led`, or `online-self-paced` with a tutor behind it), over `days-weeks` or a `term-plus`. It shapes the whole term: one structure held constant from week to week; a weekly cycle in which learners meet content, each other and the teacher by design rather than by chance; an opening week built around contact with a person; discussion that is structured to reach a conclusion; and support for learners pacing themselves. The intended changes are in what each learner can do on the course's representative task at the end of term (`general-achievement`, `skill-performance`) and in how many learners are still there (`persistence`).

Its two organising ideas come from the earlier page. Moore (1989) named the distance online learners feel **transactional distance** and argued that it is reduced by designing three kinds of interaction: learner with content, learner with instructor, and learner with learner. The Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework (Garrison, Anderson & Archer, 1999) describes a good online course as one where three "presences" overlap: cognitive presence (learners building meaning through sustained discussion and reflection), social presence (learners able to come across as real people to the group) and teaching presence (the design, facilitation and direct instruction that organise the other two). Both are frameworks, not tested findings. The wiki holds no claim page for Moore (1989); the 74-study interaction meta-analysis the earlier page cited ([Bernard et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654309333844)) is in the wiki only second-hand, as [a later chapter's report that it verified the importance of all three interaction types](../claims/three-interaction-types-verified-meta-analysis-distance-learning.md) [+W], with no effect sizes in the text the wiki read.

**No claim in this wiki tests an online course built this way against one that is not.** No claim compares courses that differ in the three interaction types or the three presences on learning or completion. What the wiki holds is: one synthesis comparing online and blended courses with face-to-face ones, in which the blended advantage cannot be credited to the format; one instrumental-variables study at a large university in which taking a course online rather than in person lowered grades in that course and in later ones; a second-hand report that interaction treatments of all three kinds matter in distance education; a synthesis of self-regulation interventions in online and blended courses; randomised field experiments of a brief planning activity in online courses; one experiment on structuring interdependence in asynchronous online groups, which found no difference in achievement; enrolment records showing where online learners drop out; and surveys, interview studies and design case studies of online courses, including one on which discussion prompts produce collaborative talk and one on auditing an asynchronous course's activities. So the sequence below is the earlier page's, kept as a **default**, with each step's evidence status stated; the response-dependent policy beside it is an **untested design proposal**.

How it sits beside its converted neighbours, each of which owns a narrower or adjacent relationship and is not repeated here:

- [Blended Learning](blended-learning.md) decides how to divide a course between online and in-person time. This page assumes the course is online for most or all of it (the blended page's convention is 80% or more online) and decides how that online course runs across a term.
- [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) holds the conditional model of a facilitated discussion community and what to observe when its inquiry stalls. This page uses it for the discussion strand of the course and adds everything else a course needs (structure, content, pacing, first week, retention).
- [Digital Learning](../principles/digital-learning.md) decides whether a digital tool earns its place and what method it carries. This page takes the decision to teach online as given and does not credit the medium with any gain.
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../principles/self-regulated-learning.md) holds the model of planning, monitoring and adjusting one's own study, which self-paced online work demands.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Prior knowledge of the content (`novice`, `intermediate`, `advanced`, `mixed`), `age` and `population_band` (`postsecondary`, `adult-workplace`, `pre-k-12`); a short task on the course's first topic, done in week 1; prior experience of studying online without a teacher in the room; device, connection and whether they can join live sessions; when in the week they can study; language and reading confidence. Record unknowns as unknown. |
| Context and activities | `online-instructor-led` or `online-self-paced`; term length (accelerated 6–8 weeks, standard 10–15 weeks, open enrolment); enrolment and the number of teaching staff; whether any learners join from a shared room while others are remote; the platform and what it records; whether live sessions are possible and at what times; whether the course shares a template with other courses in the programme. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask why each learner is taking the course and what they need from it: a credential, a skill for work, contact with peers in their field, flexibility around work and family. Record where it diverges from the designer's objective; the divergence is an observation, not a defect. |
| Designer objective | The capability and its knowledge type (`concept`, `procedure`, `principle`, `complex-skill`, `metacognitive-strategy`); the representative task; which parts of it need discussion with others, which need feedback from a teacher, and which need repeated practice with content alone. |
| Outcome | The course's end-of-term assessment or representative task (`general-achievement`, `skill-performance`), each learner's completion and the week they stopped (`persistence`), time spent (`learning-time`), and participation in each strand; each with its instrument and horizon. Presence or satisfaction surveys are `self-report` and are recorded as such, not as learning. |
| Comparison being made | What this course replaces: a face-to-face version, an earlier online version, or nothing. Record what else changes with it (time, materials, assessment, teacher), because the evidence cannot separate those from the course format. |

A request to "put this course online" or "make the online course more engaging" does not specify these. Ask for the representative task, the term length, the enrolment and the staff time available before prescribing a weekly rhythm, discussion load or feedback turnaround.

## Sequence and conditional policy

The default sequence is the earlier page's five steps, expanded into a term. Steps 2, 3, 5 and 6 have neighbouring claims, carried by extrapolation; the others rest on descriptive, correlational or second-hand claims, or on none, and are untested here. Quantities are proposals to set locally and record.

1. **Fix one course structure and hold it for the whole term.** One page per week in the same place, with the same parts in the same order: what this week is for, the content, the activities with their due dates, and when feedback will come back. Use the programme's template if there is one. Evidence status: [a programme-wide template](../claims/course-template-supports-learner-presence.md) [+W] was described by students and authors of one accelerated online MBA as giving cross-course familiarity and high ratings for course organisation (qualitative, no comparison); [organisation items](../claims/coi-presence-uneven-profile-mba.md) [+W] were the teaching-presence items rated highest in the same programme; and in a survey of 530 college students, [a clear course orientation from the instructor was among the strategies associated with less self-reported digital distraction](../claims/instructor-content-engagement-associated-lower-distraction.md) [~W] (association-rule mining, small coefficients). None shows that structure changes learning.
2. **Make week 1 a contact week, not a content week.** Before any graded work, give each learner a short task that needs a reply from a person: an introduction post linked to why they are taking the course, answered by the teacher within 48 hours, or a 15-minute live call in small groups. Check by day 3 that every learner has logged in and done one thing, and contact by message anyone who has not. Evidence status: [online attrition in orientation week was twice that of onground classes' first week](../claims/orientation-week-attrition-double-online.md) [~M], and [after instruction began, drop rates were essentially the same online and onground](../claims/no-drop-rate-difference-after-instruction-starts.md) [~M], in one university continuing-education programme over two years (observational). That locates the extra loss, not a remedy: the contact task and its timings are untested proposals.
3. **Ask each learner to write an if-then plan in week 1.** Two or three sentences: what they want from the course, the most likely obstacle, and "if (obstacle), then I will (action)". Revisit it at the midpoint. Evidence status: [forming implementation intentions improves goal attainment](../claims/implementation-intentions-improve-goal-attainment.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis of 94 tests (mostly university students), and two randomised field experiments in online courses where a brief writing activity combining mental contrasting with if-then plans raised completion for learners in individualist cultures and left learners in collectivist cultures unaffected (read from the abstract). Expect it to help some learners, not all.
4. **Run a weekly cycle with each of the three interactions in it.** A proposal for a standard 10–15 week term, about 6–9 hours of learner time a week:
   - *Learner with content* (about 2–3 hours): short segments (video or reading of 10–20 minutes each) with a few questions after each, answers shown. Put practice and self-checks here, because this is the part learners do alone.
   - *Learner with learner* (about 2–3 hours): one discussion task with a question that has more than one defensible answer and a product the group must agree on (see step 6). Initial contribution by day 3 of the week, responses and the group product by day 6.
   - *Learner with instructor* (about 1–2 hours): a weekly announcement that summarises the previous week's discussion and names common errors; feedback on each graded piece within 48–72 hours; one optional live slot a week or a fortnight.
   *Check the content strand before the term starts.* Code each week's content activities by what they ask the learner to do (passive watching, active answering, constructive producing, interactive with others; the ICAP modes) and make sure each week has at least one activity that produces an output which gets feedback, automatic or from a person; in an asynchronous course that output is the only way the teacher sees what a learner did. A content video does not need the teacher's face on screen for learning.
   Evidence status: the cycle and its quantities are untested proposals from the earlier page's three interaction types; the [second-hand report of Bernard et al. (2009)](../claims/three-interaction-types-verified-meta-analysis-distance-learning.md) [+W] supports designing all three, without saying how much of each. In one audit of an eight-unit asynchronous postgraduate course, the authors found [the ICAP framework a workable tool for judging how active each activity was and where to redesign](../claims/icap-effective-audit-tool-asynchronous-online.md) [+W], and argued that [feedback on an output is what makes a learner's work "seen" when no teacher is present](../claims/feedback-makes-behaviour-seen-asynchronous.md) [+W] (a design case and its authors' argument; neither measured learning). A narrative review reports that [adding the instructor's face to lecture videos made no significant difference to learning performance, though students rated it as helping them learn](../claims/human-embodiment-video-presence-effects.md) [~W] (second-hand; equivalence not tested). One survey of 236 students at four universities found [students ranked interaction with content first for online learning and with the teacher first for face-to-face](../claims/interaction-priority-f2f-teacher-online-content.md) [~M], which is a statement of priorities, not of what they learn from.
5. **Support self-pacing inside the course, not only with advice.** Each week, a visible schedule with due dates, a short self-check with answers, and a prompt to plan the next week ("what will you do, and when?"). Evidence status: [self-regulated learning interventions had a moderate pooled effect on learning outcomes in online and blended environments](../claims/srl-interventions-moderate-effect-online-blended.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 15 studies (5,519 participants), one synthesis; it does not say which components carried the effect.
6. **Design discussion so it needs the group and reaches a conclusion.** Move from teacher-led whole-class boards in weeks 1–3 to small groups of four to six in weeks 4 onwards, with a task the group must complete together and present (a case analysis, a ranked recommendation, a shared answer to a problem), rotating a facilitator role. Write prompts that ask learners to exchange information they hold (a case from their setting, data they collected, a resource they found) and build on each other's, rather than prompts that ask each learner to reflect privately on their own understanding. In the last third of the term, learners facilitate their group and write the synthesis; the teacher summarises across groups. Evidence status: in one online teacher-education unit of 40 students, [a student-led small-group discussion assessment produced denser peer interaction than a standard teacher-led board](../claims/student-led-discussion-task-sustains-online-participation.md) [+M] (network maps from one week of each, design case study). [Structuring role or reward interdependence in asynchronous online groups did not change posttest achievement](../claims/interdependence-type-no-achievement-effect-asynchronous.md) [-M] in one experiment with 280 undergraduate business majors, so do not expect roles or group grades alone to raise learning. In an online asynchronous professional-development course for teachers, [prompts asking for an exchange of information produced more transactive (building-on-each-other) discourse than prompts asking teachers to reflect on their content understanding or practice](../claims/information-exchange-prompts-higher-transactivity.md) [+W] (design study, discourse coding, no statistic printed). The case-study authors conclude that [online learning communities must be designed for and scaffolded through teaching and assessment activities](../claims/online-communities-must-be-designed-and-scaffolded.md) [+W]; when courses moved online in 2020, [collaborative activities largely disappeared unless they were redesigned](../claims/remote-shift-reduced-collaborative-learning.md) [+W] (case studies of 29 courses); and in one online case-based course, [learners' co-regulation correlated more strongly with all three presences than their self-regulation did](../claims/co-regulation-stronger-presence-relationships-than-self-regulation.md) [+W] (47 graduate students), which suggests that peer monitoring belongs in the discussion strand but does not show it raises learning. Group size, prompt type's effect on learning and the progression are untested.
7. **Keep the teacher visible and the discussion corrected.** Feedback on graded work within 48–72 hours; one teacher post per group per week that pushes towards a conclusion rather than praising participation; a live session where errors need correcting in real time. Evidence status: untested for learning; the earlier page's immediacy studies (Bodie & Michel, 2014) have no claim page here. [A discussion board produced no corrective feedback at all, against text chat and videoconferencing](../claims/discussion-board-no-negative-feedback-synchrony-required.md) [~M] in one second-language study, which is a reason not to rely on an asynchronous board for correction. When a live session runs, spend it on problems, discussion and questions rather than lecture: [active learning raised exam performance over lecturing](../claims/active-learning-improves-exam-performance.md) [+M] by 0.47 SD across 158 comparisons, in face-to-face undergraduate STEM courses, carried here to live online sessions by extrapolation (marker lowered for that reason); and [interactive synchronous sessions and instructor-driven reflection went with less self-reported digital distraction](../claims/instructor-content-engagement-associated-lower-distraction.md) [~W] in one survey of college students.
8. **Watch completion and participation by week, and act on the first missed week.** Record who completes each week's content and discussion. Message a learner after the first missed deadline, not after the third. Evidence status: untested proposal; [online continuing-education courses kept fewer learners than comparable onground ones](../claims/online-continuing-education-persistence-lower-than-onground.md) [~M] (79% against 84% over eight quarters, observational), and [a single "tipping point" lesson explained dropout in only about a quarter of 196 online courses](../claims/tipping-point-hypothesis-insufficient-online-dropout.md) [~M], so watch every week rather than one critical week. When contacting a learner who has stopped, ask about time and circumstances first: in the same continuing-education programme, [dissatisfaction with the technology, teaching or content accounted for only 5% of coded withdrawal reasons](../claims/dissatisfaction-not-primary-drop-reason.md) [~W] (voluntary, textually assigned codes, many unclassified), so a reduced catch-up route is more likely to answer the reason than a course redesign.
9. **Assess the course on each learner's own performance at the end of term.** Use the representative task, done individually, at the horizon the objective sets; report completion beside it. Do not use post counts, presence ratings or satisfaction as the outcome. Evidence status: untested proposal. [More interactions went with higher posttest scores](../claims/interaction-quantity-correlates-posttest-online-groups.md) [~M] in one asynchronous group experiment, a correlation that does not show more posting causes learning.

**Progression across the term** (untested proposal): before the term, audit each week's content activities (step 4) and write the discussion prompts as information exchanges with a group product (step 6); weeks 1–2, contact task, if-then plan, teacher-led whole-class discussion with model posts, feedback on everything within 48 hours; weeks 3–6, small groups with a teacher-assigned facilitator, teacher posts once per group per week, self-checks with answers; weeks 7 onwards, learner facilitators, group products presented to the class, the teacher summarising across groups and feedback concentrated on the graded pieces; the final weeks rehearse the representative task without help. A learner moves on to the next week's work on the due date whether or not the previous week is complete; a learner who has missed two consecutive weeks gets a direct contact and a reduced catch-up route (the two or three activities that the representative task needs), not the whole backlog.

The enrolment records place most of the extra online loss here, but say nothing about what brings learners back. |
| Many learners post once and never reply | Read the prompt: does it have more than one defensible answer and need others' input? Thin replies do not separate a weak prompt from too little time or low trust in the group. |

| Learners do the discussion but fail the content self-checks | Compare self-check results with discussion participation by learner; code that week's content activities by ICAP mode (passive, active, constructive, interactive) and note which produce an output that gets feedback. | The content strand is not teaching enough on its own: replace passive segments with ones that ask for an answer or a product, add worked material and checks with feedback, or a live session on that topic. Participation does not show understanding, and whether the ICAP audit's redesigns raise learning is untested in the wiki. |
| A learner falls steadily behind from week 4 | Look at when they log in and what they skip; ask about their plan from week 1. | Revisit the if-then plan, offer a reduced route and an intermediate deadline. Falling behind fits competing work, a missing prerequisite and low self-regulation equally; ask before choosing. |
| Presence or satisfaction surveys are high but end-of-term performance is weak | Compare each learner's performance on the representative task with their participation and survey ratings. | Read survey ratings as experience, not learning; look for which weeks' content the weak performers missed or failed. In the wiki's surveys, presence ratings were high and say nothing about achievement. |

## Fitting the design to a situation

**The three facts that most change the decision:**

- **How much of a teacher's time there is per learner.** The weekly cycle above assumes a teacher who answers in 48–72 hours and posts in each group every week; at large enrolments without assistants that is not possible, and the design has to move feedback onto answers, peers and summaries. If unstated, ask: how many learners per teaching person, and how many hours a week does that person have for this course?
- **Whether learners can meet live at all.** Synchronous contact is where correction and the first-week contact are cheapest; some cohorts (shift workers, several time zones, phone-only) cannot meet. If unstated, ask: can every learner attend a 30-minute call at one time in the week, and on what device?
- **Who the learners are and why they enrolled.** Adults studying around work are most at risk in the first week and least able to follow a fixed weekly time; school-age learners need more structure and contact with an adult. If unstated, ask: who are they, what are they studying for, and what else is competing for their time?

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Online, blended and face to face.** [Blended learning improves outcomes](../claims/blended-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [~M]: a meta-analysis of studies from 1996–2008, mostly in higher education and professional training, found blended instruction ahead of face-to-face (23 contrasts, g+ = 0.35) and purely online instruction ahead by less (g+ = 0.14); the authors caution that the blended arms typically also had extra learning time and resources. One synthesis, `q3`, `r?`. Carry it to "an online course need not be worse than face to face", no further; it does not test any feature of this pattern. Against it, one instrumental-variables study (Bettinger et al., 2017, at a large for-profit university) found that [taking a course online lowered grades in that course and in later courses](../claims/online-course-lower-grades-current-and-future.md) [~M], [reduced progress in college](../claims/online-course-taking-reduces-college-success.md) [~M] and [reduced re-enrolment](../claims/online-course-taking-reduces-reenrollment.md) [~M], for [students who had both options](../claims/online-course-effects-local-to-dual-option-students.md) [~M] (`q3`, causal, no effect size on the claim pages). So an online course can cost learners against its in-person version; the wiki does not say which design features of the courses studied carried that cost, which is the gap this pattern's default is meant to address and does not close.

One synthesis; the claim page does not report which supports, which learners or which horizons.

The one claim here with an online-course outcome from a randomised design; it bears on persistence, not on learning.

The nearest thing to a prompt-design rule in the wiki, it bears on the quality of the talk, not on what learners can do afterwards.

- **The presences.** In two small online graduate programmes, students [rated all three presences above 4.0 of 5](../claims/mba-students-experience-courses-as-coi.md) [+W], rated [organisation highest and facilitation and resolution lowest](../claims/coi-presence-uneven-profile-mba.md) [+W], and [social presence was the presence most associated with self-reported metacognition](../claims/social-presence-strongest-metacognition-association-cbi.md) [~M]. ## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested course.* A ten-week online certificate module in project management for 60 working adults (`adult-workplace`, `intermediate`), one tutor with about six hours a week for the module, learners in two time zones, a third of them studying mainly on phones. The brief changed the default in four places. Because live sessions cannot reach everyone, week 1's contact is a written reply from the tutor to each introduction within 48 hours, and corrective feedback goes into written comments on the graded pieces. Because one tutor has sixty learners, the content strand has automatic self-checks, the tutor posts one weekly summary of common errors, and from week 4 learners work in ten groups of six with a rotating facilitator, the tutor posting once per group per week. Because the learners work, the full set of due dates is published in week 1 and each learner writes an if-then plan naming a work obstacle. Because of the phones, every content segment is under 15 minutes with a transcript. The groups build a project plan for a real client brief across weeks 4–9 and present it in week 10; each learner is assessed individually on a plan for their own workplace. A learner says she wants the certificate for promotion; the designer's objective is planning skill. They agree to report both the certificate pass and the individual plan's score. The tutor messages anyone who misses a week's deadline within two days.

Record: **learners, enrolment and staff time → structure and weekly cycle as run → week-1 contact (who replied, when) → if-then plans written → each learner's completion of content, self-checks and discussion by week → first missed week and the contact made → group products and who contributed → individual performance on the representative task at end of term → completion and the week each non-completer stopped → candidate interpretations (access, time, prerequisite, weak prompt, weak content strand) and their basis**. Preserve each learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of completion or mastery without a calibrated model.

## Elements and limits

[Feedback](../elements/feedback.md), [peer collaboration](../elements/peer-collaboration.md), [communication channels](../elements/communication-channels.md), [structured discussion](../elements/structured-discussion.md), [discussion prompts](../elements/discussion-prompt.md) and [web-forum peer feedback](../elements/web-forum-peer-feedback.md). Principles the pattern draws on: [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md), [Collaborative Learning](../principles/collaborative-learning.md), [Self-Regulated Learning](../principles/self-regulated-learning.md), [Social Presence](../principles/social-presence.md), [Digital Learning](../principles/digital-learning.md) and [User-Centered Design for Learning](../principles/user-centered-design-for-learning.md). Theory: [CoI framework: three presences and a metacognition lens](../theories/coi-framework-three-presences-metacognition-lens.md), and two descendants of Moore's three interactions, [the Interaction Equivalency Theorem](../theories/interaction-equivalency-theorem.md) (deep learning is supported when one of the three interactions is at a high level) and [Anderson's six modes of interaction](../theories/anderson-six-modes-interaction-online-education.md); Moore's transactional distance has no theory page of its own here.

Costs and limits the earlier page named, kept: online teaching can take markedly more instructor time than face-to-face teaching, especially around advising and assessment (Tomei, 2006); institutional commitment and planning are reported as strong predictors of online course quality (Shelton, 2011); the CoI framework's evidence leans on self-report and perception measures, which has drawn methodological criticism (Rourke & Kanuka, 2009); and a course can be well designed for the presences and still fail on a confusing platform. None of these has a claim page here.

This pattern is scoped to the shape of a mostly online course across a term. How to split a course between online and in-person time is [Blended Learning](blended-learning.md); how a designer produces a course (analysis, prototyping, review) is a design process, not this pattern. Whether a course built this way produces better end-of-term performance or completion than the same course without its week-1 contact, structured small-group discussion or self-regulation supports, for which learners and at what staff cost, remains to be tested.

## Description
Online learners routinely report a sense of psychological distance or isolation that face-to-face learners do not — Moore (1989) called this **transactional distance** and argued it is reduced by deliberately designing for three types of interaction: **learner-to-learner**, **learner-to-instructor**, and **learner-to-content**. A meta-analysis of 74 distance-education studies confirmed all three types matter for achievement (Bernard et al., 2009).

The **Community of Inquiry (CoI)** framework (Garrison, Anderson, & Archer, 1999) operationalizes this at the course level by defining a quality online learning experience as the overlap of three "presences": **cognitive presence** (learners constructing meaning through sustained discourse and reflection), **social presence** (learners feeling able to project themselves as real people and connect with the group), and **teaching presence** (design, facilitation, and direct instruction that organizes and guides the other two). This pattern is the course-design counterpart to [Community of Inquiry](../principles/community-of-inquiry.md) as a general collaborative-learning principle — here it is applied specifically to structuring an entire online course, not just a single discussion.

Course-design literature converges on a recurring set of moves that build these presences across a semester: a **consistent course structure** maintained throughout (Swan, 2001); **frequent, low-latency instructor feedback** — studies of instructor immediacy found learners in high-immediacy conditions showed greater learning gains and retention (Bodie & Michel, 2014); **well-designed collaborative assignments** that create real interdependence rather than parallel individual work; and **explicit, deliberate interaction design**, since transactional distance does not close on its own just because a discussion board exists.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A course structure maintained consistently across weeks/units so learners are not relearning navigation each module
- Assignment and communication design that deliberately builds each of the three interaction types (learner-learner, learner-instructor, learner-content), not just whichever is easiest to set up (e.g., posting content is easy; instructor feedback loops require ongoing effort)

## Design

### Sequence
1. Establish a consistent course structure (navigation, weekly rhythm, assignment format) and hold it constant across the term.
2. Design at least one recurring activity for each of Moore's three interaction types: learner-to-content (structured materials/activities), learner-to-instructor (feedback loops, office hours, announcements), and learner-to-learner (discussion, peer review, group work).
3. Build cognitive presence deliberately — discussion prompts and tasks should require synthesis and application, not just response-and-reply.
4. Establish social presence early (introductions, low-stakes interaction) before demanding higher-stakes collaboration.
5. Maintain teaching presence throughout via timely, specific feedback — immediacy affects learning gains, not just satisfaction.
