Emergent Curriculum¶

Pattern · All patterns
Evidence · 14 claims (10 for, 4 mixed) · 17 studies (5 review, 5 qualitative, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 causal, 2 theoretical), q1–q4 · 3 of 17 report an effect size · 11 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable policy for letting the topic of a stretch of teaching come from interests learners express, while the teacher keeps responsibility for what is learned through it: a planned entry point (a book, an object, a question, a set of materials) → the teacher observes and writes down what learners ask, say and keep returning to → a sustained interest is webbed, with learners or among the adults, to content and activities worth learning → the thread is followed with new materials, visitors and projects, with an adult guiding the activity toward the webbed goals → observation continues and the web is redrawn → the thread closes on a product, a display or a shared account of what was found, and the next entry point is offered. The planned unit is set aside rather than rejected, and the record of observations doubles as a running assessment of what learners are learning.

The intended change is in the depth of engagement and in learner-initiated questioning and problem-solving (motivation-affect, persistence), and, where the web names target ideas and an adult guides toward them, in understanding of those ideas (conceptual-understanding). The approach's track record, on the earlier page, is concentrated in early childhood and the first years of school (child, pre-k-12); with older students the same move is made by turning a personal interest into a research question that is then studied formally.

No claim in this wiki tests emergent curriculum as such, against a planned curriculum or anything else. What the wiki holds is: two single-classroom or two-student qualitative cases in which departing from or extending beyond a designed task went with deeper self-directed inquiry; one qualitative co-design study on tools for balancing student interests with standards; a meta-analysis of guided play in early childhood; a meta-analysis and two other studies showing that unguided discovery learns less than explicit instruction for novices; one randomised study of personalising problem contexts to students' interests; general reviews on task value and autonomy support; and a single case where an open, partner-led project narrowed to its easiest meaning. Each is carried to this pattern by extrapolation, and the page says how far. The sequence is the earlier page's, with each step's evidence status stated; the response-dependent branches are untested design proposals.

How it sits beside converted pages: the Free-Choice Learning Environment Design principle owns settings with no imposed curriculum at all (a museum floor, an ungraded studio block); this pattern is the case where a teacher still owns a curriculum and lets learners' interests choose the route through it. The Autonomy principle holds bounded choice and the case against controlling incentives; the Inquiry-based Learning principle and the Problem-Based Learning pattern hold how much guidance an investigation needs and when. This page applies those models to a topic that learners chose and does not repeat them.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Age or stage (child, adolescent, adult); what the learners already know of the likely topics and of the moves the thread will need (asking a question, finding a source, making a model); what they have been seen to choose and return to in the last week or two. Preserve unknowns rather than assuming the class shares one interest.
What counts as an expressed interest Who raised it, how many learners took it up, whether they came back to it unprompted on another day, and what exactly they asked. A passing remark and a question several learners pursue for days are different observations.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-instructor-led, online-self-paced, workplace-clinical), how many adults are present, how much timetable is free to follow a thread (single-session, days-weeks, term-plus), what materials, visitors and outings can be arranged at a few days' notice.
Required content Which standards, outcomes or programme goals must still be covered this term or year, and who checks. This sets how far a thread may wander and what the web must connect to.
Learner-valued goal What the learners want to find out, make or do with the topic (build a model, help the birds, make a game that others play). Keep it apart from the designer objective and record where they diverge.
Designer objective Which capability the thread is meant to build: concepts in the topic's domain (concept, principle), inquiry or research moves (complex-skill), literacy or numeracy practised through the topic, or interest itself (attitude-motivation). Name the one that will be observed.
Outcome What will be recorded as evidence of learning: the teacher's dated notes, learners' products and explanations, a closing account, a check on the required content; and when (end of thread, end of term).

A request to "use an emergent curriculum" does not specify these. Ask who decides when an interest is real, what must still be covered, and how much time a thread may take before choosing the entry point or the web.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

The default sequence is the earlier page's five steps, with the closing step and the doses added. Steps 1, 2 and 5 are untested; steps 3 and 4 have neighbouring claims, carried by extrapolation; step 6 is a proposal.

  1. Offer a planned entry point, held loosely. Start from a planned activity or topic (a read-aloud, a set of materials, an object, a visit) chosen because it has several directions a question could go. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. IDC's interest cycle [+W] (a theoretical commentary) argues that interests should be treated as launching points for deeper immersion, not as fixed topics.
  2. Observe and record continuously. During ordinary activity, write down learners' questions, comments and what they return to, with the date and who said it. Proposed dose: a few lines per session, and a 10–15 minute review of the notes at the end of each day or week by the adults together. An interest is "sustained" when several learners return to it unprompted on at least two separate days (proposal). Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
  3. Web the interest to content worth learning. Brainstorm connections between the learners' questions and possible content, activities, materials, visitors and outings: with the learners (10–20 minutes for young children, proposal), by the teacher alone, or with co-teachers and assistants. Mark on the web the target ideas the thread is for, and which required standards or outcomes each branch reaches. Before launching a larger thread, try the opening activity on colleagues "in student hat" to see whether it raises questions that lead to the target ideas or into dead ends. Evidence status: co-design tools for standards analysis, student-interest data and launch rehearsal [+M]: participants in one two-year co-design partnership (teachers designing secondary science units) credited unpacking standards, interest data and rehearsal with balancing the two goals; self-report, no outcome measure, and not an emergent curriculum.
  4. Set the plan aside and follow the thread, with an adult guiding. Bring in books, materials, a visitor or a project that serves the learners' question; let learners propose solutions and lead discussion; the adult asks questions, models, supplies tools and keeps the activity aimed at the webbed ideas. Evidence status: Spontaneous responsiveness to real-world events in PBL [+M] (one bilingual third-grade classroom: more self-initiated questioning and problem-solving when the teacher left the written unit to respond to an unplanned event than in a designed lesson; a single case) and choice-rich infrastructure [+M] (two sixth-graders extending one challenge into a year-long project; an extreme case chosen for its length). On guidance: guided play [~M] did better than free play or direct instruction on some early-childhood outcomes and no differently on most; unassisted discovery [~S] learned less than explicit instruction, while discovery with feedback, examples or scaffolding did better.
  5. Keep observing and redraw the web as the topic evolves, treating the notes and products as ongoing assessment of what each learner is learning. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
  6. Close the thread when interest has fallen in the notes for two or three sessions running, or the webbed ideas have been reached, or the product is finished: learners make, show or tell what they found (a model, a mural, a display, a short talk to another class or to families), the teacher records which webbed ideas and standards each learner showed, and a new entry point is offered. Evidence status: untested proposal.

Dose and progression (proposals; no claim gives them).

Thread length One to two weeks Two to six weeks, as long as the notes show learners returning to it

When to move on Interest falls for two or three sessions, the target ideas are reached, or the product is shared The same, plus learners can say what they want to find out next

For older students the earlier page's route is the default: two or three short sessions in which students bring personally relevant problems or experiences, discuss them with classmates and formulate their own research questions, followed by several weeks of formal, systematic study of one question with a stated method, and a seminar or presentation at the end (from the earlier page; untested here).

Several learners ask about something unplanned during the entry activity Put a related book or material out the next day without comment; see who returns to it and what they ask. If several return and ask further questions, web it. If they do not, record it as a passing interest and continue the plan. Neither branch is tested.
The thread is lively but a few vocal learners are driving it; others drift Look at the notes by learner: who has spoken, made or asked anything in the last few sessions? Ask a quieter learner privately what they would like to try. Open a second branch of the web, or a parallel role (drawing, building, recording), so each learner has an entry point. Do not read whole-class enthusiasm as each learner's interest.
Activity continues, but it has narrowed to making, collecting or a single easy goal (money, decoration) and the webbed ideas are not appearing Ask learners to explain why something happens or why they chose it; check the notes for any question about the target idea. Add a conceptual frame: a question that requires the idea, a visitor who brings it, a comparison. Community projects without conceptual framing [~W] (one school, one course) describes this narrowing and its partial repair through facilitated reflection.

If they lack the knowledge or moves to go further, guide directly (model, show, explain), then hand back. If they are content and the thread has served its purpose, close it. Proposed; the guidance evidence is from discovery and play studies, not emergent curricula.
Older students name a topic but no question they could investigate Ask what they would need to find out, and how they would know. Support question formulation before any research starts; if the question cannot be studied in the time, narrow it with them. Untested.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • How much the learners already know of the topic and of the moves the thread needs. A thread that leaves learners to discover essential content unaided learns less than explicit teaching; guided forms do better. If the brief does not say, ask: given the likely topic, can these learners ask a question about it, find something out and explain it, or will an adult have to show them how?
  • How much required content must be covered, and who checks. The approach gives up the guarantee that a set list is covered. If the brief does not say, ask: which outcomes must every learner show by the end of the term, and how much of the timetable is free?
  • How many adults are present and how much of their time is free. Continuous observation and webbing are adult work. If the brief does not say, ask: who will watch, write the notes and review them, and for how long each day?

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Departing from the plan to follow an unplanned event. Spontaneous responsiveness to real-world events in PBL can deepen student-directed inquiry beyond what designed curriculum achieves [+M]: one bilingual third-grade classroom of 20 students, studied through 192 hours of observation, interviews and artefacts (qualitative, q2, no effect size). When the teacher paused a written PBL unit on birds to respond to a spring snowstorm, students generated their own solutions, led the discussion, consulted an outside source on their own initiative and showed concern for the birds; in a designed lesson on the same topic engagement stayed within the script. The authors attribute the move to the teacher's expertise and autonomy, and the first author co-wrote the curriculum. This is the closest the wiki comes to the pattern's own relationship, and it is one case: it shows what a flexible curriculum and a willing, experienced teacher made possible once, not how often.
  • Extending beyond a designed task. In one extreme case, two sixth-graders with a choice-rich, ungraded STEAM infrastructure extended a game-design challenge into a year-long self-directed project well beyond its designed scope [+M]: two sixth-grade students in an ungraded in-school studio spent about 22.5 hours across 34 sessions extending one game-design challenge into two original games, resolving 23 technical problems, with problems becoming less frequent and quicker to resolve after midyear. An extreme case selected for its length; most students in the same studios did not do this, and there is no comparison group.
  • Guidance inside child-led activity. Guided play outperforms direct instruction or free play on a few early-childhood outcomes, with no reliable difference on most others, in one meta-analysis [~M]: a meta-analysis of 39 early-childhood intervention studies (17 pooled, N=3893) found guided play ahead of direct instruction on early maths (g=0.24), shape knowledge (g=0.63) and task switching (g=0.40), and ahead of free play on spatial vocabulary (g=0.93, with high heterogeneity), with no reliable differences on most other outcomes. It tests adult guidance of play, not interest-chosen topics; it is the strongest reason for step 4's adult in the activity.
  • The limit: unguided discovery. Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [~S]: its table gathers 5 distinct studies; in one meta-analysis explicit instruction outperformed unassisted discovery across 580 comparisons (d = 0.38 favouring explicit instruction), and discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations outperformed other instruction across 360 comparisons; with 112 third- and fourth-graders, many more learned the control-of-variables procedure from direct instruction. An emergent thread that leaves the content to be found unaided is in the condition these studies say fails.
  • The limit: a thread that narrows. Community projects need conceptual framing to avoid narrowing the learning object [~W]: one Finnish middle school's entrepreneurship course with outside partners, 11 students in the focal year (q1): without a disciplinary frame the activity converged on "money-making" and left its intended critical questions unexamined, until a facilitated cross-group discussion raised another framing. A caution for any open thread, from one site.
  • Balancing interests and standards. Co-design tools for standards analysis, student-interest data, and launch rehearsal support balancing standards alignment with student interests [+M]: 27 participants (19 teachers) in a two-year co-design partnership credited unpacking standards, writing target explanations, disaggregated interest surveys and rehearsing launches "in student hat"; the wording of one survey item changed which demographic groups rated a phenomenon highly, and the team concluded that surveys should be supplemented with focus groups or interviews. Self-report about designing units; no learner outcome.
  • Interest as context rather than topic. Personalization to student interests improved accuracy on hard knowledge components involving algebraic expression-writing within the tutoring unit [+M]: a randomised experiment with 145 ninth-grade Algebra I students in an intelligent tutor; students given problems set in their stated interests were more accurate on the hard expression-writing steps within one unit (odds = 1.53). The only causal test in the wiki of adapting instruction to learners' interests, and it keeps the content fixed; it supports interest-based contexts, not emergent topics.
  • Why interest should help. Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals [+M]: its table gathers 4 distinct studies, three reviews and one associational, none with an effect size coded; Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement, and persistence in learning [+M]: 8 distinct studies, mainly reviews and theory, with one meta-analysis on rewards. Both are general mechanisms, not tests of letting learners choose the topic.

Do not rank emergent curriculum against a planned curriculum from these claims: none compares them, none measures coverage of required content, and none follows learners past the thread. Do not read a lively class, or a few learners' sustained projects, as the class having learned the webbed ideas. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (an interest-led thread with adult guidance against a planned unit on the same content, on engagement and on the target ideas, at the end of the thread and later) and the local observations that would stand in for it: the dated notes by learner and each learner's explanation of the target ideas at the close.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does seeing a task as interesting, important or useful raise the effort learners invest in it? Learners invest more effort in tasks they perceive as interesting, personally important, or useful to their future goals [+M]
  • Should novices be left to discover the target content without support? Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction [~S]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims bearing on the pattern that are not used as core evidence above, each with what it is and how far it bears.

  • Rather than the initial linear theory of action, a cycle of learning emerged generated by flexibility, relationships, and responsiveness [+W] — cited by the earlier page. A report on the first year of a teen library learning space (130 hours of observation, interviews with 20 teens and 15 staff), describing learning driven by staff flexibility and responsiveness. Out of school and voluntary, so nearer the free-choice principle than to a teacher-run curriculum; descriptive, no outcome.
  • IDC's interest cycle requires educators to treat interests as launching points for deeper immersion, not static topics [+W] — a theoretical commentary; used above for step 1's framing only.
  • Autonomy, meaningful purpose, and interest improve intrinsic motivation and deeper processing [+W] — a practitioner synthesis (review · r1); a general statement, not a test of interest-led topics.
  • In a small subgroup of struggling students (n = 25), interest personalization greatly improved expression-writing accuracy within the tutoring unit [+W] — the same randomised study as the personalisation claim above; a subgroup of 25, so read as a lead about lower-achieving learners, not a finding.
  • Low-income US schools focus most on testing and have the fewest resources for interest-driven activities [~W] — a theoretical commentary on equity; used above as context for the low-resourced row only.

The Constructivism theory page, which the earlier page cited as supporting theory, is the framework the approach draws on; it is not a claim and is not counted as evidence here.

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested lesson. A kindergarten class of 22 with a teacher and an assistant has a planned autumn unit on leaves. During a walk, several children stop at puddles and ask why some leaves float and others sink; the assistant's notes show five children still talking about it the next morning and three bringing objects from home. The adults web it in a 15-minute after-school meeting: floating and sinking, materials, measuring, counting and recording results, and two early science outcomes and one numeracy outcome the school requires. With the children they draw a picture web the next day ("what floats? what sinks? can we make a sinker float?"). For two weeks, 40 minutes a day, the children test objects in water tubs; an adult sits at the tubs each session, asks children to predict before they drop something, shows them how to sort results on a two-column chart, and introduces the word "material". A parent who builds boats visits in the second week. Interest falls in the notes on days nine and ten; the thread closes with the children showing families their chart and a foil boat that carries coins. One child's family prefers that the class keep to the leaves unit; the teacher records this and finishes the unit's required leaf-sorting activity in two short sessions. The guided-play and guidance claims cover adults guiding young children's activity, not this choice of topic; whether the children learned more than the leaves unit would have taught is not something this design can show.

Record: entry point → dated notes of questions and returns, by child → the decision to web (who, how many, how many days) → the web, with target ideas and required outcomes marked → materials, visitors and guidance given, by session → each child's predictions, sorting and explanations → candidate interpretations of any child not taking part (no interest, no entry point, cannot yet do the moves) and their basis → the closing product and audience → required outcomes reached, by child → what children said they want to find out next. Preserve the children's own questions in their words and any change in them. An agent may summarise this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of learning without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Observation, learner choice, question formulation, public display, and the strategies guided play, free play, interest inventories and co-design tools for standards and interest balancing.

Limits: no claim in this wiki compares an emergent curriculum with a planned one, measures coverage of required content under it, or follows learners beyond a thread; the two cases nearest the pattern are single qualitative studies, one chosen as an extreme case; the guidance evidence comes from play and discovery studies; and the only causal test of adapting to interests keeps the content fixed. The record above is how a setting would gather its own evidence.

Related Patterns¶

  • Socratic Seminar — the Hawkins (2006) high-school example channels emergent, student-generated interest into the kind of structured discussion this pattern describes
  • Problem-Based Learning — its conditional model of guidance for novices applies inside an emergent thread
  • Scripted Personally Meaningful Inquiry — a structured sequence in which students frame a personally meaningful question and investigate it, close to the older-student route here
  • Free-Choice Learning Environment Design (principle) — settings with no imposed curriculum at all
