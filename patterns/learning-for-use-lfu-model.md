# Learning-for-Use (LfU) Model

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 16 claims (8 for, 8 mixed) · 26 studies (7 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 6 review, 3 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative, 1 design), `q1`–`q4` · 6 of 26 report an effect size · 11 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable policy for a unit of inquiry in which **learners first meet a task they cannot yet do and that makes the target knowledge necessary → they build the knowledge through guided investigation, with explicit instruction that connects their findings to the canonical idea → they use the knowledge in a consequential task that demands it → they revisit it on a new case later**. Edelson (2001) named the three phases motivate, construct and refine. The intended change is in `conceptual-understanding` and in using the idea on a case not yet seen (`near-transfer`, `far-transfer`), not in recall of facts or speed with a procedure. The goal is usually a `concept` or `principle` inside a `complex-skill`, at unit grain over `days-weeks`.

**No claim in this wiki tests Learning-for-Use as such**: no recorded study compares a motivate-construct-refine unit with the same content taught another way, and no claim page names the model. What the wiki does hold are claims about each phase, carried here by extrapolation:

- **Motivate (meet the task before the idea).** Letting learners attempt problems the concept solves before it is taught, then teaching it from their attempts, improved conceptual knowledge and transfer and not procedural skill, with the effect reversed for young children and for domain-general skills ([productive failure](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+S]). That is the nearest tested relative of "create a need to know", but it measures learning, not the motivation the phase is named for. No claim recorded here measures whether a need-to-know opening raises interest; two descriptive reports from a storyline curriculum and one teacher's account are the closest.
- **Construct (guided investigation).** Guided inquiry in school science is associated with better achievement than the usual instruction, and teacher-led inquiry with larger effects than student-led ([inquiry-based teaching](../claims/inquiry-based-teaching-improves-science-achievement.md) [+S]); unassisted discovery learns less than explicit instruction for `novice` learners ([minimal guidance](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S]).
- **Refine (use in a consequential task).** That practising whole tasks improves transfer is a training-design argument that no study recorded here tests ([whole-task practice](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+W]), and for absolute novices with high-element-interactivity tasks, practising parts first reduced load ([part-task practice](../claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices.md) [~M]).

So the sequence below is the earlier page's, with each step's evidence status stated; the response-dependent branches are **untested design proposals**.

**How the converted siblings sit beside it.** [Problem-based learning](problem-based-learning.md) is the more general pattern for problem-centred units and owns how much guidance novices get and whether a problem-first phase comes before instruction; LfU applies those decisions and adds a fixed arc (need, construction, use, revisit) whose last phase must require the knowledge built. [Anchored instruction](anchored-instruction.md) owns building one shared scenario; an LfU unit may use an anchor as its motivating task. [4C/ID](4cid-four-component-instructional-design.md) owns whole-task training for complex skills, which is the nearest relative of the refine phase. The principles it draws on, [inquiry-based learning](../principles/inquiry-based-learning.md), [productive failure](../principles/productive-failure.md) (teach first or attempt first), [scaffolding and fading](../principles/scaffolding-and-fading.md) and [transfer of learning](../principles/transfer-of-learning.md), each own their relationship; this page does not repeat their models.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Before the unit, give each learner the motivating task, or a small version of it, and ask what they would do and what they would need to know. Record whether they can begin at all, what ideas they bring (including wrong ones), and whether they can generate more than one approach. Preserve unknowns rather than labelling learners `novice`. Record age (`child`, `adolescent`, `adult`) and setting (`pre-k-12`, `postsecondary`, `adult-workplace`). |
| The target knowledge | Whether it is a `concept` or `principle` that a task can make necessary, a `procedure` or domain-general skill (for which teaching first did better), or facts to recall. How high its element interactivity is in the whole task. |
| The motivating and consequential tasks | What task creates the need, and what later task requires using the knowledge (a decision, a design, an explanation, a product for someone). Whether the second task can be done without the target knowledge: if it can, it does not refine it. |
| Context and activities | `classroom`, `online-self-paced`, `online-instructor-led`, `workplace-clinical` or mixed; `single-session` or `days-weeks`; group size; how much teacher or facilitator time there is to check in; which tools, data or simulations are available for investigation. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask what the learner wants from the unit (to solve the local problem, to pass the course, to be able to do the job). Record where it differs from the designer objective; the motivating task works only if learners see it as theirs. |
| Designer objective and outcome | Which outcome the assessment weighs: conceptual explanation, use on a new case (`near-transfer`, `far-transfer`), procedure fluency, or factual recall; immediate or `delayed`. Name the one the unit is judged by, since the phase evidence differs by outcome. |

A request to "design an LfU unit on X" does not specify these. Ask what task makes X necessary, what later task requires it, who the learners are and how the unit will be judged before choosing the problem or the inquiry.

## Sequence and conditional policy

The default sequence is the earlier page's four steps, with doses and progression added. Steps 1 and 4 have no claim that tests them as specified; steps 2 and 3 carry neighbouring claims by extrapolation. Doses are **untested proposals** unless a claim gives them.

1. **Motivate: meet the task first (one session; 20–40 minutes of a first lesson).** Present the motivating task (a local problem, a design challenge, a decision) and let learners attempt it briefly, alone or in pairs, before any teaching. Ask each to write what they tried and what they would need to know; collect the questions publicly. Evidence status: attempting problems before instruction improved conceptual knowledge and transfer when instruction followed and built on the attempts ([productive failure](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+S]; [invention tasks](../claims/invention-tasks-prepare-future-learning.md) [+M] found the benefit only when a learning resource was available later). The "need to know" as a motivational mechanism is untested; a teacher's account that opening on learners' existing ideas stimulates interest is one author's experience ([inquiry first](../claims/inquiry-first-stimulates-interest-and-organizes-course.md) [+W]).
2. **Construct: guided investigation with explicit consolidation (3–8 sessions of 40–60 minutes, proposed).** Learners investigate with data, a simulation, readings or experiments chosen so the target idea is what explains the results. Guidance is planned, not optional: a driving question per session, prompts, a structured procedure, and feedback. Each session ends with a teacher-led synthesis (10–15 minutes) that compares learners' findings and attempts with the canonical idea and states it. A reading placed after an investigation where results disagree can serve the same need ([reading with a need to know](../strategies/reading-with-a-need-to-know.md), a strategy page, untested). Evidence status: guided inquiry raises science achievement and teacher-led inquiry more than student-led ([inquiry-based teaching](../claims/inquiry-based-teaching-improves-science-achievement.md) [+S]); guidance improved post-inquiry learning outcomes ([guidance in inquiry](../claims/guidance-improves-inquiry-learning-outcomes.md) [+M]); attempts help only when instruction builds on learners' solutions or uses contrasting cases ([PS-I design features](../claims/ps-i-fosters-learning-only-with-contrasting-cases-or-student-solutions.md) [~M]).
3. **Refine: use the knowledge in a consequential task (2–4 sessions, proposed).** Learners apply what they built to the motivating task, now in full, or to a new task that cannot be done without the idea (a recommendation to a real audience, a design that must work, an explanation of a new case). Prompt them to explain why their solution follows from the idea. Evidence status: prompted self-explanation improved understanding and problem solving on immediate tests ([self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]); whole-task practice for transfer is an untested argument ([whole-task practice](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+W]).
4. **Revisit on a new case (one 20–30 minute task, 1–2 weeks after the unit, proposed).** Give a case with different surface features and ask learners to use and explain the idea without the unit's materials. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. It doubles as the unit's delayed outcome.

**Support and progression across sessions (proposals).** In the first unit of a course, the designer supplies the motivating task, the investigation procedure and the data, and the teacher leads every synthesis. By the second or third LfU unit, learners pose some of the sub-questions, choose among two or three investigation routes and lead part of the synthesis; the canonical statement is still checked by the teacher. Prompts in the refine phase move from sentence starters to a single "why does this follow?" question. Contingent support, raised when a learner fails and lowered when they succeed, is the form the evidence favours, but almost all of it comes from one-to-one tutoring ([contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]).

**When a learner moves on (proposals).** From construct to refine: the learner can explain the target idea in their own words and use it correctly on two of three short new cases at the end of a synthesis. From refine to the next unit: the consequential task uses the idea, and the learner can say why. A learner who meets neither after the planned sessions gets explicit teaching of the idea with a worked example, not another round of open investigation.

| Observation | Discriminating probe | Proposed action, with its uncertainty |
|---|---|---|

The productive-failure evidence assumes learners generate solutions; not tested for this case. |
| Interest is high at the start and fades over the unit | Ask privately what they are now trying to find out and why. | If learners cannot connect the day's activity to the task, open each session by restating the driving question and what is still unknown; shorten the construct phase. Teachers in a storyline curriculum reported this fading ([OpenSciEd enactment](../claims/openscied-enactment-challenges-fatigue-pacing-pseudoagency.md) [~M]); the remedy is untested. |
| Learners finish the investigation with findings but no idea ("the water near the farm had more nitrates") | Ask them to predict a new case from their finding. | If they cannot, the synthesis has not connected findings to the idea: state it, and compare their findings with it. Unassisted discovery of the target idea is the condition where learning drops ([minimal guidance](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S]). |
| The refine task is done well without using the idea | Ask which part of their solution depended on the idea. | If none did, the task does not require the knowledge: change the task, not the learners. Untested proposal. |
| The class "agrees" on the idea but individuals cannot use it | Collect individual written explanations before discussion; score individual use on the revisit case. | If individuals fail, add individual practice with feedback before the refine task; do not read group agreement as each learner's understanding. Untested proposal. |
| Learners have no idea what to investigate next | Ask what question the last result raised. | Supply the next question, and say it is supplied; nearly half of students in one curriculum's field test had no ideas about what to investigate next ([OpenSciEd enactment](../claims/openscied-enactment-challenges-fatigue-pacing-pseudoagency.md) [~M]). The same report describes pseudoagency: students seeing a teacher's attempt to open sensemaking as disingenuous, so a supplied question is better named as supplied. |

## Fitting the design to a situation

**The three facts that most change the decision:**

- **Whether learners know enough to attempt the motivating task.** The problem-first advantage depends on learners generating solutions, reversed for second to fifth graders, and unassisted discovery learns less than explicit instruction for novices. If unstated, ask: given the task cold, can they describe at least one way to start?
- **Whether the target is a concept or principle, or a procedure or fact set.** Attempting first helped conceptual knowledge and transfer, not procedural knowledge; for domain-general skills teaching first did better; problem-based formats may cost basic knowledge. If unstated, ask: what will the final assessment ask learners to do?
- **How much time there is, and whether it runs over days.** The full arc needs several sessions, and in one meta-regression the benefit of a faithful problem-first design tended to shrink for interventions spanning only a few hours ([time span](../claims/psi-fidelity-time-span-interaction.md) [~M], a marginal interaction). If unstated, ask: how many sessions, of what length, and over how many weeks?

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Attempt first, then teach from the attempts.** [Productive Failure Improves Conceptual Learning](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [+S]: across 53 studies and 166 comparisons, problem solving followed by instruction beat instruction followed by problem solving on conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36) with no difference on procedural knowledge (g = −0.03); larger with higher fidelity to productive-failure design, and reversed for second to fifth graders and for domain-general skills. The claim's table gathers 8 distinct studies. [Invention tasks](../claims/invention-tasks-prepare-future-learning.md) [+M]: ninth-graders who tried to invent a statistical procedure before instruction transferred better than tell-and-practice students only when a worked-example resource was present at test (two classroom experiments, n = 95 and 102). [PS-I design features](../claims/ps-i-fosters-learning-only-with-contrasting-cases-or-student-solutions.md) [~M]: a review concludes the benefit appears only with contrasting cases or instruction built on student solutions. [Grade level](../claims/psi-effect-increases-with-grade-level.md) [~M]: the pooled effect was negative for second to fifth graders (g = −0.09) and largest for postgraduates and professionals (g = 1.03). Carry these to LfU's motivate phase as "an attempt at the task before teaching can prepare learners to learn from the teaching, if the teaching uses the attempts", not as evidence that a motivating problem raises interest.
- **Guide the investigation.** [Inquiry Based Teaching Improves Science Achievement](../claims/inquiry-based-teaching-improves-science-achievement.md) [+S]: a meta-analysis of 37 studies found g = .50 for inquiry-based science teaching, with teacher-led inquiry about .40 larger than student-led, and a synthesis of 138 K–12 studies found a positive trend for conceptual understanding without a pooled effect; the claim's table gathers 8 distinct studies, most quasi-experimental. [Guidance in inquiry](../claims/guidance-improves-inquiry-learning-outcomes.md) [+M]: one meta-analysis, d = 0.50 on post-inquiry learning outcomes. [Minimal guidance](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [~S]: unassisted discovery learned less than explicit instruction (580 comparisons, d = 0.38 favouring explicit instruction), while enhanced discovery beat other instruction (d = 0.30); 5 distinct studies in its table. This is the boundary of the construct phase: an LfU unit with driving questions, prompts and teacher-led synthesis is a guided form; the same unit left to "explore" is not.
- **Make the applied phase generative.** [Self-explanation](../claims/self-explanation-improves-conceptual-understanding.md) [+S]: prompting learners to explain improved understanding and problem solving by a moderate average amount (g = .55 across 69 effect sizes), on immediate tests, with little evidence on delayed or classroom gains; 9 distinct studies in its table. It supports a "why does this follow?" prompt in the refine task, not the refine task itself.
- **Whole task or parts.** [Whole-task practice](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+W] is a training-design argument (van Merriënboer, Kester & Paas 2006); its table gathers 5 distinct studies (3 theoretical, 2 causal), none with an effect size coded. [Part-task practice](../claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices.md) [~M]: for novices with high-element-interactivity material, isolated parts first helped (one experiment). Use the consequential task whole for learners who can manage it, and isolate parts first for absolute novices.
- **Contingent support.** [Contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]: fully contingent one-to-one tutoring of long division beat fixed or no support at immediate and one-month tests (8 per condition), and interactive tutoring helped transfer; a meta-analysis found explicit strategy training beat scaffolding. Its table gathers 25 distinct studies across many narrower claims. Mostly one-to-one; a qualification for class-wide inquiry.
- **What the storyline curriculum reports.** [OpenSciEd relevance and coherence](../claims/openscied-student-relevance-coherence.md) [+M]: in field tests of a storyline science curriculum that opens units on an anchoring phenomenon, more than 90% of students reported instruction relevant and 87% said they understood how the lesson tied to the bigger picture (descriptive survey percentages from one white paper, no comparison). [OpenSciEd enactment](../claims/openscied-enactment-challenges-fatigue-pacing-pseudoagency.md) [~M]: teachers reported declining interest in the anchoring phenomenon, 20% of those interviewed struggled with unit length and pacing, and nearly half of students had no ideas about what to investigate next. Neither claim page calls the curriculum LfU.

Do not rank LfU against direct instruction or other inquiry designs from these claims: none compares them, and none measures the time the arc costs. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (a motivate-construct-refine unit against the same content and consequential task taught explanation-first, on use of the idea in a new case at a delay) and the local observation that would stand in for it: individual use and explanation on the revisit case.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested unit.* A grade 8 science class (`adolescent`, `classroom`, `days-weeks`) of 28 learners has three weeks, four 50-minute lessons a week, for a unit on water quality. The town has asked residents whether a stream is safe for children to paddle in. **Motivate (lesson 1):** pairs get last year's readings for three sites and are asked to give an answer in 15 minutes; each writes what they would need to know; the class lists 20 questions. **Construct (lessons 2–8):** each lesson opens on one listed question; groups test samples or work with the town's data using a structured sheet, and the teacher closes each lesson with a 12-minute synthesis comparing groups' results and stating the idea (dissolved oxygen, nitrate sources, indicator species). A reading on nitrate runoff comes after two groups' results disagree. At lesson 8, learners explain the idea and predict two new sites; those who cannot get a worked example and a re-teach. **Refine (lessons 9–11):** groups write a recommendation to the town council and must explain which reading supports it and why. **Revisit (two weeks later, 25 minutes):** each learner judges a different river from new data, alone, without the unit's sheets. A learner says she wants to know whether her younger brother can paddle; the designer's objective is explaining the idea on a new case. They agree to score the recommendation and the individual revisit separately.

The table changed the default in three places: the vocabulary of the readings was taught before lesson 1 because the starting attempts showed learners could not read the data table; the synthesis was kept every lesson because these were `novice` learners; and the unit was held to three weeks because of the reported fading of interest in long units. These are local choices to observe, not settled ones.

Record: **motivating task and what learners attempted, with their questions → each session's driving question, investigation and guidance given → what each synthesis stated and which attempts it used → individual explanation and prediction at the construct-to-refine check → the consequential task and whether the idea was used, and why → individual use on the revisit case at the stated delay → candidate interpretations (idea built, task done without the idea, group agreement only, could not read the data) and their basis**. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of understanding without a calibrated model.

## Elements and limits

[Problem presentation](../elements/problem-presentation.md), [scaffolded inquiry](../elements/scaffolded-inquiry.md), [invention](../elements/invention.md), [scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md), [self-explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md), [application](../elements/application.md), [transfer tasks](../elements/transfer-tasks.md), [part-task practice](../elements/part-task-practice.md), [practice](../elements/practice.md) and [reflection](../elements/reflection.md). Principles the pattern draws on: [Problem-based Learning](../principles/problem-based-learning.md), [Inquiry-based Learning](../principles/inquiry-based-learning.md), [Productive Failure](../principles/productive-failure.md), [Scaffolding and Fading](../principles/scaffolding-and-fading.md), [Transfer of Learning](../principles/transfer-of-learning.md), [Authentic Audiences & Purposes](../principles/authentic-audiences-purposes.md) and [Real-world Math](../principles/real-world-math.md).

This pattern is scoped to units in which a task can make a concept or principle necessary and a later task can require it. Procedures and domain-general skills, fact sets, and very young learners are better served by teaching first ([direct instruction](direct-instruction.md)). The policy supports observation and design reasoning; whether a motivate-construct-refine unit produces better use of an idea on new cases than the same content taught explanation-first, for which learners, at what time cost and at what delay, remains to be tested.

## Description
Learning-for-Use is a pattern for designing inquiry so that knowledge is learned in response to a meaningful need and then refined through application. The model typically moves through three broad phases: motivate the need for new knowledge, support knowledge construction, and then refine that knowledge through use in a consequential task. The central idea is that knowledge becomes more retrievable and transferable when learners acquire it for a purpose rather than as isolated content.

LfU is especially useful when designers want inquiry to remain conceptually grounded instead of becoming a loose research activity. It provides a way to connect motivation, construction, and application inside one coherent sequence.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A meaningful knowledge need**: Learners should encounter a problem or task that creates demand for understanding.
- **Inquiry with conceptual purpose**: Exploration should be tied to building usable knowledge, not just information collection.
- **An application phase**: Learners need to use what they built in a consequential context.
- **Instructional guidance**: Teachers need to help learners connect experience, concept formation, and later application.
#### Constraints
- **Artificial motivation weakens the pattern**: If the initial problem does not genuinely create a need to know, the sequence feels staged.
- **Inquiry drift**: Exploration can become diffuse without conceptual checkpoints.
- **Novice support needs**: Some learners need more structure before inquiry becomes productive.
- **Time intensity**: The full motivate-construct-refine cycle takes more time than straightforward exposition.
#### Grain Size
- Lesson
- Unit

### Target Goals
- **Usable conceptual knowledge**: Learning ideas in ways that support later retrieval and application.
- **Transfer**: Connecting understanding to meaningful action beyond the first lesson.
- **Problem-centered inquiry**: Organizing learning around a reason to know.

### Target Learners
- **Learners in science, engineering, and applied domains**: Strong fit where concepts are tightly tied to real tasks.
- **Learners who need relevance to sustain inquiry**: Useful when motivation grows from meaningful problems.
- **Groups working through inquiry with application**: Best when exploration leads into use, not only discussion.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Situated learning perspectives — knowledge is more usable when learned in meaningful contexts.
- Constructivist perspectives — learners build understanding through active inquiry and application.
- Transfer-oriented perspectives — application after construction helps stabilize and retrieve knowledge later.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Motivation should create a need for knowledge, not distract from the concept itself.
- Some learners need more explicit instruction within the inquiry sequence than the model may initially suggest.

## Design

### Sequence
1. Motivate learning by presenting a meaningful problem, challenge, or need to know.
2. Support knowledge construction through inquiry, exploration, and sense-making.
3. Refine knowledge through application in a consequential task or context.
4. Revisit what was learned to strengthen transfer and future use.
