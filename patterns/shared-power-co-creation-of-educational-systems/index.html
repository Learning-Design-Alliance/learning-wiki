Shared power: previously excluded students, families, and communities co-create and shape educational systems, policies, and practices¶

Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 28 claims (16 for, 11 mixed, 1 against) · 21 studies (13 qualitative, 4 design, 1 causal, 1 review, 1 associational, 1 theoretical), q1–q2 · 0 of 21 report an effect size · 28 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable policy for giving students, families and communities who are present in an educational decision process, but cannot use its decision-making machinery, an actual share of the decisions: document where the group drops out of deciding → set up a body (or seats) with written decision standing → redesign the meeting's tools (language, agenda, pacing, ground rules) around the group → run a cycle in which staff support is handed over to the group → report back what was done with every recommendation → track participation, decisions changed and adoption, and plan for leadership turnover. It applies to school and district policy (school safety, school climate, a programme's evaluation survey), to a school's or classroom's learning environment co-created with students from the identity groups that most often experience inequities, and to civic or organizational processes such as participatory budgeting. The intended change is in the group's sustained participation in the deciding phase (persistence), in its members' sense of entitlement to decide (motivation-affect), and in what the institution decides and keeps doing. The participants are adult family and community members, adolescent students and staff; the knowledge being built is a complex-skill (deliberating and deciding together) and an attitude-motivation target.

The policy's central relationship is the one the folded pattern Differentiated Activity Structures for Inclusive Participation named: when a group is in the room but structurally unable to exercise the decision (a "double-bind"), outreach plus interpretation into the unchanged process is expected to fail, and redesigning the activity structure is expected to sustain participation. One claim in this wiki tests a version of it, inside one case: in a Chicago ward's participatory budgeting, outreach plus sequential interpretation lost all seven Latino volunteers within two meetings, while a Spanish-Language Committee with decision standing sustained participation through a full cycle [+M]. That is a before/after comparison of two iterations at one site, with the researcher as co-designer and no comparison ward. No claim in this wiki compares the redesign with a partial version (own-language deliberation without a separate body, say), and none measures a learning outcome for students. The other claims are interview accounts from district–community co-design projects of what participants perceived and what made the work last. The sequence below is therefore the earlier pages' guidance, kept as a default with each step's evidence status stated; the response-dependent branches are untested proposals.

It sits beside two converted principles. Community-Based Learning owns the relationship of learners contributing to a community as a way of learning course content; this page is the reverse direction, the community's members taking part in deciding how the school works. Culturally Responsive Classroom Norms owns widening a classroom's norms of talk and participation; this page uses the same move at the scale of a committee or policy process and does not repeat that model.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
The group and its starting observation Who is meant to share power (students, families, a language community, staff), and what happens now: attendance by group across the last few meetings, who speaks in the input phase and who in the deciding phase, and whether the group's priorities ever reached the agenda. Record observations, not labels such as "hard to reach".
The decision on the table What exactly can be decided (a budget share, a policy draft, a survey, school norms), by whom, and what the institution is bound to do with the group's decision or recommendation. If nobody can say, that is the first finding.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, a school, a district, a civic process), meeting mode (in person, remote, mixed, phone-only), languages spoken, meeting length and frequency, the cycle length (single-session, days-weeks, term-plus), who facilitates and how much of their time is available.
Learner-valued goal Ask the group's members what they want from taking part (a specific change at their school, being heard, a skill, a credential, a safer route to school). Record where it differs from the institution's objective and keep both.
Designer or institution objective Which of these is the target: a decision made with the group, a sustained structure, a co-designed artifact (framework, survey, norms), or members' capacity to lead. Name the one that will be checked.
Outcome Attendance by group per meeting across the cycle; who speaks and decides in the deciding phase; recommendations made, adopted, declined (with reasons given); whether the structure survives a leadership change; and, separately, participants' own account of whether the outcomes they defined were met.

A request to "co-create with families" or "add student voice" does not specify these. Ask what decision is on the table and what the institution will do with the group's answer before choosing a structure.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

The default sequence joins the two folded pages' guidance. Step 1 and the structure in steps 2–3 have the one case behind them; steps 4–7 rest on interview accounts or are untested. Doses are proposals for a decision cycle of one school year or one budget cycle; shorten them proportionally for a shorter cycle.

  1. Document the double-bind (2–4 weeks, before anything is redesigned). Observe two or three existing meetings; count attendance by group at each; note where the group is present but silent or absent, especially in the deciding phase; and name the interactional mismatch (long turns interpreted one after another, an agenda that leaves out the group's priorities, meetings at times the group cannot attend). Talk directly with members of the group where they already are, rather than sending a survey. Evidence status: the participatory budgeting case [+M] documented exactly this drop (seven at orientation, one at the second meeting, none after); coalition members held that systems change needs direct engagement with communities rather than passive feedback requests [+W] is their view, not a test.
  2. Give the group a body or seats with written decision standing. Write down which decisions the body makes or co-makes, what share of the budget or policy it controls, and what the institution must do with its recommendations (adopt, or decline in writing with a reason, within a set time). Make it a full deliberative body, not a listening session or a subcommittee that reports upward. Evidence status: the same case [+M]: the Spanish-Language Committee had standing as a full decision-making body. Untested against a body without that standing.
  3. Redesign the meeting's tools around the group. Deliberate in the group's own language rather than through turn-by-turn interpretation; let members put their own priorities on the agenda; set ground rules with the group before the first working session (honesty, confidentiality, listening without judgment). Hold meetings of 60–90 minutes every two to four weeks across the cycle (proposal). Evidence status: own-language deliberation and flexible agenda norms are part of the participatory budgeting redesign [+M], not tested apart from it; youth designers asked for ground rules before a sensitive activity so participants feel safe [+W] is one design record.
  4. Hand the work over across the cycle. Early sessions (first quarter of the cycle): staff facilitate, bring data in a usable form and model the decision steps. Middle sessions: group members co-facilitate and interpret data with staff. Late sessions: members run the meetings, decide and present; staff support on request. Name the role shift aloud ("you are deciding, not advising") and leave ten minutes of each meeting for the group to reflect on what it decided. Move to the next stage when members are running the previous stage's tasks without being prompted (proposal). Evidence status: co-leadership was more evident where staff support let partners progressively take on inquiry, facilitation, interpretation and decision-making, and less evident where staff carried the process [+M], from staff reflections across projects; the timings are untested.
  5. Close the loop on every recommendation. Within two weeks of each decision (proposal), report back in the group's language what was adopted, what was changed and what was declined, and why. Evidence status: youth advisors reported that students were not adequately involved in decisions, that surveys were the usual channel and that follow-through on feedback was unclear [~W] (four interviews); district leaders filtered student and community voices through their own perspectives and kept security personnel despite calls to end policing [-M] shows what an unwritten decision rule allows.
  6. Track two kinds of success separately. Group level: attendance by group per meeting, members' claims-making ("we have the right to…"), decisions the group changed. System level: whether the structure is adopted by other units, written into policy or kept after the cycle. Add participants' own judgment of whether the outcomes they defined were met, as a third and weaker signal. Evidence status: the case found the two levels separable [+M]; Core Team participants in four pilot districts perceived that their co-designed solutions met the outcomes they had defined [+W] is self-report with no independent outcome measure.
  7. Plan for turnover and embed the structure. Before the cycle ends, write the body into an existing system (a standing committee, the school improvement plan, a budget line), secure support at more than one level of the district, and brief any incoming leader. Evidence status: teams that sustained co-designed solutions did so through buy-in at several levels, iteration, embedding in existing systems and showing benefits [+M]; staff turnover was named as the critical threat to sustaining them [~M]; the WSS authors argue durability depends on institutionalizing engagement as core practice [~W] (their interpretation, not a result).

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • Whether the institution will actually cede the decision. The policy depends on a decision with written standing; without it, the honest design is consultation, named as such. If unstated, ask: what decision will this group make, and what happens if the board or principal disagrees?
  • What the interactional mismatch is. Language, meeting times, agenda control and pacing each call for a different redesign. If unstated, ask: in the last meetings, where did the group stop attending or stop speaking, and what was going on at that point?
  • How long the process runs. Handover of facilitation and the evidence on sustaining structures need a cycle of several months; a single meeting can only document the double-bind and agree what comes next. If unstated, ask: is this one event, one term, or a standing structure?

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Redesigned activity structure against outreach plus interpretation. In one participatory budgeting case, Latino immigrant participation lapsed under outreach with interpretation and was sustained only after the activity structure was redesigned [+M]: an embedded, interventionist ethnography of Chicago's 49th Ward participatory budgeting across two iterations (fiscal years 2012–2014). In the first, seven Latino volunteers joined an English-run committee with sequential Spanish interpretation; one came to the second meeting and none after. In the second, a Spanish-Language Committee with native-language deliberation, flexible agenda norms and full decision standing sustained participation through a whole cycle, members began using claims-making language, and the model was later taken up by the ward's Leadership Committee, a Youth Committee and other wards (that adoption documented by observation and practitioner reports). Coded q2, i2, qualitative · r2. The researcher co-designed the intervention; there is no comparison ward, so continued outreach or changes in ward leadership are not ruled out. Carry it to "in that kind of double-bind, changing the structure did what added outreach did not", not to a ranking of the components.
  • How support is handed over. Deeper co-leadership emerges when staff support lets partners progressively take on responsibility, but recedes when staff carry the process [+M]: one Digital Promise analysis of staff reflections across collaborative innovation projects (q2, qualitative · r2, two entries). Staff accounts, no effect size; it supports the handover in step 4, not its timing.
  • What participants perceived. Core Team participants across four pilot districts perceived their co-designed solutions met the outcomes they had defined [+W] (interviews, q2, r2; no independent outcome measure); collaboration among school leaders, teachers and students improved buy-in and created student leadership opportunities [+W] (three California schools, interviews and focus groups, q2, r?); educators kept student voice and co-leadership in their later roles after the pilots [+W] (four pilots, q2). These are the participants' own accounts and say nothing about student learning.
  • What limits it. District leaders filtered student and community voices through their own perspectives, so neither district fully removed security personnel [-M]: interviews with board members and administrators in two districts plus board transcripts (q2, r3). Voice gathered without a binding decision rule was reinterpreted by those who held the decision. Turnover [~M] and the barriers Core Teams named [~M] (communication among students and among adults, leadership turnover, teacher engagement) qualify how long a co-designed solution lasts.

Do not read participants' satisfaction as evidence that power was shared or that outcomes changed: the perception claims are self-report from the people who ran the work. Do not rank this policy against consultation, advisory councils or interpretation-only inclusion on any learner outcome from these claims; none measures one. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (a redesigned body against interpretation into the existing body, or against own-language deliberation without separate standing, on sustained participation and decisions changed, across several sites) and the local observation that would stand in for it: attendance by group per meeting and the record of recommendations made and acted on.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does giving an excluded group a body with decision standing and a process redesigned around it sustain its participation and change what is decided, where outreach and gathering its views do not? In one participatory budgeting case, Latino immigrant participation lapsed under outreach with interpretation and was sustained only after the activity structure was redesigned [+M]
  • What keeps a co-created arrangement going after the pilot: handing facilitation over, embedding it in standing structures, or the people who championed it? After the Inclusive Innovation pilots, district-community teams sustained solutions through multi-level buy-in, implementation iteration, embedding in existing systems, and demonstrating benefits [+M]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims the earlier page cited that are not core evidence above, each with what it is and how far it bears on this page.

  • District officials and CBO leaders viewed the WSS Framework development process as a novel, replicable approach to community-engaged policymaking [+W]: 12 interviews with the Chicago Whole School Safety steering committee (q2, r3). Participants' view of a co-design process; the source itself notes the approach follows a long tradition, which complicates the novelty. Bears on the page as perception only.
  • CBO leaders characterized the collaborative WSS development process as healing and restorative [+W]: same study. Suggests that where an institution has harmed a group, the process may need time for repair before deciding; used above for sensitive material.
  • Iterative co-design served as a culturally responsive methodology for designing alumni surveys [+W]: a descriptive practitioner brief from five Early College High Schools (q1, design · r1); no test. Used above for keeping a brief's own routine.
  • Youth advisors report students are inadequately involved in school decision-making and want more transparent communication [+W]: four youth advisors interviewed (q1). A needs statement for step 5, not evidence that the policy meets it; the earlier page marked it as support.
  • Framing instructors as part of the solution shifted the design toward supporting instructor judgment and co-design [+W]: one project team's dialogue (q1). Bears on staff as the group sharing power; used above.
  • Eleven of 33 state guidance documents mention co-design or feedback loop structures, and 27 include student, teacher, parent, and community voices [+W]: a count from a review of state AI-in-education guidance (q2, review · r2). Context on how often policy names co-design; it does not bear on whether co-design works.
  • Staff and students in three California schools describe collaboration among leaders, teachers and students as improving buy-in and creating student leadership opportunities [+W]: used above among the perception claims.

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested design. A secondary school wants to revise its behaviour and safety policy within one school year. Attendance records show Somali- and Spanish-speaking parents come to the first open meeting and not again; the student council has no members from the groups most often excluded. The table changed the default: the school documents the drop over three meetings and asks parents, through a family liaison, why they stopped; sets up a parent body that meets in Somali and Spanish on Saturday mornings with a phone dial-in, and a student panel that meets in school time; writes down that the two bodies co-decide the policy's section on removals from class and that the principal must answer each recommendation in writing within two weeks; staff run the first two meetings, a parent co-chairs from the third, and the bodies present the draft to the governing board themselves. A parent says she wants her son to stop being sent home; the school's objective is a policy that cuts removals. They agree to track both the number of removals and whether families say they were told why. The participatory budgeting case covers an adult civic process in one ward, not this school, these groups or this horizon; these are local choices to observe, not settled ones.

Record: the decision on the table and its written standing → the group and its starting attendance and speaking turns by phase → the redesign made (language, agenda, times, mode, ground rules) → attendance by group at every meeting → who facilitated each meeting, and when tasks were handed over → recommendations made, and the response to each with its reason → decisions the group changed → whether the structure was written into a plan or policy, and what happened at a leadership change → the group's own judgment of whether its outcomes were met, kept apart from the institution's. Preserve each member's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or claim that power was shared from attendance alone.

Elements and limits¶

Belonging, the Rightful Presence Implementation Guide, co-designed research questions, the method Make the Invisible Visible (Power Dynamics), the process Humanizing Co-design with Educators, and the strategies engaging student voice, family-school partnerships, listening to the voices of students in PBL and building a trusting community. Theory: Cultural-Historical Activity Theory reads the redesign as a formative intervention that introduces new tools and roles to resolve a double-bind, and the separation of group-level from system-level learning in step 6 comes from it.

This pattern is scoped to sharing a real decision with a group the existing process excludes. It does not cover consultation (gathering views for others to decide), which is a legitimate design if named as one, or classroom participation norms, which the culturally responsive norms principle owns. Whether a redesigned structure sustains participation better than partial measures, across sites, and whether it changes anything students learn, remains to be tested; the wiki's evidence is one before/after case and participants' accounts.

Claims¶

  • Perceived-desired discrepancies decreased for co-designers but often increased or decreased less for the rest of the class [~W]
  • Teacher–student disagreement diminished for co-designers and, in most cases, for the rest of the class [~W]
  • Student awareness of improvement differed sharply by role: most co-designers noticed improvement, most control students noticed none [+W]
  • Centering teachers of color as leaders, designers, and implementers throughout design is the most broadly appreciated aspect of the Design Team process [+W]
  • Participatory redesign produced significantly more positive perception effects on instruction aspects relevant to the formulated improvements than on irrelevant ones [+W]
  • Power should be conceptualized as diffuse and structural, and as people's capacity to act under constraint [~W]
  • A scaffolded participatory exercise in which students wrote rules governing the instructor's own AI use produced an accountability framework the instructor found more usable than institutional guidance [+W]
