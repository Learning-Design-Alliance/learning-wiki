Self-Directed Learning¶

Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 18 claims (9 for, 7 mixed, 2 against) · 27 studies (9 quant-synthesis, 7 causal, 5 review, 4 theoretical, 2 associational), q1–q4 · 8 of 27 report an effect size · 9 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable course- or unit-level policy for handing learners control over their own goals, resources, pace and evaluation in stages: probe what the learner can already plan and judge → agree a written plan (goals, if-then plans for obstacles, the evidence that will show progress, dates) → teach the self-direction moves inside the subject work → release choice in steps (pace first, then resources and tasks, then goals and evidence), each step earned by an observed criterion → brief scheduled check-ins against the plan → the learner evaluates the result against stated criteria, an outside check is compared with it, and the next cycle is planned. The intended change is twofold: learning in the subject (achievement, measured on the course's own outcome) and the capability to run the next cycle with less support (self-regulation, observed as plans kept, accurate self-ratings and sensible choices, not only questionnaire scores).

It sits beside three converted pages and does not repeat them. The Self-Directed Learning principle owns the relationship (when learner-directed study helps, and for whom); this page owns the sequence. The Self-Regulated Learning pattern owns the within-task plan–monitor–act prompting and its fading; use it for step 3 below. The Autonomy principle owns the motivational case for choice, and Goal Setting and Monitoring the goal-setting relationship.

No claim in this wiki tests self-directed learning as this page defines it, that is, a course in which control of goals, resources, pace and evaluation is handed to learners, against a teacher-directed course on the same outcome. The nearest tests point in different directions: giving learners control within educational technology showed an overall effect near zero across 18 studies; self-paced mastery courses lost more students than conventional ones; and explicit training in planning, monitoring and evaluating raised outcomes in school meta-analyses. So the sequence below is the earlier page's requirements made concrete, each step labelled with its evidence status, and the staged release of control is an untested design proposal.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Before handing over any choice: a short content probe on the course's first topic; a written plan for one sample week; a prediction of the probe score set beside the actual score. Record whether the learner plans unprompted, how far the prediction misses, and prior knowledge of the domain. Preserve unknowns rather than labelling a learner novice or "self-directed".
What can be handed over Which of goals, resources, sequence, pace, products and evaluation criteria the course can actually let learners decide, and which are fixed by an exam, a licence, a standard or an employer.
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-self-paced, online-instructor-led, workplace-clinical, mixed); single-session or days-weeks; how many minutes a week a facilitator has per learner; whether learners have devices beyond a phone; group size.
Learner-valued goal Ask what the learner wants from the course and what would make it worth their time. Record disagreement with the designer objective; in this pattern the learner's goal often becomes part of the plan, so record where it does and where it cannot.
Designer objective Which matters most: course content mastered, completion, the learner's capability to plan and judge their own work, or a product. Say which supports are still permitted when the target is measured.
Outcome An instrument per objective: the subject test or product rubric; completion by the deadline; calibration (self-rating against scored result); traces (plans kept, choices made, check-ins attended); any questionnaire labelled as self-report. Horizon: end of unit, end of course, delayed.

A request to "make this course self-directed" or "let learners take ownership" specifies none of these. Ask what can be handed over, to whom, and what will show it worked, before choosing a stage or a menu.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

The default runs over a course of about 8–12 weeks. Durations and release criteria are labelled proposals; where a claim bears on a step it is named, with how far it is carried.

  1. Probe readiness (session 1, 30–40 minutes). Content probe, a plan for a sample week, and a score prediction before the probe is marked; then show the learner the gap. Evidence status: self-assessment accuracy is low without training [~S]: in one randomized experiment 80 secondary students who studied modelling examples of self-assessment rated their genetics problem-solving more accurately than those who did not, and a meta-analysis of 51 higher-education studies found student and teacher marks agreed inconsistently, more closely in advanced courses. Do not rely on a learner's own judgment of readiness. Durations untested.
  2. Agree a written plan, a learning contract (session 1–2, 20–30 minutes, revised every 2–3 weeks). The plan names: the course goal and, for learners new to the content, process goals (the steps or strategies to practise) rather than only a score; two or three if-then plans for the obstacles the learner expects ("if my shift runs late on Tuesday, then I do the 15-minute review on Wednesday morning"); the evidence that will show progress; and dated checkpoints. Evidence status: process goals outperform outcome goals for novices [+S] (84 high-school girls learning a writing-revision skill; a shift to outcome goals once the skill is mastered also suggested); implementation intentions improve goal attainment [+S] (meta-analysis of 94 tests, d = .65, mostly university students; in two randomized online-course experiments with 17,963 learners an eight-minute activity combining mental contrasting with if-then plans raised completion for learners in individualist cultures and left learners in collectivist cultures unaffected). Neither tests a learning contract as a whole.
  3. Teach the self-direction moves inside the subject work (weeks 1–3, about 10–15 minutes of each session). Model, on this course's material, how to set a session goal, choose between two resources, check work against the criterion and choose a next action; use short task-specific planning, monitoring and evaluation prompts at natural pauses, paired with feedback. Run the prompting and fading as in the Self-Regulated Learning pattern. Evidence status: self-regulation strategy instruction improves achievement [+S] (84 school intervention studies, average 0.69 over performance, strategy use and motivation combined, larger when researchers rather than regular teachers delivered it); metacognitive prompts improve learning [+M] (computer-based environments, g = 0.40 on learning outcomes against unprompted controls, varying with feedback, task specificity and adaptivity).
  4. Release control in stages, each earned (untested proposal).
  5. Stage A, weeks 1–3: the teacher sets goals and sequence; the learner chooses pace within weekly deadlines and the order of two or three set resources.
  6. Stage B, weeks 4–6: the learner chooses tasks and resources from a menu (a choice board of four to six options mapped to the same criteria) and sets weekly sub-goals.
  7. Stage C, week 7 on: the learner proposes a goal, resources and the evidence that will show it, within the course outcome; the teacher approves or negotiates.
  8. Move on when, on two consecutive checkpoints, the learner completed what the plan said and the self-rating fell within about 10 percentage points of the scored result (a proposal; set the band locally). Step back a stage after two missed checkpoints in a row. Evidence status: learner control in general showed almost no effect across 18 studies, while learner pacing helped on complex material in one experiment [~S]: a meta-analysis of learner control within educational technology found g = 0.05 overall, near zero across most characteristics of control and context; one experiment with primary-school students found learner-paced animation better only on difficult questions. Control as such is not what helps. Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement and persistence [+M] is the motivational case for meaningful choice (and its meta-analysis of 128 experiments found expected tangible rewards undermined intrinsic motivation, d = -0.4); it does not show choice raises achievement.
  9. Keep scheduled check-ins and dated checkpoints throughout (weekly, 5–10 minutes per learner, or an asynchronous progress post answered within two working days). Review the plan against what was done; the learner names the next action. Evidence status: self-paced mastery programmes reduce college course completion [-M]: in 32 evaluations of Keller's self-paced Personalized System of Instruction, 23 found higher completion in the conventional class (h = -0.14 on attrition), though the examination advantage held either way. Self-pacing with no deadlines costs completers; dated checkpoints are the proposed response, untested.
  10. Evaluate and plan the next cycle (end of each 2–3-week cycle, 20–30 minutes). The learner rates the work against the stated criteria first, then an outside check (teacher, rubric-scored product, test) is compared with it; the difference and the next plan are written down. Evidence status: self-assessment accuracy is low without training [~S], above; the cycle length is a proposal.

Plan written, checkpoints missed Ask what got in the way; check whether the planned work was too large, the content too hard, or time was taken by something else. If an obstacle recurs (work, family), add an if-then plan for it and keep the stage. If the content is too hard, return to Stage A for that topic and give a worked route. If time is the constraint, shrink the plan rather than add prompts. Untested.
Self-ratings far above scores Have the learner score an exemplar with the rubric, then rate their own work again; run a short content probe. If calibration improves with the exemplar, keep exemplar-scoring before each self-rating. If the probe is weak, treat it as a knowledge gap and hold the stage. Neither branch is a validated diagnosis.
Chooses only easy or familiar options from the menu Ask what each option would show against the criteria; compare chosen options with the plan's goal. If the learner cannot say what an option shows, map each option to a criterion on the menu. If the choice is deliberate avoidance, negotiate one stretch option per cycle. Untested.
Keeps plans and calibration, subject scores flat Score the topics the learner chose separately from the broad test; compare with the plan's goals. If chosen-topic scores rise, the outcome may not cover what was chosen; agree the outcome first. If not, the self-direction is working on the wrong content; narrow the menu. Untested.
Withdraws, says they would rather be told what to do Ask about time, confidence and prior schooling; offer a fully set week and observe whether work resumes. If work resumes with a set plan, keep Stage A longer and release one choice at a time; preference for guidance is information, not failure. Untested.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

The three facts that most change the decision:

  • How much the learners already know of the content and how well they judge their own work. Self-ratings are unreliable without training, and process goals helped novices more than outcome goals. If unstated, ask: on a short probe, how far do their predictions miss their scores, and could they plan a week of this subject now?
  • Whether a facilitator will see the learners regularly, and for how long. Self-paced courses lost more students than conventional ones; the check-in in step 5 is what holds the pace. If unstated, ask: how many minutes a week does anyone look at each learner's plan?
  • What can actually be handed over. If the goal and the assessment are fixed, choice is limited to pace, resources and order. If unstated, ask: which of goal, resources, pace, product and criteria can the learner decide?

One teacher or expert for many learners Run check-ins in small groups of 4–6 on a rota, each learner reporting against the plan in two minutes; the teacher reads only plans flagged as off track. Untested proposal
Mixed modes: some in the room, some remote; phone-only learners Keep the plan and tracker in one shared place that works on a phone; hold check-ins by short message or voice note for remote learners; put the menu in text, not a large file. Untested proposal
A single session only Do not run the staged release; run steps 1 and 2 only (probe, a written plan with one if-then plan, a date), and hand the learner the plan to keep. Untested proposal

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Handing over control as such. Letting learners pace instruction helped on complex material in one experiment, while learner control in general showed almost no effect across 18 studies [~S]: a meta-analysis of learner control within educational technology (18 studies, 29 effects) found g = 0.05, near zero across most characteristics of control and classroom context, with moderate effects only for social-studies and history courses and comprehensive technology programmes; one experiment with primary-school students found learner-paced animation better than system-paced only on difficult, high-element-interactivity questions. Its synthesis table counts 8 distinct studies on who should hold control. Do not expect a gain from choice itself; any gain has to come from what learners do with it.
  • Self-pacing and completion. Self-paced mastery programmes (PSI) reduce college course completion, though not by enough to explain their examination advantage [-M]: 32 evaluations, 23 with higher completion in the conventional class, h = -0.14; examination advantage 0.46 SD where PSI completion was lower and 0.55 SD where it was higher. The claim page's discussion attributes the cost to self-pacing rather than mastery gating. This limits the pattern: give deadlines.
  • Teaching the moves. Self-regulation strategy instruction improves achievement [+S]: 84 primary and secondary intervention studies, average 0.69 across performance, strategy use and motivation combined (no separate figure for achievement in the abstract), larger with researcher delivery and in mathematics; and a correlational meta-analysis found measured SRL only weakly related to achievement (r = 0.20 metacognitive, r = 0.11 cognitive strategies). Its general hub, Self-regulated learning improves achievement [+S], counts 11 distinct studies in its table. Training is the strongest support for step 3; it says nothing about handing over goals or resources.
  • Prompts. Metacognitive prompts improve learning [+M]: g = 0.40 on learning outcomes in computer-based environments, and a systematic review of 35 online studies judged prompting effective but found effects varied with implementation and prior knowledge; in one reviewed study lower-prior-knowledge learners benefited only once trained to use prompts. Its table counts 6 distinct studies.
  • Plans and goals. Implementation intentions improve goal attainment [+S] (d = .65 over 94 tests; the online field experiments helped only learners in individualist cultures, and when the main obstacle was predictable and surmountable) and process goals outperform outcome goals for novices [+S] (one experiment and a theoretical chapter) are the basis for the plan in step 2.
  • Judging one's own progress. Self-assessment accuracy is low without training [~S]: one randomized experiment (80 students, ηp² = .10 for modelling examples) and one meta-analysis (51 studies); its table counts 10 distinct studies. This is why step 6 compares the learner's rating with an outside check and why stage release rests on calibration.
  • Motivation and specific populations. Autonomy support increases intrinsic motivation, engagement and persistence [+M] (8 distinct studies in its table, one meta-analysis among them) supports offering meaningful choice for motivation, not achievement. Self-determination skills instruction for students with disabilities increases self-determination on some measures but not others [+M] supports teaching the component skills to students with disabilities, on self-determination measures.

Do not rank this pattern against a teacher-directed course from these claims: none compares them, the outcomes differ (performance, strategy use and motivation combined; completion; calibration; self-determination scales), and the 0.69, 0.40 and .65 figures are not on one measure. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (a course that hands over goals, resources and pace in stages against the same course teacher-directed, on the course outcome and on completion, at the end of the course and later) and the local observations that stand in for it: completion by checkpoint, calibration, and the course outcome.

The evidence by question¶

  • Does handing learners control over pace, path or tasks improve learning, and when? Letting learners pace instruction helped on complex material in one experiment, while learner control in general showed almost no effect across 18 studies [~S]
  • Can the skills of directing one's own learning (goals, plans, monitoring, self-evaluation) be taught, and does teaching them raise outcomes? Self-regulated learning improves achievement [+S]

Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

Claims read for this rewrite but not used as core evidence above, or used only in one row; each says how far it bears on the page.

  • In one synthesis of group-based mastery learning, five observational studies found more time-on-task in mastery classes and one community-college evaluation found lower attrition in seven of eight disciplines [+M] — the group-paced contrast to the self-paced completion cost; bears on step 5 indirectly.
  • Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions [+M] — a review and a theoretical synthesis; supports making progress visible (step 5), measures nothing itself.
  • Learner characteristics — prior knowledge, self-regulatory skills, cognitive styles and attitudes — moderate hypermedia learning effectiveness [~M] — one review's reason why self-controlled hypermedia is hard to show effective; bears on the readiness probe.
  • Student-driven, independent study is the dominant mode in online charter schools, with 33 percent offering only self-paced instruction [~W] and online charter students receive less live teacher contact in a week than conventional students in a day [~W] — survey descriptions of a setting where self-direction is the default and contact is scarce; no outcome measured.
  • Not all students in connectivist courses could autonomously direct their own learning [~M] — used in one row; second-hand reports.
  • The Coach's hint budget was managed poorly [~W] — used in one row; one pilot.
  • One adult educator's reflection on facilitated discussion [+W] — used in one row; a first-person account, no measures.

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested design. Pharmacy technicians, adults working shifts, take a ten-week online-self-paced course on sterile compounding before an employer competency check, with a tutor who has about ten minutes a week per learner. Because the outcome is fixed and stakes are high, only pace, resources and order are handed over. Week 1: a 15-item probe and a predicted score; one learner predicts 80% and scores 52%. Her written plan has process goals (the aseptic steps in order), two if-then plans ("if a night shift runs over, I watch the 10-minute video on my next break, not the reading"), and checkpoints every Friday. Weeks 1–3 run at Stage A with the tutor modelling a self-check against the competency checklist on a recorded demonstration. Week 4 her last two self-ratings were within 10 points of her scores and both checkpoints were met, so she moves to Stage B and chooses among a video, a simulation and a reading for each topic. Week 6 she misses two checkpoints after a rota change; she steps back to Stage A for one week and adds an if-then plan for rota changes. She says she wants to stop feeling slow on the ward; the employer's objective is passing the check. Both are recorded, and the check, completion by week 10 and her calibration are reported separately. The claims behind these choices tested other learners and settings; these are local choices to observe.

Record: what was handed over and what stayed fixed → probe score, prediction and plan → stage and its release criterion → checkpoints met or missed, with the stated obstacle → choices made from the menu → self-rating against outside check at each cycle → candidate interpretations (knowledge gap, poor calibration, time, avoidance, preference for guidance) and their basis → stage change and why → course outcome and completion at the stated horizon. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities that a learner is "ready for self-direction" without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Learning contracts, goal setting, learning goals, learner choice, choice boards, progress tracking, check-ins, self-monitoring, self-assessment, reflection, portfolio, fading scaffolding and mastery progression. Principles it draws on: Self-Directed Learning, Self-Regulated Learning, Autonomy and Goal Setting and Monitoring.

This pattern is scoped to a course or unit in which control is handed to learners in stages within a structured environment. Fully open networked learning, informal learning outside any course, adaptive systems that make the choices for the learner, and within-task prompting are other configurations. Whether a staged hand-over of goals, resources and pace produces better course outcomes or completion than the same course teacher-directed, for which learners and at what horizon, remains to be tested; the release criteria above are proposals to observe, not thresholds.

Claims¶

  • Student choice about learning content and path was limited: most teachers did not report high levels of student choice, and two-thirds to three-quarters of students reported rarely or never choosing [-W]

Related Patterns¶

  • Self-Regulated Learning — the within-task plan–monitor–act prompting this pattern uses in its teaching step

Examples¶

  • An adult upskilling course where the employer fixes the competency and each learner plans route and pace in a written contract with weekly checkpoints (see the illustration above)
  • A secondary independent-study unit run as a choice board of tasks mapped to the unit's criteria, with a weekly five-minute conference
