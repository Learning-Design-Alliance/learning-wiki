    Interdisciplinary Course-Based Research Experience¶

    Pattern · All patterns

    Description¶

    A Course-Based Research Experience (CBRE) has a whole class work on authentic, open research instead of individually supervised capstone projects. This pattern adapts CBRE for interdisciplinary contexts by deliberately recruiting students across disciplines and levels (undergraduate and graduate together) and organizing the course around a shared scientific paper as a boundary object that different students engage differently depending on their own discipline, while the class collectively builds a shared understanding of what counts as a strong research paper across disciplines. It extends Productive Disciplinary Engagement (Engle & Conant's four supports: problematizing, granting authority, holding accountable, providing resources) into what the source study calls Productive Syncretic Engagement (PSE): instead of committing learners to one discipline's problem-framing, authority structure, accountability norms, and resources, the course explicitly asks them to explore, weigh, and negotiate among different disciplines' versions of each.

    Implications¶

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Intentional recruitment across disciplines, career stages, and experience levels (the source study mixed undergraduate and graduate students across natural and social sciences)
    • A shared object — here, "writing a scientific paper" — broad enough to be recognizable and valuable across all represented disciplines
    • Iterative, low-stakes, frequent feedback cycles (peer review, drafts) rather than a single high-stakes final product
    • Access to distributed mentorship: peers, the instructor, and — critically — students' own external disciplinary advisors/labs

    Constraints¶

    • Disciplinary "noncoherence" (different fields disagreeing on what a good paper looks like, when a hypothesis should be formed, what a reasonable data-collection sequence is) does not resolve on its own; learners cope with it via distinct tactics (separation — writing different papers for different audiences; domestication — reframing differences as minor variations; care — treating rubric-building as collaborative, provisional tinkering) and a course should expect and support this variability rather than assume convergence
    • Works best over a full semester or longer; the source study's iterative four-moment structure took 13 weekly modules

    Grain Size¶

    • Course (semester-length; the source study iterated the design over 6 years)

    Target Goals¶

    • Building genuine cross-disciplinary integration of research practice, not just co-located parallel research projects
    • Course-based research experience boundary objects support disciplinary integration and authentic research networks

    Target Learners¶

    • Undergraduate and graduate students from different disciplines and career stages, ideally with some access to an external disciplinary mentor/lab (the human-capital resource this pattern relies on instead of pre-packaged disciplinary content)

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Boundary Crossing Learning — the shared scientific paper functions as a genuine boundary object, shifting meaning across the four moments below
    • Situated Learning — students move from observing and discussing papers toward full participation as paper-writing researchers embedded in real disciplinary networks

    Contradicting / Qualifying¶

    • Productive Disciplinary Engagement (Engle & Conant) as originally formulated assumes one set of disciplinary norms; this pattern requires extending it (as PSE) to contexts where norms genuinely conflict across the room

    Claims¶

    • Course-based research experience boundary objects support disciplinary integration and authentic research networks [+M]

    Design¶

    Sequence¶

    The shared object (a scientific paper) is engaged in four moments across the term, each shifting its function: 1. Compelling object: the instructor introduces the paper-writing goal as broadly urgent and recognizable, drawing on her own cross-field publishing experience to recruit students across disciplines who might not otherwise see a shared stake in the same activity. 2. Concrete objects: students bring their own discipline-specific papers into class; the group examines them side by side to articulate what a paper "looks like" and "does" differently across fields, connecting the abstract genre-goal to concrete disciplinary instances. 3. Normative object: the class collaboratively "live-blogs" the structure of example papers and builds a shared rubric — surfacing which paper attributes are common across disciplines and which are locally specific. 4. Novel objects: students draft their own papers through iterative, low-stakes peer review cycles, with mentorship from both classmates and their own external disciplinary advisors/labs; papers are revised repeatedly rather than produced once.

    Affordances¶

    • Authentic Audiences & Purposes
    • Inquiry-Based Learning
    • Agentic Data-Engagement Positions — students move through researcher/analyst/author roles with respect to the shared paper object

    Personalization¶

    • Disciplinary mix and career-stage mix can be tuned; the source study found the pattern worked with both undergraduate-only and mixed undergrad/graduate cohorts
    • The "human capital" content strategy (relying on students' own disciplinary networks rather than pre-built disciplinary content) depends on learners already having, or being able to access, some outside disciplinary connection — less feasible for younger or more isolated learner populations

    Related Patterns¶

    • Problem-Based Learning (PBL) — shares an inquiry-driven, iterative structure, but this pattern is explicitly organized around a single recurring object rather than a driving problem
    • Organization Simulation for Interdisciplinary Learning — a parallel higher-education pattern using a different shared object (a client offer) to the same integrative end

    Examples¶

    • A six-cohort, 96-student undergraduate/graduate course pairing scientific-writing instruction with authentic research mentorship; roughly 39% of students who completed a paper listed an outside coauthor, and most coauthors (89%) were people outside the course, indicating the shared object connected students to real external research networks.
