Cooperative Learning¶

Pattern · All patterns
Evidence · 38 claims (21 for, 17 mixed) · 36 studies (13 quant-synthesis, 7 causal, 6 review, 6 design, 2 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 13 of 36 report an effect size · 30 claims rest on one study

Description and scope¶

A reusable policy for organising small-group work so that each member's learning, not only the group's product, is the target and is checked. It instantiates the conditional principle. The study configurations below are evidence; the response-dependent policy is an untested design proposal. Use it to gather better evidence about each member, not to certify that a group "worked". Named structures such as jigsaw, Student Teams-Achievement Divisions or reciprocal peer tutoring are configurations of this policy, each with its own evidence; none defines it.

Inputs: establish the design brief¶

Field Record or elicit
Learner and starting observation Task-specific prior knowledge for each member, from an individual response before grouping; age band; language and access needs; known status or friendship patterns. Preserve unknowns rather than labelling members "high" or "low".
Context and activities Setting (classroom, online, workplace), group size and how groups are formed, how long groups stay together, time available, the task and whether one person could do it alone, roles, talk supports, teacher monitoring, and exactly how any group reward or grade is computed.
Learner-valued goal Ask each learner what they want from working in a group and why. Record disagreement with the designer objective, such as valuing the group grade or an even workload over individual mastery.
Designer objective The capability each member should have afterwards, on what kind of knowledge (concept, principle, complex skill, collaboration itself), and what each member must be able to do without the group.
Outcome An individual instrument aligned with the objective, a locally chosen criterion, immediate and delayed horizons, and any separate outcome (collaboration skill, persistence, belonging) with its own measure. A group product score is not this outcome.

A request for "more group work" or "more engagement" does not specify these inputs. Ask for them before choosing a structure, a reward scheme or a predicted gain.

Sequence and conditional policy¶

  1. Elicit individually first. Take a short individual response on the target before grouping. It is both the starting observation and the comparison for later individual checks.
  2. Choose a task that needs the group. Prefer a task with something to explain, compare or reconcile. If one member could complete it alone, expect parallel or divided work and either change the task or treat the session as individual practice.
  3. Set interdependence and individual accountability together. Make the group's recognition depend on each member's individual learning (for example, the sum of members' individual quiz improvements), and say in advance how each member will be checked. Record the exact scheme delivered.
  4. Support the interaction. Assign or rotate roles and give talk supports where members are young, new to group work or uncertain; monitor talk and give process-level feedback.
  5. Check each member individually. Use a new task aligned with the objective, under the agreed aids, then use the table below to choose the next activity provisionally.
  6. Reobserve at the stated horizon, and have groups reflect briefly on how they worked. Retain unresolved explanations; if individual results disagree with the expectation, revise the configuration rather than the definition.
Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
Group product is strong but one or more members fail the individual check Ask each member to explain or redo a part of the product they did not author; compare with a changed task. If members cannot explain others' parts, make the task less divisible or require each member to present any part chosen at random. If they can explain but not transfer, give individual practice. Neither branch is a validated diagnosis.
One member dominates talk Ask each member privately for their answer before group discussion; rotate the reporter role. If others had sound answers, adjust roles or status supports; if they had none, the gap is knowledge, so give targeted support before the next group task.
Talk is off task Check whether the task needs discussion and whether members can start it individually. Redesign the task or supply prerequisite instruction first. Do not read off-task talk alone as a motivation problem.
Members converge on a shared misconception Compare individual answers before and after discussion; present a contrasting case. Give the group the contrast and ask for a revised, individually justified answer; monitor whether a confident member is carrying the group.
A member stays silent but passes the individual check Ask privately how they experienced the group and what they want from it. Silence alone is not non-learning; adjust participation demands only if collaboration itself is a stated objective or the learner wants it.

Choosing configurations from evidence¶

  • Reward on individual learning, not a single product. For school-age learners in programmes of four weeks or more, group rewards based on each member's individual learning [+M] are associated with larger achievement effects than single-product or no-reward methods (median +.32 against +.07, vote counts from a best-evidence review), and a randomised component study of at-risk 4th and 5th graders found reward plus structured tutoring ahead of either alone. Not settled against its sources (1 of 3 entries confirmed, abstracts). The same review reports structured interaction working without rewards, so a reward scheme is one candidate configuration, not a requirement; effects on intrinsic motivation over time are unresolved on the claim page.
  • Structure the interaction. In peer tutoring, structured programmes produced larger achievement gains than unstructured ones [+M] in a meta-analysis of school tutoring, though unstructured tutoring still helped and no magnitude for the difference is reported; in elementary peer-assisted learning, structured roles were related to larger social, self-concept and behavioural effects. Not settled (abstracts). It does not say which component (training, scripts, monitoring) matters.
  • Expect a programme-level gain in undergraduate STEM, not a forecast. Small-group against non-group instruction [+M] gave weighted d = 0.51 on achievement across 39 studies, larger when the investigator taught and smaller (non-significant) at two-year colleges. Not settled (abstract). It does not isolate which structural features were present.
  • Plan for failure in a given implementation. Science-classroom comparisons [~W] include null results and a chemistry course in which control classes won all 60 comparisons. Second-hand and unchecked, with no effect sizes; it is a reason to check individual outcomes, not an estimate of failure rates.

Do not convert vote counts or median effects into pooled estimates, rank these syntheses against each other, or read a group product score as evidence about any member. A precise abstention names the missing input (individual starting response, outcome, horizon) and how to get it.

Fitting the design to a situation¶

Ask for three facts when the brief omits them: the learners' age and experience of group work, whether the format is groups, pairs or peer tutoring, and what the individual check in step 5 will be and when.

If the brief says… Then change… Basis
School-age learners, a programme of four weeks or more In step 3, compute the team's recognition from members' individual quiz results or improvement, and say so at the start Group rewards on every member's individual learning [+M]
The format is peer tutoring Script the tutor and tutee roles and train them; structured programmes gave larger gains than unstructured ones Structured peer tutoring outperforms unstructured [+M]
The learners are English learners developing the language Use peer tutoring and peer response groups for language development Peer tutoring and response groups for English language development [+M]
The goal is oral proficiency through cross-age tutoring over a few months Do not rely on tutoring alone; three months of daily sessions showed no significant gain Cross-age peer tutoring: no significant oral-proficiency effect [~W]
The structure chosen is jigsaw Add an individual quiz that counts for the home team Secondary studies: STAD most, Jigsaw least successful [~W]
Groups are asynchronous online Use roles in step 4 to organise the process; expect reward-only groups to drift off task; do not expect either to raise achievement Interdependence type shifted the kind of interaction [~W]; Structuring interdependence did not change achievement online [~M]
Lab groups of four or five share one procedure Use groups of three or four, or rotate the roles across steps Large groups limited individual participation [~W]
Student group leaders will run the discussion Train leaders to elicit each member's answer before the group settles one Poor group leaders undermined discussion [~W]
Learners include neurodivergent students Ask each learner's preferred group size; neurodivergent students in one survey were most comfortable in teams of three to five Neurodivergent and neurotypical students prefer different team sizes [~W]
Instructors are new to cooperative structures Plan staff development with immersion sessions, peer observation and shared scripts before launch A threefold staff development plan supported implementation [+W]
One short session with no later unit Use a short structure (individual answer, pair discussion, individual re-answer) and skip the group reward untested proposal

The evidence by question¶

  • Does structured cooperative group work raise achievement more than individualistic, competitive or traditional whole-class instruction? Cooperative Learning Improves Achievement [+S]
  • Which structure matters: group rewards tied to each member's learning, assigned roles, or interdependence of any kind? In school studies, cooperative and peer-learning methods that reward groups on every member's individual learning raised achievement more than methods without such rewards [+M]
  • Does structured peer tutoring, a paired form of cooperative work, raise achievement, and does its structure matter? Peer Tutoring Improves Achievement [+S]

Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

Invented illustration, not a tested lesson. In an undergraduate chemistry lab, students want a finished report and a fair workload; the instructor wants each student to predict and justify a reaction outcome on an individual test next week. Before grouping, each student answers one prediction item individually. Groups of three get a lab task with rotating roles (procedure, prediction, data check); the team's recognition is the sum of members' individual improvements on a short prediction quiz, announced in advance. At the end, any member may be asked to explain any part of the report. Agree a reasoning rubric and repeat an unaided prediction item a week later.

Record: individual starting response → group, task, roles and reward scheme as delivered → observed talk and division of work → each member's individual response → candidate interpretations and confidence basis → discriminating probe → chosen next activity and justification → individual response at the stated horizon. Preserve each learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise this record, but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical state probabilities without a calibrated model.

Elements and limits¶

Group work, group roles, group processing, peer explanation, class discussion, assessment, quizzes and feedback.

This pattern is scoped to small-group work where each member's individual learning is the target. Collaboration skill as an outcome, intergroup relations, belonging, online or adult workplace teams, and long-running project teams need their own outcome measures and evidence. The policy supports observation and design reasoning; its predictive accuracy and the role of learners' valued goals remain to be tested with actual learners.

  • The effect of cooperative learning differs by the location of the meta-analysis studies, with single-country studies showing higher effects than mixed-country studies [~M]
  • Neurodivergent students prefer teams of 3–5 students while neurotypical students most prefer teams of 2 [~W]
  • Accountability can produce positive or negative effects depending on type, decision context, and task nature [~W]
  • Editorial reports that meta-level talk about group process, but not about individual actions, is associated with superior group outcomes [~W]
  • Off-task activity appeared more common in the larger six-to-seven-member teams than in the compact teams, though not in every larger team [~W]
  • Kindergarten triads sharing one computer with three mice and interdependent roles enhanced social skills [+W]
  • Team regulation correlates positively with learning results only in the CHT condition [~W]
  • Cooperative learning exercises were rated highly useful by >94% of students and were associated with roughly doubled class attendance [+W]
  • Cooperative learning gains were large for observation and interpretation but small for analysis in 4th graders [~W]
  • Cooperative learning reduced disparities between students' critical thinking scores, with lower performers gaining most [+W]
  • Participation within teams was fairly equal in all conditions, with Gini coefficients close to zero and no significant between-condition differences [~W]
  • Cooperative learning with Think-Pair-Share and RoundTable was followed by larger critical thinking gains than traditional teaching in 4th graders, in a two-class quasi-experiment [+W]

Related Patterns¶

  • Collaborative Learning — the broader category; cooperative learning is its most structured and best-evidenced form
  • Discussion-Based Learning — shares the mechanism of peer explanation, but at whole-class scale without built-in individual accountability
  • Cognitive Apprenticeship — complementary: peer coaching within cooperative teams can enact the coaching and articulation phases
  • Nine types of positive interdependence teachers can structure into cooperative activities

Examples¶

  • The science unit 'How does alcohol consumption affect our body?' — disciplinary groups regrouped into multidisciplinary groups of three to build a shared model

Jigsaw (Aronson): Each member of a home team learns one segment of material in an expert group, then teaches it to teammates. Every member is essential by design — the canonical positive-interdependence structure. See https://www.jigsaw.org.

Kagan Structures: Named protocols (Numbered Heads Together, RoundRobin, Pairs Check) that build accountability and equal participation into the mechanics of the activity. See https://www.kaganonline.com.

Peer Instruction (Mazur, Harvard physics): Students answer individually, convince a neighbor, then answer again — individual accountability plus peer explanation in a large lecture. See https://peerinstruction.mit.edu.

PALS (Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies): Structured reciprocal peer tutoring pairs in reading and math, with role switching and point systems; extensively validated in elementary classrooms. - Cooperative Learning Activities for Adult Learners manual with instructor-contributed scripts and handouts - Jigsaw Method - Jigsaw Classroom - Jigsaw - Pigelleto's Summer School of Physics: a full-immersion orientation program for high-school students - Full-immersion day structure: morning background lectures, afternoon group laboratories, evening observation - Cooperative Classroom Activities - Cooperative Learning - Encourage Cooperation - Adopt and adapt parts of several cooperative learning models to fit your teaching style and situation - ARDESOS-DIAPROVE program
