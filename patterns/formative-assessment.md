# Formative Assessment

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 28 claims (15 for, 12 mixed, 1 unmarked) · 39 studies (12 quant-synthesis, 10 causal, 6 review, 5 qualitative, 3 theoretical, 2 associational, 1 design), `q1`–`q4` · 16 of 39 report an effect size · 17 claims rest on one study

## Description

A reusable elicit–interpret–act–reobserve policy, qualified by what a response can establish and by whether a relevant next action is available.

## Principle

This reusable policy instantiates the [formative assessment principle](../principles/formative-assessment.md).

## Learner state, objective and valued goal

This is a reusable design model. A study result supplies a bounded observation; it does not reveal the state or future outcome of a new learner.

Before selecting a configuration, record the target task and representation, task-specific prior experience, access and language constraints, time available, observed responses and the exact help provided. Preserve an unknown rather than replacing it with a generic label such as novice. Ask the learner what they want to accomplish and why it matters. Record that **valued goal** separately from the designer's **learning objective**; agreement is an observation to elicit, not an assumption. If they diverge, negotiate the task or purpose and retain the unresolved difference.

Define the intended capability, a representative assessment task and scoring instrument, the criterion chosen for this design, and the assessment horizon. Agree what response would warrant changing support and how to check later independent performance. Criteria are local decisions, not universal mastery thresholds. If the brief only asks for better learning, elicit these fields before selecting an exact dose or forecasting a gain.

Keep the record in this form: **response under stated conditions → uncertain state hypotheses → discriminating observation → next activity → response at the stated horizon**. Difficulty, accuracy, speed, confidence and engagement are different observations. None alone proves learning. Confidence in particular is not a reading of the state: across 22 meta-analyses, self-evaluations agreed with measured ability only moderately (mean r = .29), more closely on familiar, domain-specific tasks, and in one undergraduate course the lowest performers were grossly overconfident — [Prior knowledge is needed for accurate self-assessment](../claims/prior-knowledge-needed-for-accurate-self-assessment.md) [~M]. Performance during learning also misleads about the horizon: with prose passages, restudying beat recall on a test five minutes later and lost to it at two days and one week — [Retrieval practice improves long-term retention](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. So the response that settles a branch is the one taken at the stated horizon, not the one taken during the session. Record a learner's changed purpose or constraints as well as a changed performance.

## Observation and adaptation

This diagnostic policy is a **design proposal; synthesis-level effects do not validate its specific branches**. Elicit an unassisted explanation or application aligned with the intended capability, plus the learner's reasoning and confidence. Record the prompt, response mode, language demand, stakes and assistance. Interpret the response using explicit rival hypotheses before selecting feedback; a checkpoint without a usable next action is not this pattern.

Four findings bear on the steps, though none of them tests these branches. **Elicit** with an unassisted recall or production attempt where the target allows it: across 159 effect sizes from 61 studies, a practice test beat restudying, more so at delays of a day or more and when feedback followed, and with no feedback and initial performance at or below 50% the benefit was about zero — [Retrieval practice improves long-term retention](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]. **Act** with information about the task and how to do it, not a verdict or praise: across 435 educational studies high-information feedback (d = 0.99) far outran reinforcement or punishment (d = 0.24), and in a cross-setting meta-analysis more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance, effectiveness declining as feedback drew attention toward the self — [Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [+S]; [Feedback works best at task and process levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. **Direct the correction at the errors**: in a word-pair experiment, giving the correct answer after an incorrect retrieval greatly raised retention a week later, while feedback after correct responses made little difference even when learners were unsure; in classroom quizzing studies, quizzes followed by corrective feedback showed a larger testing effect (g = 0.537 against 0.374, a between-study comparison that an earlier meta-analysis did not replicate) — [Feedback enhances retrieval practice](../claims/feedback-enhances-retrieval-practice.md) [+S]. **Make the next action contingent on the response**: in one-to-one long-division tutoring, support raised after a failure and lowered after a success beat fixed high, moderate or no support at immediate and one-month tests, with eight learners per condition — [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+S]. Before the feedback, asking the learner to judge their response against the criterion is the self-monitoring step that two syntheses hold makes feedback usable; it is a mechanism argument, with no effect size recorded — [Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M].

| Observation | Rival interpretations and discriminating observation | Proposed next action |
|---|---|---|
| An explanation omits an important relation | A conceptual misconception and an expression/language barrier can produce this. Compare two cases varying one relevant quantity, ask for a prediction and justification, and allow a diagram or accessible oral response. | If the relation still fails across response modes, give a targeted contrast and invite revision. If reasoning is coherent in another mode, address expression/access rather than reteach the concept wholesale. |
| A revised answer becomes correct | Answer copying and revised understanding both fit. Ask for a new unassisted case and justification, then repeat at the intended delay. | If only the feedback example succeeds, retain targeted support and another independent attempt. If the new case succeeds, reduce that support provisionally and check later. |
| A learner stays silent or guesses | Uncertainty about the concept and evaluative threat/disengagement are different possibilities. Ask privately about purpose and constraints and compare a low-stakes accessible attempt with the original response. | Adjust the conditions when they obstruct expression; negotiate purpose when the task lacks learner significance. Do not interpret participation alone as competence. |

For repeated force-only explanations, ask how acceleration changes when mass varies at the same force, and ask why. Connect the task to a learner-valued aim such as explaining bicycle braking while separating the teacher's conceptual target. Agree a reasoning rubric for the relation, an unassisted new braking example after feedback today and another next week. Those are local design choices; the source does not establish their optimality. If the target, purpose or starting explanation is missing, elicit it before declaring a misconception or choosing feedback.

For the third row, one design-based study in two community dance centres, with no comparison condition, observed youth sharing more elaborate, exploratory representations after a facilitator reframed the check as a conversation rather than a show, and drawing on movement, music and vernacular language to connect physics ideas to their own experience — [Multimodal, culturally grounded formative assessment supports engagement and agency](../claims/multimodal-culturally-grounded-assessment-supports-engagement-and-agency.md) [~W]. That makes the reframing and the wider response modes a feasible move against evaluative threat, not a tested effect, and the researchers' existing relationships with the sites may be part of why it worked.

## Evidence-bounded expectation

Kingston and Nash's corrected synthesis reports a science mean of 0.19 with 95% CI 0.06–0.31 across 17 effects; the erratum supersedes the original 0.09 science estimate: [assessment-for-learning observation and comparison frame](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md). It also removes an inappropriate comparison between two formative approaches. The synthesis supports a bounded program-level expectation and a need for better research; it cannot forecast this learner's response, distinguish these diagnostic branches or establish next-week transfer.

Before comparing another synthesis or applying an effect-size label, record assignment and baseline comparability, learner/context characteristics, exact treatment and control, outcome alignment and independence, score-range/standardization constraints, horizon and uncertainty. Preserve **unknown** fields. A correction removes one known error; it does not establish that all included estimates are comparable. Report raw magnitude alongside a justified context-sensitive interpretation; do not rank interventions or infer individual probabilities from these pooled values. The contingent step has its own limit: the evidence that support adjusted to each response beats fixed support comes mainly from one-to-one tutoring, and the review behind it reports that genuine contingency is scarce and hard across a whole class — [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~S]; so in a class the act step is more often a decision about groups than about each learner. The formative loop's causal pathway remains a hypothesis to test against subsequent responses.

### How large a gain should a designer plan for, and over what cycle?
- **Default:** plan for a small gain, and run the cycle within or between units rather than only at the end. A meta-analysis of 33 controlled US K-12 studies found d = .29 overall, with larger effects for a medium cycle within or between units (d = .52) and for formal evidence such as written feedback on quizzes (d = .40); a second K-12 meta-analysis of 13 usable studies found a weighted mean of .20, against the .40–.70 often cited — [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]
- **Tested with:** US and other K-12 classrooms in mathematics, literacy, arts and science (effects .32 in English language arts, .17 in mathematics, .09 in science in Kingston & Nash 2011).
- **Not settled:** the cycle-length and evidence-type figures are meta-regression results from one synthesis, not experiments that varied them. How often to check within a lesson has no wiki evidence.

### What should the feedback after a check say?
- **Default:** give information about the task and how to do it, not a verdict or praise. Across 435 studies, feedback improved learning by d = 0.48, but by d = 0.24 for reinforcement or punishment, 0.46 for corrective feedback and 0.99 for high-information feedback — [Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [+S], [Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]
- **Changes when:** the feedback turns attention toward the learner as a person → it works less well; across 607 effect sizes more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance, and effectiveness declined as feedback moved attention toward the self — [Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [-S]
- **Tested with:** educational studies of all levels (Wisniewski et al. 2020); laboratory and field performance tasks, not only education (Kluger & DeNisi 1996).
- **Not settled:** Wisniewski et al. coded information content, not the task/process/self levels, so the level hierarchy is supported indirectly; effects on motivational outcomes were smaller (d = 0.33).

### What should the check ask, and does it need feedback?
- **Default:** make the check a retrieval attempt, and follow it with the correct answer. Across 159 effect sizes, a practice test beat restudying, more so after delays of a day or more and when the test used recall or gave feedback; with no feedback and initial performance at or below 50%, the benefit was about zero — [Retrieval practice improves long-term retention](../claims/retrieval-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]; in classroom studies, quizzing with corrective feedback gave a larger testing effect than without (g = 0.537 against 0.374) — [Feedback Enhances Retrieval Practice](../claims/feedback-enhances-retrieval-practice.md) [+S]
- **Changes when:** no feedback will follow the check → multiple-choice items may serve better than short-answer ones, because students succeed more often on them initially (86% against 54% in one experiment); short-answer checks came out ahead when feedback followed, as a review chapter reports — [Initial short-answer tests outperform initial multiple-choice tests mainly when feedback follows them; without feedback, the higher initial success of multiple-choice tests can favor multiple-choice](../claims/feedback-determines-whether-short-answer-retrieval-outperforms-multiple-choice.md) [~M]
- **Tested with:** a meta-analysis of 61 studies; prose passages (Roediger & Karpicke 2006), word pairs (Pashler et al. 2005) and classroom quizzing studies (Yang et al. 2021).
- **Not settled:** the feedback moderator is a between-study comparison, and an earlier meta-analysis (Adesope et al. 2017) found no such moderation, as the claim page notes. The format evidence is reported second-hand from one chapter.

### Should evidence be elicited before teaching as well as after?
- **Default:** a short pretest on the coming material, followed by the instruction, helps. Learners pretested on to-be-read material, answering essentially at floor, retained it better than those given equivalent extra study time, and failed retrieval attempts followed by feedback improved later learning — [Pretesting enhances learning](../claims/pretesting-enhances-learning.md) [+S]
- **Tested with:** two laboratory studies, with educational passages, trivia facts and word pairs.
- **Not settled:** whether a diagnostic pretest used to plan teaching gives the same benefit; the claim page's boundary conditions (alignment with instruction, low stakes) are stated without cited studies.

### When should the feedback come?
- **Default:** the wiki's evidence does not favour immediate over delayed. In the one recorded comparison, reported second-hand, immediate and delayed feedback after multiple-choice tests were equally effective, each cutting later lure intrusions roughly in half (from 20% to about 10%) — [Providing feedback after initial multiple-choice tests cut lure intrusions on final short-answer tests roughly in half, with immediate and delayed feedback equally effective](../claims/feedback-after-multiple-choice-tests-halves-lure-intrusions.md) [~M]
- **Tested with:** laboratory studies of multiple-choice testing (Butler et al. 2007; Butler & Roediger 2008, as reported by Karpicke 2017).
- **Not settled:** timing for feedback on written work, revision cycles or classroom questioning has no wiki evidence; the timing notes on the feedback claim pages are discussion, not recorded studies.

### Should learners assess their own work, or each other's?
- **Default:** include supported self-assessment. In the K-12 meta-analysis, interventions supporting student-initiated self-assessment had larger effects (d = .61) — [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]; across 19 studies, self-assessment interventions improved students' use of self-regulated-learning strategies (d = 0.23) — [Self Assessment Improves Self Regulated Learning](../claims/self-assessment-improves-self-regulated-learning.md) [+M]; peer assessment improved academic performance by a small-to-medium amount over no assessment, and about as much over teacher assessment, across 54 studies — [Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M]
- **Changes when:** learners are new to the domain or untrained → do not treat their self-ratings as accurate, and train them first. Students who studied worked modelling examples of self-assessment rated their own performance more accurately; self–other agreement averages only r = .29 and rises with familiar, domain-specific tasks; the lowest-performing undergraduates were grossly overconfident — [Self Assessment Accuracy Is Low Without Training](../claims/self-assessment-accuracy-is-low-without-training.md) [~S], [Prior Knowledge Needed For Accurate Self Assessment](../claims/prior-knowledge-needed-for-accurate-self-assessment.md) [~S]
- **Tested with:** K-12 and higher education; secondary students (Kostons et al. 2012); undergraduates (Hacker et al. 2000).
- **Not settled:** whether peer review needs a fixed rubric or script. The one meta-analysis found no significant difference between scripted (g = 0.29) and freeform (g = 0.42) peer assessment, with a small freeform subgroup — [Structured criteria in peer assessment are not shown to improve its effect on academic performance over freeform review](../claims/peer-assessment-structured-criteria-improve-learning.md) [~M]. Whether giving or receiving peer assessment produces the gain is also open.

### Who interprets the evidence and adjusts, and what do teachers need?
- **Default:** support teachers to act on what they see. In Kingston and Nash's meta-analysis, implementations built on professional development (.30) or computer-based formative systems (.28) did better than other approaches — [Assessment for learning improves achievement](../claims/assessment-for-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+S]; teachers using a classroom response system with formative-assessment training reported learning more about student thinking and changing lessons as a result — [Teachers reported that CRS-and-TEFA use gave them more information about student thinking and that they changed lessons in response to that formative information](../claims/tefa-formative-information-changed-lessons.md) [+M]
- **Changes when:** the adjustment is meant to be contingent on each learner's response → the evidence comes mainly from one-to-one tutoring, and contingency is reported to be rare and hard across a whole class — [Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support.](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~S]
- **Tested with:** K-12 teachers; one-to-one tutoring studies for contingent support.
- **Not settled:** whether the benefit comes through teachers adapting instruction or learners acting on feedback, as the assessment-for-learning page notes; the teacher-report evidence is qualitative and self-reported.
