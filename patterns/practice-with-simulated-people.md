# Practice with Simulated People

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 111 claims (55 for, 43 mixed, 13 against) · 54 studies (12 causal, 12 quant-synthesis, 11 review, 8 qualitative, 7 design, 4 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 16 of 54 report an effect size · 106 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

Practice in which the learner performs a professional or interpersonal task with someone who plays a role: a standardized patient (a trained actor), a virtual patient, a simulated client, customer or colleague, avatar students in a classroom simulation, a pedagogical agent or virtual human, or a role-play partner played by a large language model. The decisions it covers are which performance to rehearse, how realistic and how responsive the simulated person is, how many attempts a learner gets, how feedback and debriefing follow each attempt, and how practice is carried over to real patients, clients, students or customers. It applies the [debriefing principle](../principles/debriefing.md) and the [deliberate practice principle](../principles/deliberate-practice.md), and draws on the [transfer of learning principle](../principles/transfer-of-learning.md).

It draws on three neighbouring literatures as well as the simulation literature: **pedagogical agents** and virtual humans (embodied characters whose appearance, voice and responsiveness shape how learners engage), **teachable agents** (rehearsing by teaching or coaching a simulated learner), and **computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL)**, where a scripted or AI partner takes a role in a dialogue and collaboration scripts structure who does what.

The study configurations below are evidence; the response-dependent policy and the doses are an **untested design proposal**. Use the page to gather better evidence about a learner group, not to certify that a learner "can communicate with patients" or "can manage a classroom" from in-simulation scores.

**What the evidence does and does not test.** The wiki's strongest evidence is two meta-analyses of simulation-based learning in higher education and the health professions, against no intervention or other instruction, and one meta-analysis of virtual patients against traditional teaching. They establish that simulation-based practice raises complex skills on average, and that the gain is smallest for communication skills. They do not isolate the *simulated person* from the rest of the simulation (mannequins and procedural simulators are pooled in), and they report few delayed or workplace outcomes. Two randomized trials test simulated people directly for communication: a virtual-human trainer for medical students against a computer-based module (blinded OSCE several days later) and a multi-agent LLM standardized patient against a structured non-LLM control. AI role-play partners are otherwise represented in the wiki by reviews, small design studies and uncontrolled pilots, and one review reports a trial in which AI-assisted simulation was *inferior* to human standardized patients for a hands-on skill. Teacher-education simulations appear in the wiki only as one small design-based study comparing a virtual simulation with authentic classroom video; the wiki records no claim about mixed-reality avatar classrooms of the TeachLivE or Mursion kind, nor about "approximations of practice" as a teacher-education design. Treat those configurations as plausible transports from the health-professions evidence, not as tested.

[Case-based learning](case-based-learning.md) is the neighbouring pattern for analysing a written case; this pattern begins where the learner has to *act* in an exchange with a person who responds. [AI tutoring](ai-tutoring.md) covers an AI that teaches the learner; here the AI plays the person the learner must serve, persuade, assess or teach.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Task-specific experience (`novice`, `intermediate`, `advanced`, or `?`), prior practice with real or simulated people, and one short baseline encounter: what the learner says and does, what they miss, how they recover. Record confidence or anxiety separately from performance. Preserve unknowns. |
| The performance | The named interaction (history-taking, breaking bad news, a parent conference, a de-escalation, a sales or support call, a requirements interview, explaining a concept to a confused learner) and its kind: `communication`, `diagnostic`, `procedural`, `collaboration`, `teaching`. Communication and hands-on procedural skills have different evidence here. |
| The simulated person | Who or what plays the role (`standardized-patient`, `scripted-virtual-person`, `llm-role-play`, `avatar-operated-by-a-person`, `teachable-agent`), how they respond (fixed script, branching, generative), what they may disclose and under what conditions, and how their realism and consistency have been checked. |
| Context and activities | Setting and mode (`online-self-paced`, `online-instructor-led`, `classroom`, `workplace-clinical`, `lab`), time per encounter and in total, who gives feedback and debriefs (mentor, peer, the system, nobody), whether attempts can be repeated without penalty, and the technology (screen, voice, VR headset). |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask what the learner wants from the practice and why (for example, to stop freezing in a difficult conversation, to pass a practical exam, to be ready for a first placement). Record disagreement with the designer objective; negotiate rather than assume agreement. |
| Designer objective | The behaviours that count (a rubric with observable items), the minimum standard, and whether the target is the rehearsed scenario or a family of scenarios. |
| Outcome | A performance rated with a *different* person (another simulated person, a human standardized patient or a real encounter), rated by someone blind to condition where possible; knowledge, confidence and satisfaction recorded separately; immediate and delayed horizons. In-simulation scores are a process measure, not the outcome. |

A request for "realistic AI role-play" does not specify these inputs. Ask for them before choosing a simulated person, a number of attempts or a predicted gain. Check first that the simulated person behaves as the role requires: that it discloses only what a real person in that situation would, that it does not drift out of role or correct the learner, and that a scored rubric item can actually be earned in it.

## Sequence and conditional policy

1. **Name the performance and the rubric with the learner.** State the observable behaviours that count and show one strong and one weak example of them (a recorded encounter, a worked transcript, a demonstration). For novices, keep the example; for learners with experience, a short statement of criteria may be enough. About 10 minutes.
2. **Baseline encounter.** One short encounter with the simulated person, unscripted for the learner, recorded. Keep it short and treat it as a learning exposure as well as a measurement.
3. **Feedback and a debrief after every attempt.** Immediately after the encounter: specific feedback against the rubric items (what the learner did, which item it earned or missed, what to try), then a short debrief in which the learner says what happened, what they were trying to do at one or two decision points and what they will change. Use the [debriefing principle](../principles/debriefing.md) for the debrief's structure. A score or a "wrong" flag without reasons is not this step.
4. **Repeat the same scenario once.** A second run-through of the same scenario, attempting the named change, followed by feedback again. Record the change between attempts.
5. **Vary and harden across sessions.** In later sessions use new scenarios from the same family, with different people, presentations and complications (an emotional reaction, a withheld fact, a misconception, an interruption), and fade the examples and prompts. Move a learner on when they meet the standard on a *new* scenario, not on a repeated one.
6. **Probe transfer with a different person.** At the end of the unit, and again after a delay where it matters, rate a performance with a different simulated person or a human standardized patient, ideally by a rater blind to how the learner practised; where real encounters follow, collect a rating from them.

**Dose and progression (proposal, untested as a whole).** Encounters of roughly 10–20 minutes, each followed by 5–15 minutes of feedback and debrief; two or three encounters per session; three to six sessions spread over the unit for one family of interactions. Early sessions: one scenario at a time, examples and rubric prompts visible, the same scenario repeated once. Middle sessions: new scenarios, fewer prompts, the learner self-assesses against the rubric before seeing feedback. Late sessions: unannounced complications, no prompts, a different simulated person, and the transfer probe. In the meta-analysis below, simulations lasting several hours to a day showed larger effects than ones under an hour, and duration beyond a month was not established; the 11-minute median session observed in one LLM role-play deployment for novice teachers is a description of use, not a recommended dose.

| Observation | Discriminating probe | Proposed action, with its uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Learner knows what to say when asked, but does not do it in the encounter | Ask them to name the rubric behaviour for the moment just passed; rerun the same moment. | If they can name it but not do it, the gap is performance: give more repeated attempts with feedback on that item. If they cannot name it, give the example again first. Neither branch is a validated diagnosis. |
| Scores improve on the repeated scenario but not on a new one | A new scenario of the same family with a different simulated person. | Expected rather than a failure: in one trial scores improved sharply between run-throughs of the *same* scenario, and repetition in language tasks improved the repeated task without transfer to a new one. Add varied scenarios before moving on. |
| Learner is anxious or freezes | Ask what they fear in the encounter; compare a low-stakes private attempt with an observed one. | Offer private, repeatable attempts without grade penalty first, then observed ones. Reviews report AI practice as lowering anxiety, but one trial found higher state anxiety with AI simulation than with a standardized patient, so check rather than assume. |
| The simulated person answers everything, volunteers information, corrects the learner or drifts from the role | Rerun with a probing question the real person would deflect; compare two runs of one persona. | Fix or replace the simulated person before scoring learners on it. An adaptive patient that disclosed more as the trainee explored behaved more realistically than a prompt-only one; LLMs prompted to play novices may not hold a misconception. |
| Good communication items, weak diagnostic or technical items | Score the domains separately. | Add targeted practice on the weak domain; the multi-agent trial raised communication scores without changing diagnostic accuracy, and simulation effects were largest for technical and smallest for communication skills. |
| High satisfaction and confidence, no change in rated performance | Blinded rating of a performance with a different person. | Do not read satisfaction or confidence as learning. Revise the feedback or the scenario family, and keep the rating as the outcome. |
| Learner fails a hands-on component practised only with an AI or screen-based person | Observe the component with a human standardized patient or a physical model. | Move the hands-on component to a human or physical simulation. One trial in a review found AI-assisted simulation inferior to a standardized patient for a breast self-examination skill. |

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Whether to expect a gain from simulation-based practice at all.** [Simulation-based learning has a large positive overall effect on complex skills in higher education](../claims/simulation-based-learning-large-positive-effect-complex-skills.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 145 studies (10,532 participants) found g = 0.85, with no significant difference between waiting controls (g = 1.02), differently instructed controls (g = 0.82) and pre-post baselines (g = 0.88). [Simulation-based education improves outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]: across 609 health-professions studies (35,226 trainees, 137 randomized), technology-enhanced simulation against *no intervention* gave pooled effects of about 1.1–1.2 for knowledge and skills, about 0.8 for behaviours, and 0.50 for direct effects on patients (k = 32); it found no consistent interactions with design features such as feedback or repetitive practice, and heterogeneity was large. Neither isolates a simulated person from mannequins and procedural trainers, and the no-intervention comparison overstates what to expect against other good instruction.
- **Which performance.** [Simulation effects vary by complex skill](../claims/simulation-effects-vary-by-complex-skill.md) [~M]: in the same 145-study meta-analysis, communication (g = 0.44) and collaboration (g = 0.50) gained least, technical performance (g = 1.06) most, with diagnostic skills (g = 0.82) and problem solving (g = 0.88) between. Practice with simulated people mostly targets the skills with the smallest average gains, so plan more attempts and better feedback for them rather than fewer.
- **Simulated people for communication: the two direct trials.** In a blinded multisite randomized trial, second-year medical students who practised with MPathic-VR, a virtual-human trainer for intercultural and interprofessional communication, scored higher than students who completed a computer-based learning module on an advanced communication OSCE several days later, rated by standardized-patient instructors blind to condition ([composite OSCE scores](../claims/mpathic-vr-osce-composite-transfer.md) [+M], η² = 0.0145, means .806 vs .752), with the clearest advantage on [nonverbal communication](../claims/mpathic-vr-nonverbal-osce-advantage.md) [+M] (η² = 0.032). Within the trainer, [scores improved sharply from the first to the second run-through](../claims/mpathic-vr-repeat-runthrough-improvement.md) [+M] (η² = 0.45 and 0.36 for the two scenarios), and students [reported more positive attitudes](../claims/mpathic-vr-more-positive-attitudes.md) [+M]. So the within-simulation gain was large and the transfer gain on a different, human-rated encounter was small; plan for that gap. In a randomized study of medical students (47 vs 48), [a multi-agent LLM standardized patient with scaffolding raised final OSCE-aligned examination scores](../claims/ma-scaffolding-improves-osce-exam-score.md) [+M] (71.8% vs 55.6%, Hedges' g about 0.8) against a structured non-LLM control, [most in communication](../claims/communication-largest-osce-gain.md) [+M] (3.53 vs 2.64 on a 1–5 scale), but [did not change diagnostic accuracy or worksheet completion](../claims/no-diagnostic-accuracy-difference.md) [~M] (86% vs 84%; equivalence not tested). It is a preprint with one site.
- **Virtual patients: replacing or adding to teaching.** A meta-analysis of randomized trials found [virtual patients no different from traditional education for knowledge](../claims/vp-traditional-knowledge-no-difference.md) [~M] (SMD 0.11, 95% CI −0.17 to 0.39, n = 927) but [better for skills](../claims/vp-traditional-skills-advantage.md) [+M] (SMD 0.90, 0.49 to 1.32, n = 897; 9 of 14 studies favoured them). Added to traditional teaching, they [improved knowledge](../claims/vp-blended-knowledge-advantage.md) [+M] (SMD 0.73, n = 439, 5 studies) while the [skills effect was positive but its interval crossed zero](../claims/vp-blended-skills-mixed.md) [~M] (SMD 0.60, −0.07 to 1.27, n = 247). Heterogeneity was high throughout (I² 74–88%). The authors' exploratory reading is that [the advantage is larger over passive teaching (lectures, reading) than over active learning](../claims/vp-passive-versus-active-comparison.md) [~M], and the few comparisons with other digital education [found no significant differences](../claims/vp-vs-other-digital-no-difference.md) [~M]. Use a simulated person to practise a skill, and do not expect it to beat an active alternative already in place.
- **AI role-play partners against human standardized patients.** A mixed-methods review of AI-powered simulation in nursing found its three trials support [short-term gains in communication confidence and structured knowledge with limited or inferior effects for tactile psychomotor skills](../claims/ai-simulation-short-term-gains-nursing.md) [~M]; one trial there found [AI-assisted simulation inferior to standardized patient simulation for breast self-examination skills](../claims/ai-simulation-inferior-bse-skills.md) [-M], and [AI simulation produced higher state anxiety than a standardized patient in one trial while qualitative studies reported less](../claims/ai-simulation-anxiety-divergent.md) [~M]. A scoping review of generative AI in medical education reported [short-term objective gains in some controlled virtual-patient studies, with positive findings not universal](../claims/genai-virtual-patient-short-term-gains-mixed.md) [~M]: some implementations measured only confidence or usability, and human or conventional cases were sometimes rated more educational or authentic. Keep human or physical simulation for hands-on components and use AI partners for conversational ones.
- **Repetition and deliberate practice.** [Simulation-based education with deliberate practice improves clinical outcomes](../claims/simulation-based-education-with-deliberate-practice-improves-clinical-outcomes.md) [+M]: across 14 studies, simulation with repeated, goal-directed practice, mastery standards and immediate feedback outperformed traditional clinical education (d = 0.71, 95% CI 0.65–0.76), mostly on procedural skills. [Longer simulations were associated with larger effects](../claims/longer-simulation-duration-higher-effects.md) [+M] (under an hour g = 0.65; several hours to a day g = 0.81; up to a month g = 0.80; over a month, 4 studies, inconclusive). The task-repetition evidence is a warning: [rehearsal improved fluency and complexity on the repeated task but did not transfer to a new task of the same type](../claims/task-repetition-fluency-complexity-same-task-only.md) [~M] (second-language speaking tasks). Hence step 4 repeats once and step 5 varies.
- **Feedback after each attempt.** [Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but more than a third of feedback interventions reduce performance](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [~S]: high-information feedback (d = 0.99) outperformed corrective (d = 0.46) and reinforcement-only (d = 0.24) feedback. In a qualitative pilot with ten graduate speech-language pathology students, [simulation feedback that marked answers wrong without explanation was judged insufficient](../claims/simucase-feedback-insufficient.md) [-M], while the same students [valued the debrief with the clinical instructor](../claims/debriefing-psychometric-instruction-valued.md) [+W] and [unlimited attempts without grade penalty](../claims/simucase-learning-mode-perceived-beneficial.md) [+W]. Among virtual-patient design variants, which mostly showed no differences, [empathic feedback improved communication skills over no feedback](../claims/vp-design-variants-mostly-null.md) [~M]. Medical students using an LLM standardized patient [valued repeatable practice and recorded review but questioned missing actions and feedback criteria](../claims/learners-value-repeatable-practice-question-gaps.md) [~M]; their peers described human SP practice as [valuable for embodied cues but episodic and unevenly connected to feedback](../claims/human-sp-practice-episodic-uneven-feedback.md) [~M].
- **Scaffolding by prior knowledge.** In the 145-study meta-analysis, adding a single type of scaffolding did not raise effects, and prompts alone were associated with *lower* effects (g = 0.65 with prompts vs 0.92 without), but some combinations did better, notably examples with prompts in four medical-education studies (g = 1.60) ([scaffolding added value partly supported](../claims/scaffolding-added-value-partly-supported.md) [~M]). [Prior knowledge moderated which scaffold helped](../claims/prior-knowledge-moderates-scaffolding-effectiveness.md) [~M]: reflection phases did more for learners at higher education levels (g = 1.10), examples for those at lower levels (g = 0.88, against 0.52 for reflection). Hence the example-first start for novices and the shift to self-assessment and reflection later.
- **Authenticity and fidelity.** [High-authenticity simulations showed larger effects than low-authenticity ones](../claims/high-authenticity-simulations-higher-effects.md) [+M] (g = 0.86, N = 76 vs g = 0.58, N = 26), and [document-based simulations were the least effective type](../claims/simulation-type-moderates-effectiveness.md) [~M] (g = 0.31, N = 15). [More technology support was associated with larger effects](../claims/technology-support-associated-with-greater-effects.md) [~M] (simulators in medicine g = 1.07; VR g = 0.85; screen-based g = 0.68; none g = 0.74), an association across study categories, not a within-study contrast. Authenticity here is how fully the situation is represented, not visual polish: a review of serious games reports that [higher perceptual fidelity does not reliably improve learning](../claims/fidelity-fallacy-serious-games.md) [~M] and can add load and cost. In an embodied clinical-communication study (59 participants), a conversational model that participants judged [more human-like produced the same measured performance](../claims/hal-human-likeness-did-not-transfer-embodied.md) [~M] (0.437 vs 0.438). Responsiveness is the property with direct evidence: an [adaptive virtual patient whose disclosure climbed across a session](../claims/avp-climbing-disclosure-vs-static-flat.md) [+M] responded to the [trainee's exploration but not measurably to their empathy](../claims/avp-exploration-drives-disclosure-change.md) [~M], where a prompt-only patient with the same model stayed flat (20 clinicians and trainees, within-subjects).
- **Pedagogical agents and virtual humans: appearance and voice.** A retrospective review reports a meta-analysis of 43 studies in which [pedagogical agents enhanced learning compared with environments without agents](../claims/pedagogical-agents-enhance-learning.md) [+M] (no effect size printed). Reviews attribute the effect [mainly to the agent's voice rather than its visual presence](../claims/agent-effects-due-to-voice-not-visual-presence.md) [~M], report [animated personas outperforming static ones, with voice sometimes contributing more than the animation](../claims/animated-persona-and-voice-effects.md) [+M], and report that [facial expressions helped attitudinal learning but hurt procedural learning](../claims/nonverbal-effects-depend-on-task-type.md) [~M]. Agents as coaches and tutors are reported to be [most useful for novices, while virtual role-players benefit learners across expertise levels](../claims/agents-most-effective-novice-learners.md) [~M], an interpretive summary rather than a single test. Give the simulated person a voice before a face, and add facial expression where the target is attitudinal or emotional rather than procedural.
- **Teaching a simulated learner (teachable agents and simulated students).** Learning by teaching has the firmest evidence of the configurations here, though with human tutees: [preparing to teach and teaching improved the tutor's own learning](../claims/learning-by-teaching-improves-tutor-learning.md) [+M] (28 studies; g = 0.56 for teaching after preparation, 0.35 for preparation alone, larger when the teaching was interactive). With simulated learners, [adding a teachable agent to a fourth-grade science curriculum improved learning gains](../claims/ta-improves-basic-and-added-value-gains-fourth-grade.md) [+M] (seven classrooms, quasi-experimental, d = 1.1 and 0.93), and university students who taught a ChatGPT agent [gained more programming knowledge than students learning from videos](../claims/chatgpt-teachable-agent-knowledge-gains.md) [+M] (20 vs 21; the printed η² = 0.74 overstates the effect) but [not more code correctness](../claims/chatgpt-teachable-agent-no-correctness-gain.md) [~M]. For teacher candidates rehearsing with simulated students, teachers in an AI-guided professional development programme said [simulated student errors moved them from solving tasks to reasoning about students' thinking](../claims/simulated-student-reasoning-pck-rehearsal.md) [+W] (qualitative). Whether an LLM can play a realistic novice is itself unsettled: an argument holds that [capable LLMs cannot genuinely unknow expert knowledge](../claims/competence-paradox-llm-student-simulation.md) [-W], and in one test [multi-turn interaction did not make LLM simulators hold a misconception faithfully and destabilised weaker ones](../claims/multi-turn-reflection-does-not-restore-sfs.md) [-M], while [simulators trained on real student data outperformed prompted LLMs on behavioural fidelity](../claims/studentsim-outperforms-baselines-behavioral-fidelity.md) [+M] in chess, second-language writing and mathematics. A [simulated persona's self-reported traits stayed stable across a long conversation](../claims/llm-persona-self-report-stable-within-conversation.md) [+M], but [model family and prompt format changed how strongly the persona was expressed](../claims/model-and-prompt-moderate-persona-expression.md) [~M]. Validate a simulated learner against real learners' responses before training teachers on it.
- **Simulated partners in collaboration scripts.** The wiki's evidence on CSCL scripts comes from two meta-analyses of human groups by one research group, not of AI partners. Against unscripted collaboration, [scripts raised domain learning a little](../claims/cscl-scripts-small-positive-domain-learning-effect.md) [+M] (d = 0.20; g = 0.24 in the update) and [collaboration skills more](../claims/cscl-scripts-large-effect-collaboration-skills.md) [+M] (d = 0.95, 0.65 without two outliers), which is the case for scripting the roles a simulated partner plays. In the moderator analyses, the [script level](../claims/script-level-moderator-not-significant.md) [~M], [whether the script prompts transactive talk](../claims/transactivity-moderator-not-significant.md) [~M] and [added content support](../claims/content-support-moderator-not-significant.md) [~M] did not significantly moderate effects (with post hoc power of 8–16%, so these are absences of detected differences), while [subject discipline moderated effects on collaboration skills](../claims/further-moderators-discipline-duration-measurement-level.md) [~M]. Where an AI partner takes a scripted role, the evidence does not say which role or script works best.
- **Teacher education: simulation against authentic video.** In a design-based study with preschool teacher candidates (eight groups), [authentic classroom video produced higher observation-assignment scores than a virtual simulation](../claims/av-outperforms-vs-total-observation-score.md) [-M] (85.0 vs 81.75, d about 1.2), with the advantage on [analysis and support dimensions](../claims/av-advantage-analysis-support-dimensions.md) [-M] and [no difference on objective description](../claims/vs-av-no-difference-observation-dimension.md) [~M], while the virtual simulation [prompted more self-reported purposeful reflection](../claims/vs-higher-purposefulness-reflection.md) [+M] (d = 0.51). The order was fixed (simulation first), so the comparison is confounded with sequence. For teacher candidates learning to *read* classrooms, pair simulation with real footage rather than replacing it.
- **Transfer and horizon.** No study recorded here follows practice with simulated people to a delayed workplace outcome beyond the 32 patient-outcome studies pooled in the health-professions meta-analysis. Reviews report that [simulation games support near transfer, with far and long-term transfer less robust](../claims/simulation-near-transfer-robust-far-transfer-weak.md) [~M], that [simulation advantages over physical labs on immediate tests did not persist to later examinations](../claims/simulation-advantage-immediate-not-sustained.md) [~M] (physics, not people), and that [simulation games helped as a supplement (d = .51) but not as standalone instruction (d = −.12)](../claims/simulation-games-as-supplement-not-standalone.md) [~M]. Plan the transfer probe and a delayed probe, and embed the simulation in a course rather than offering it alone.

Do not rank these results against one another: comparators (no intervention, lectures, computer modules, structured non-LLM practice, human standardized patients, authentic video), populations and outcomes (OSCE ratings, in-simulation scores, knowledge tests, confidence) differ. A precise abstention names the missing comparison, outcome or observation and the next way to resolve it.

## Fitting the design to a situation

Ask for four facts when the brief omits them: **what exactly is the performance (communication, diagnostic, hands-on, teaching), and how will it be rated with a different person?**, **who or what plays the simulated person, and has it been checked to stay in role?**, **who gives feedback and leads the debrief, and how soon after each attempt?**, and **how much experience do learners bring to this kind of encounter?**

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Learners are novices in this kind of encounter (`novice`) | Start with a modelled example and rubric prompts; move to self-assessment and reflection phases only after they meet the standard on a new scenario | [Prior knowledge moderated which scaffold helped](../claims/prior-knowledge-moderates-scaffolding-effectiveness.md) [~M]; [agents as coaches most useful for novices](../claims/agents-most-effective-novice-learners.md) [~M] |
| Learners are experienced practitioners (`advanced`) | Drop the worked example, use harder scenarios with complications, and put the time into reflection after each attempt | [Reflection phases did more at higher education levels](../claims/prior-knowledge-moderates-scaffolding-effectiveness.md) [~M] |
| Learners are school-age, rehearsing by teaching | Use a teachable agent inside the regular curriculum, not as a separate activity | [Teachable agent improved fourth-graders' gains](../claims/ta-improves-basic-and-added-value-gains-fourth-grade.md) [+M]; carried from one quasi-experiment |
| School-age learners would practise social or emotional situations with an AI character | Keep it a supervised, bounded rehearsal with an adult debrief; set age limits locally | [Teachers describe AI companions as a low-stakes rehearsal space but tie acceptability to age](../claims/ai-companion-rehearsal-space-struggling-students.md) [~W]; teachers' views, not outcomes |
| The goal is communication or interpersonal skill | Plan more attempts and richer feedback than for technical skills; rate the outcome with a human, blind to condition | [Communication gained least from simulation](../claims/simulation-effects-vary-by-complex-skill.md) [~M]; [MPathic-VR OSCE transfer](../claims/mpathic-vr-osce-composite-transfer.md) [+M] |

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Illustration only, invented, not a tested unit.* An online, competency-based unit for working adults preparing to hold difficult conversations with clients: breaking unwelcome news and responding to the client's reaction. The designer's objective is that each learner, with a client they have not met before, acknowledges the reaction, checks understanding and agrees a next step, scored on a six-item rubric. One learner says she wants to stop over-explaining when people get upset. Session 1: learners watch a recorded strong and weak encounter scored against the rubric, then hold a 12-minute voice conversation with an LLM-played client whose disclosure rules were checked beforehand (it reveals a worry only if asked an open question). Each attempt is followed by item-level feedback from an automated assessor and a written debrief prompt; the learner then repeats the same conversation. Sessions 2–4 use new clients (an angry one, one who goes quiet, one who withholds a key fact), with fewer prompts and self-assessment before feedback; a mentor reviews one recording per learner and leads a 15-minute live debrief. Week 6: a blinded rating of a conversation with a human role-player the learner has not practised with; week 12: the same rubric applied to a real conversation, rated by a supervisor.

Record: **performance and rubric → simulated person (who or what, disclosure rules, consistency check) → each learner's baseline encounter → feedback and debrief actually given after each attempt, by whom, and how soon → change between run-throughs of the same scenario → performance on new scenarios, by rubric item → anxiety and confidence, recorded separately → transfer rating with a different person at the stated horizon**. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical state probabilities without a calibrated model.

## Elements and limits

[Simulation](../elements/simulation.md), [role-playing](../elements/role-playing.md), [debrief](../elements/debrief.md), [feedback](../elements/feedback.md), [immediate feedback](../elements/immediate-feedback.md), [worked examples](../elements/worked-examples.md), [scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md), [reflection](../elements/reflection.md) and [pedagogical agent signalling](../elements/pedagogical-agent-signaling.md).

This pattern is scoped to rehearsal of an interaction with a person who responds. Procedural simulation with mannequins and part-task trainers, written cases with no one to talk to, simulations of physical systems, and assessment of competence for certification need distinct configurations and evidence. The wiki records no randomized comparison of an LLM role-play partner with a human standardized patient on a transferred communication outcome, no study of mixed-reality avatar classrooms for teacher candidates, and no study in business or technology settings of practice with simulated clients or customers; the diagnostic branches and doses above remain to be tested with actual learners.
