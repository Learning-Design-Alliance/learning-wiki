# Massive Open Online Course (MOOC)

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 37 claims (10 for, 22 mixed, 5 against) · 34 studies (8 associational, 7 causal, 7 review, 5 quant-synthesis, 3 design, 3 theoretical, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 6 of 34 report an effect size · 33 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable course-level configuration for a course that anyone may join, free or nearly so, at an enrolment so large that no teacher can respond to each learner: `online-self-paced` (or on a weekly release that learners follow at their own pace), over `days-weeks`, for mostly `adult` learners in `postsecondary` and `adult-workplace` bands. Its defining decision is what replaces individual teacher time: **short video or reading segments with self-checks → a planning activity and recurring planning and monitoring prompts → discussion seeded and summarised by a small staff → automated scoring for closed items and rubric-guided, calibrated peer review for open work → an individual final task → evaluation by what each learner came for, not only by completion**. The intended changes are in each learner's performance on the course's representative task (`general-achievement`, `skill-performance`) and in how far each learner gets towards their own purpose (`persistence`).

Two historical forms share the name. The first MOOCs (Downes and Siemens, 2008–2009) were built on [Connectivism](../theories/connectivism.md): learners were to build and navigate their own network of people and distributed content. Commercial "xMOOC" platforms (Coursera, Udacity, edX) adopted a broadcast model of video lectures and quizzes after 2011 ([Weller, 2018](https://edtechbooks.org/lidtfoundations/twenty_years_of_edtech)), and Wiley (2014) argues that most of them fail the [5R openness test](../elements/open-educational-resources.md) because their content cannot be freely copied, revised or redistributed. This page covers both, as two configurations of one course form; the choice between them is a row in the situation table below.

**No claim in this wiki tests a MOOC against another way of teaching the same content**, on learning or on completion. What the wiki holds is: second-hand reports that few registrants finish (about four percent in one report, under 10% in another) and that viewing falls off in videos longer than about nine minutes; two randomised field experiments in large online courses of a brief planning activity, which raised completion for some learners and not others; two quasi-experiments, one of them in a MOOC writing course, of rubric guidance for peer reviewers; syntheses of self-regulation prompts and interventions in online environments, few of whose studies were run in MOOCs; one case report of automated grading in a MOOC platform and a review warning of bias in such grading; and reviews and surveys of connectivist courses, video styles and institutional adoption. So the sequence below is the earlier page's six steps, expanded and kept as a **default**, with each step's evidence status stated; the response-dependent policy beside it is an **untested design proposal**.

A MOOC is that course with the teacher's individual time removed, so this page decides what replaces it. Where a MOOC has a tutor for each small cohort, use that page instead.
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../principles/self-regulated-learning.md) (principle) and [its pattern](self-regulated-learning.md) hold the plan–monitor–act model this page leans on for planning and monitoring prompts.
- [Self-Directed Learning](../principles/self-directed-learning.md) (principle) and [its pattern](self-directed-learning.md) own when learner-directed study helps and how to sequence it; the connectivist configuration here is one case of it.

| Context and activities | Expected enrolment and its spread across countries and time zones; staff hours per week (lead teacher, teaching assistants, volunteer community mentors); fixed weekly release or fully self-paced; length in weeks; platform features (auto-graded items, peer-review workflow, forums, analytics, downloads); whether any learners take the course together in a room (a campus using it in a blended course, an employer's study group); licence of the content. |
| Learner-valued goal | The registrant's own purpose, asked at enrolment and again at the midpoint. Record where it diverges from the designer's objective: a learner who wants one module and leaves after it has not failed the course. |
| Designer objective | The capability and its knowledge type (`concept`, `procedure`, `principle`, `complex-skill`, `metacognitive-strategy`); the representative task; which parts a machine can score, which a calibrated peer can judge against a rubric, and which need an expert (embodied performance, clinical judgement, nuanced writing). |
| Outcome | Performance on the individual final task, with its instrument and horizon; completion counted against learners who intended to complete, with the week each learner stopped (`persistence`); time spent (`learning-time`); accuracy of peer and automated scores against an expert sample (`assessment-accuracy`). Satisfaction and self-assessed competence are `self-report` and recorded as such. |
| Comparison being made | What the MOOC replaces or adds to: nothing (new access), a campus course, a paid online course, a textbook. Record what else changes with it (cost, credential, pacing, staff), because no claim here separates those from the format. |

A request to "turn our course into a MOOC" or "raise our completion rate" does not specify these. Ask what registrants come for, what the representative task is and who can judge it, and how many staff hours a week exist before choosing segment lengths, a peer-review load or a completion target.

## Sequence and conditional policy

The default is the earlier page's six steps (produce recorded content; open enrolment; release content with low-stakes quizzes; run forums; assess by machine or peers; charge for an optional certificate), expanded into a course with the substitutes for teacher time made explicit. Steps 3, 4, 5 and 7 have neighbouring claims, carried by extrapolation; the others are untested here. Quantities are proposals to set locally and record. A typical frame: 4–8 weeks, 2–5 hours of learner time a week.

1. **Decide openness before production.** Choose the licence (an open licence that allows copying, revision and redistribution, or a closed one) and whether there are start and end dates, paywalls on graded work, or a fee for the certificate. State the choice on the course page. Evidence status: untested; Wiley's (2014) argument that a closed, dated, fee-gated xMOOC is "open" only in the open-entry sense is an argument, not a claim tested here (see [Open Educational Resources](../elements/open-educational-resources.md)).
2. **Ask each registrant what they are here for, and offer routes.** At enrolment, two optional questions: "what do you want from this course?" (one module, the whole course, a certificate, a look around) and "how many hours a week can you give it?" Offer an audit route (any module, no deadlines) and a completion route (all modules, a final task). Report outcomes by route. Evidence status: untested proposal. [About half of registrants never viewed a lecture and about four percent finished, in one report](../claims/mooc-completion-four-percent.md) [-W], and [MOOC completion is typically under 10%](../claims/moocs-low-completion-embodied-music-learning.md) [-W], both second-hand reports; neither says what those registrants intended, which is why the question is asked.
3. **In week 1, run an eight-minute planning activity.** Each learner writes the best outcome of finishing, the most likely obstacle, and an if-then plan ("if (obstacle), then I will (action)"). Show it back at the midpoint. Evidence status: [forming implementation intentions improves goal attainment](../claims/implementation-intentions-improve-goal-attainment.md) [~S]: a meta-analysis of 94 tests (mostly university students, d = .65), and two randomised field experiments in online courses (n = 17,963) in which a brief mental-contrasting plus if-then writing activity made learners in individualist cultures 32% and 15% more likely to complete, left learners in collectivist cultures unaffected, and worked when the main obstacle was predictable and surmountable (work or family) rather than a lack of time. Expect it to help some learners, not all.
4. **Cut content into short segments, each followed by questions.** Proposal: video segments of 3–6 minutes, never over 9, a talking head beside slides or a hand-drawn style for worked explanation; transcripts and captions; after each segment, 2–4 auto-scored questions with immediate answers and a one-line explanation. Evidence status: [in 6.9 million MOOC viewing sessions, the fraction viewed fell as videos got longer, with median engagement under half the duration beyond nine minutes](../claims/mooc-engagement-declines-with-video-length.md) [+W] (a dissertation's report of a log analysis; viewing, not learning); [short videos, a talking head on slides and the drawn style were the more engaging production styles](../claims/guo-video-style-engagement-findings.md) [+W] (a review's report of one edX study, science and engineering courses only). The segment length and question count are proposals.
5. **Put planning and monitoring prompts into every week.** At the start of each week, "what will you do this week, and when?"; after each segment's questions, "which question did you get wrong, and what will you re-watch?"; at the end of the week, a self-check with answers and "what will you change next week?" Make the prompts task-specific, and give feedback with them. Evidence status: [metacognitive prompts improve learning](../claims/metacognitive-prompts-improve-learning.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of experiments in computer-based learning environments (learning outcomes g = 0.40 against unprompted controls; larger with feedback, task-specific and adaptive prompts) and a systematic review of 35 online-learning studies in which lower-prior-knowledge learners in one study needed training before prompts helped; [self-regulated learning interventions had a moderate pooled effect in online and blended environments](../claims/srl-interventions-moderate-effect-online-blended.md) [+M] (15 studies, 5,519 participants). [The online review found that support helped only when learners used it, that feedback alone showed no effect, and that very few experiments had been run in MOOCs](../claims/self-regulated-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M]; this step is carried to MOOCs from online courses generally.
6. **Seed the discussion, form small cohorts, and summarise weekly.** One discussion question per week that has more than one defensible answer; optional study groups of 10–20 learners matched by time zone or language; one staff post per week summarising the main threads and the commonest error. Use volunteer community mentors (former learners) where staff hours are short. Evidence status: untested proposal. In early connectivist courses [not all learners could direct their own learning, and some felt disconnected and demotivated](../claims/connectivist-courses-student-agency-problems.md) [-W] (a survey's second-hand report of two course studies), which is a reason to give the discussion structure and a visible staff presence rather than to leave it self-organised.
7. **Score closed items by machine, open work by calibrated peers, and audit both.** For open work (an essay, a design, a recorded performance), give a rubric with three or four criteria and level descriptors; before reviewing, each learner rates two or three anchor samples and sees the expert ratings; each submission gets three peer reviews; staff spot-check a sample. If open text is scored automatically, compare its scores with expert scores on a sample that includes non-native writers before relying on it. Evidence status: [rubric guidance helped MOOC writing-course peers place samples in the right quality level, mainly on subjective, technically complex criteria](../claims/rubrics-improve-peer-feedback-quality.md) [+M] (two quasi-experiments; the other, with 47 graduate students, found the rubric changed what reviewers commented on and restrained reflective comment); [NLP grading in one MOOC platform cut grading time by 70% and raised satisfaction by 15%](../claims/mooc-nlp-grading-70-percent-time-reduction.md) [+W] (a case report, no comparison of learning); [automated grading may disadvantage international and non-Western students](../claims/automated-grading-bias-non-native-english.md) [-W] (a review's stated risk). The anchor-sample calibration and the number of reviews are proposals.
8. **Watch the first week and every week after, and act on the first miss.** Record who opens each week and who completes its checks. Send one message (automated, personal in tone) after the first missed week, linking the learner's own plan from step 3 and a reduced catch-up route. Evidence status: untested proposal. In online courses (not MOOCs), [attrition in the orientation week was twice that of onground classes' first week](../claims/orientation-week-attrition-double-online.md) [~W], and [a single "tipping point" lesson explained dropout in only about a quarter of 196 online courses](../claims/tipping-point-hypothesis-insufficient-online-dropout.md) [~W], so watch every week, with extra care in the first.
9. **End with an individual task and evaluate by route.** The final task is the representative task, done alone; the optional certificate (step 1) rests on it. Report performance on it, completion among learners on the completion route, and the week each learner stopped. Do not report completion over all registrants as the course's result. Evidence status: untested proposal.

**Progression across the course** (untested proposal). *Weeks 1–2:* the planning activity; the shortest segments (3–5 minutes) with questions after each; prompts at the start and end of each week with model answers shown; peer-review calibration on anchor samples before any real reviewing; staff summary posts twice a week. *Middle weeks:* segments up to 6–8 minutes; one planning prompt a week, without model answers; real peer review, three reviews per submission, with staff spot-checking about one in ten; study groups take over the weekly question, with a staff summary once a week. *Final week or two:* no new content; learners rehearse the final task on a practice version with peer review, then do the final task alone. On the completion route a learner moves to the next week's material when they have done that week's checks (any score), not when they have passed them; a learner who has missed two weeks gets the reduced route (the two or three activities the final task needs), not the backlog. On the audit route there are no deadlines.

## Fitting the design to a situation

**The three facts that most change the decision:**

- **What registrants come for and how far they can direct their own study.** A MOOC's substitutes for teacher time (prompts, planning, self-checks) carry learners who can use them; learners new to the domain or to studying alone need more structure, and many registrants never intend to finish. If unstated, ask: what do registrants say they want, and have they studied online on their own before?
- **Who or what can judge the representative task.** Closed items can be scored by machine; complex written or designed work can be judged by peers with a rubric and calibration; embodied performance and expert judgement may need an expert. If unstated, ask: what is the final task, and could a trained peer with a rubric tell good work from poor?
- **How much staff time there is, and whether anyone is local.** A lead teacher with a few hours a week for thousands of learners can summarise and spot-check, not respond; a campus or employer group studying together can add what the MOOC lacks. If unstated, ask: how many staff hours a week, and do any learners study together in one place?

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Planning for completion.** [Forming implementation intentions (if-then plans) improves goal attainment](../claims/implementation-intentions-improve-goal-attainment.md) [~S]: a meta-analysis of 94 tests (8,461 participants, mostly university students) found an if-then plan raised goal attainment over a goal alone (d = .65; academic domain d = .72); two randomised field experiments in online courses (n = 17,963) found a brief mental-contrasting plus if-then activity raised completion for learners in individualist cultures (32% and 15% more likely) and not for learners in collectivist cultures, read from the abstract, with no completion rates by arm reported. This is the nearest test in the wiki of anything a MOOC does about completion. It tests a component, not the MOOC, and the combination, not the plan alone.
- **Prompts for self-regulation.** [Metacognitive prompts improve learning](../claims/metacognitive-prompts-improve-learning.md) [+M] (a meta-analysis of experiments in computer-based environments, g = 0.40, and a review of 35 online-learning studies; 6 distinct studies stand behind its table as that table counts them) and [SRL interventions in online and blended environments](../claims/srl-interventions-moderate-effect-online-blended.md) [+M] (15 studies, a pooled 0.65). [Self-regulated learning improves achievement](../claims/self-regulated-learning-improves-achievement.md) [~M] gathers 11 distinct studies in its table, mostly school-age; its online review records that very few experiments had been run in MOOCs, that learners gained only when they used the support, and that feedback alone showed no effect. Carry these to "task-specific prompts, used and answered, help in self-paced online study", not to any claim about MOOC completion.
- **Feedback on open work at scale.** [Rubric-guided peer review](../claims/rubrics-improve-peer-feedback-quality.md) [+M] rests on two quasi-experiments, one in a MOOC writing course (n not stated in the abstract), neither reporting an effect size; it is about the accuracy and content of peer ratings, not the learning of the reviewed. [NLP grading in a MOOC platform](../claims/mooc-nlp-grading-70-percent-time-reduction.md) [+W] is one case report of time saved and satisfaction; [automated grading bias](../claims/automated-grading-bias-non-native-english.md) [-W] is a review's warning. Together: peers with calibration for complex criteria; machines for closed items and audited before open ones.
- **Video length and style.** [Viewing declines with video length](../claims/mooc-engagement-declines-with-video-length.md) [+W] (6.9 million sessions, reported second-hand) and [Guo et al.'s production-style findings](../claims/guo-video-style-engagement-findings.md) [+W] are engagement measures from logs, not learning; [video style clusters by discipline](../claims/mooc-video-styles-discipline-clusters.md) [~W] is descriptive; [an instructor's face showed no learning difference, with some learners preferring it](../claims/human-embodiment-video-presence-effects.md) [~W] (equivalence not tested).
- **Completion as the outcome.** [About four percent finished, in one report](../claims/mooc-completion-four-percent.md) [-W] and [under 10%, in another](../claims/moocs-low-completion-embodied-music-learning.md) [-W] are second-hand figures without registrants' intentions; they describe the form's typical result, not the effect of any design choice.
- **The connectivist configuration.** [Some connectivist-course learners could not direct their own learning](../claims/connectivist-courses-student-agency-problems.md) [-W] (4 distinct studies in its table, `q1`–`q2`) is the limiting claim for this page: the more the design leaves to learners, the more it depends on their self-direction. [A structural model linked connectivist features with achievement](../claims/connectivist-principles-positive-effect-achievement.md) [+W] is reported without statistics.

Do not rank a MOOC against a campus or paid online course from these claims: none compares them, and none measures learning in a MOOC against an alternative. Do not report completion over all registrants as a course's effectiveness, or engagement in logs as learning. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (the same content as a MOOC with and without a named substitute for teacher time, on the individual final task among learners who intended to complete) and the local observation that would stand in for it.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested course.* A university offers a six-week open course on reading clinical research for health workers worldwide; 20,000 register; one lecturer has four hours a week and two former learners volunteer as mentors. At enrolment, 40% say they want one or two modules, 35% the certificate, the rest are looking around. The table changed the default: segments of 4–6 minutes with a talking head beside the slides, each followed by three questions; an eight-minute planning activity in week 1 that names the learner's shift pattern; weekly prompts with model answers in weeks 1–3; study groups by time zone and language, with a facilitator guide for the three hospitals that take it as a group; a final task (appraising one short trial report against a four-criterion rubric) peer-reviewed by three calibrated peers, with the lecturer spot-checking one in ten, and the certificate resting on a sample of finals re-scored by staff. No automated scoring of open text is used, because many learners write in a second language. A nurse says she wants to know whether to trust the drug leaflets on her ward; the designer's objective is appraisal of trial reports. They agree to add one leaflet as a practice item in week 5. Completion is reported among the 35% on the certificate route, with the week each learner stopped. None of the claims cited tested this population, course or outcome; these are local choices to observe.

Record: **registrant's stated purpose and route → device and time available → segments watched and questions answered by week → planning-activity text and whether its obstacle recurred → prompts answered → peer ratings against staff spot-checks by criterion → automated scores against staff scores by language background, if used → week each learner stopped and the message sent → final-task performance by route → candidate interpretations (browsing, access, time, missing prerequisite, unclear content) and their basis → re-observation on the next run**. Preserve each learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarise this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or treat completion over all registrants as the course's effect.

Theory: [Connectivism](../theories/connectivism.md). This pattern is scoped to open courses at a scale where teacher time per learner is negligible. With a tutor for each small cohort, [Online Course Design](online-course-design.md) applies; with regular in-person time, [Blended Learning](blended-learning.md). The policy supports design and observation; whether a MOOC built this way teaches as much as the same content taught another way, for which learners, at what cost and with what completion among those who intend to finish, remains untested in this wiki.

## Description
The MOOC combines several earlier innovations — open enrollment, video-based instruction, discussion-forum interaction, and open-content licensing — into a single, free, large-scale online course (Weller, 2018). The earliest MOOCs (Downes and Siemens's open courses in 2008–2009) were built around [Connectivism](../theories/connectivism.md): learning was meant to happen through learners forming and navigating their own network of connections across distributed content and peers, not through a fixed instructor-delivered sequence.

That original design was largely abandoned once the pattern scaled commercially. After Stanford's 2011 course (Sebastian Thrun) drew over 100,000 learners and attracted venture-capital investment, "xMOOC" platforms (Coursera, Udacity, edX) adopted a broadcast, video-lecture-plus-quiz model — closer to a traditional course delivered at scale than to the connectivist, learner-networked design the format is named for. Weller (2018) notes this is a recurring edtech pattern: the pedagogical model a technology is originally named for and the model it is actually implemented with can diverge sharply once it scales for a commercial audience. Wiley (2014) makes a sharper version of the same critique from a licensing angle: because xMOOC content is typically not openly licensed (no free copying, translation, or redistribution), imposes registration deadlines and start/end dates, and charges for credentials, it fails the [Open Educational Resources (5Rs)](../elements/open-educational-resources.md) test for genuine openness — in his words, once you strip away the "open entry" framing, a typical xMOOC is "nothing more than" a traditional online class.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Infrastructure capable of serving very large, unbounded enrollment with minimal marginal cost per additional learner — which pushes toward pre-recorded video and automated or peer assessment rather than instructor-graded feedback
- A content licensing decision (open vs. closed) that determines whether the course is "open" only in the open-entry sense or also in the 5R licensing sense
#### Constraints
- At scale, individualized instructor feedback becomes impractical, so assessment shifts to automated quizzes or peer grading — a real reduction in feedback quality and immediacy relative to smaller-enrollment courses
- The connectivist pedagogy the pattern was originally built around does not survive contact with a broadcast, video-lecture delivery model — a "MOOC" today is more reliably described by its scale and open enrollment than by any specific pedagogy
- Completion rates for MOOCs are notoriously low relative to traditional courses, a direct consequence of minimal enrollment commitment and light-touch support structures

- Course

- Reaching very large, geographically dispersed audiences with a single course offering
- Reducing per-learner marginal cost for widely-demanded introductory content

- Learners for whom free, open enrollment (regardless of prior credentials) is the primary access barrier being removed

## Design

### Sequence
1. Produce pre-recorded video lectures and other content, since live, individualized delivery does not scale to the intended enrollment.
2. Open enrollment broadly, with minimal or no prerequisite screening.
3. Deliver content asynchronously, typically week-by-week, with embedded low-stakes quizzes.
4. Support peer interaction through discussion forums (with variable, self-organized engagement).
5. Assess primarily through automated quizzes or peer/rubric-based grading rather than instructor feedback.
6. Optionally charge a fee for a verified certificate or credential, separate from free access to the content itself.

### Elements Used
- [Open Educational Resources (5Rs)](../elements/open-educational-resources.md) (when the content is genuinely openly licensed)

### Affordances
- [Online Course Design (Community of Inquiry)](online-course-design.md) — the same interaction-design concerns (learner-learner, learner-instructor, learner-content) apply at MOOC scale, though instructor interaction is necessarily far thinner

### Personalization
- Some platforms allow self-paced progression through pre-recorded content rather than a fixed weekly schedule
- Discussion forums allow learners to self-select which topics or peer discussions to engage with
