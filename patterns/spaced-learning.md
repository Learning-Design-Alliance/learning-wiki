# Spaced Learning

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 75 claims (30 for, 42 mixed, 3 against) · 53 studies (26 causal, 11 review, 7 quant-synthesis, 3 associational, 3 theoretical, 2 design, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 15 of 53 report an effect size · 70 claims rest on one study

## Description

A reusable policy for distributing learning opportunities, preserving total practice, gap and assessment horizon when comparing schedules.

## Principle

This reusable policy instantiates the [spaced learning principle](../principles/spaced-learning.md).

## Learner state, objective and valued goal

This is a reusable design model. A study result supplies a bounded observation; it does not reveal the state or future outcome of a new learner.

Before selecting a configuration, record the target task and representation, task-specific prior experience, access and language constraints, time available, observed responses and the exact help provided. Preserve an unknown rather than replacing it with a generic label such as novice. Ask the learner what they want to accomplish and why it matters. Record that **valued goal** separately from the designer's **learning objective**; agreement is an observation to elicit, not an assumption. If they diverge, negotiate the task or purpose and retain the unresolved difference.

Define the intended capability, a representative assessment task and scoring instrument, the criterion chosen for this design, and the assessment horizon. Agree what response would warrant changing support and how to check later independent performance. Criteria are local decisions, not universal mastery thresholds. If the brief only asks for better learning, elicit these fields before selecting an exact dose or forecasting a gain.

Keep the record in this form: **response under stated conditions → uncertain state hypotheses → discriminating observation → next activity → response at the stated horizon**. Difficulty, accuracy, speed, confidence and engagement are different observations. None alone proves learning. Record a learner's changed purpose or constraints as well as a changed performance.

## Observation and adaptation

This diagnostic policy is a **design proposal, not a validated scheduling algorithm**. Start with an unassisted sample of the target items, record item-level errors and the time since last exposure, then separate cued recognition, recall and meaningful use. Record restudy, corrective feedback, retrieval opportunities and actual gaps; changing them together changes the intervention.

| Observation | Rival interpretations and discriminating observation | Proposed next action |
|---|---|---|
| An item is not recalled | Weak initial encoding and loss of access across the gap are different possibilities. Check comprehension/recognition with a cue and whether the learner can explain the association; retest unassisted after relearning and an interval. | If the association is not understood, clarify and re-encode it. If understood but inaccessible, use corrective feedback and another separated retrieval. Do not optimize the gap from a single failure. |
| Immediate recall is fluent | Recent repetition and durable retrieval both predict this. Use an unassisted check after the target delay. | Maintain the delayed check rather than count fluency as retained learning; revise the schedule only after collecting later evidence. |
| An item is recalled correctly at its first spaced test | A durable memory and a recent, easy retrieval both predict this; which one it is depends mostly on how long the gap was. In one experiment with undergraduates learning foreign-word pairs, dropping an item from practice once it was recalled reached about the same final recall in fewer trials when about 23 other items came between its presentations, but gave lower final recall when only 5 did ([Long-gap dropout matched conventional schedules in fewer trials](../claims/dropout-long-isi-similar-performance-fewer-trials.md) [+W]; [Short-gap dropout lowered final recall](../claims/short-isi-dropout-lower-performance.md) [-W]; [Dropout depends on the interval](../claims/dropout-effectiveness-depends-on-interval.md) [~W]). Dropped items lost a little against keeping them at long gaps, offset by the extra practice missed items got ([Dropping correct items cost a little, offset by gains on missed ones](../claims/dropout-cost-for-initially-correct-items.md) [~W]); in both experiments items missed at first were more often recalled at the final test under long-gap dropout than under either conventional schedule, which the authors attribute to the extra trials those items received, not to the gap or item difficulty ([Dropout helped initially missed items most](../claims/dropout-benefits-initially-incorrect-items.md) [+W]). Massed repeated recall after a first correct recall added nothing on a one-week test ([Spaced retrieval wins at the final test despite lower initial recall](../claims/spaced-retrieval-outperforms-massed-retrieval-despite-lower-initial-recall.md) [+M]). "About the same" there is a nonsignificant difference, not shown equivalence. | If the gap before that recall was long, the item may leave this session's practice and the time go to missed items; keep it in the later spaced reviews. If the gap was short, keep practising it after a longer gap rather than dropping it. Do not repeat it immediately; massed repetitions add nothing. |
| The learner wants to skip reviews because the items feel known | Feeling of knowing after recent exposure and durable recall predict the same report; learners judge massed study more effective even when their own delayed test shows spacing worked better ([Learners misjudge spacing benefits](../claims/learners-misjudge-spacing-benefits.md) [~M]). Asking learners to rate how likely they are to recall each item did not change the spacing benefit in one free-recall experiment, so collecting such ratings is not itself a cost ([Likelihood ratings left the spacing effect unchanged](../claims/spacing-effect-free-recall-dp-beats-mp.md) [+W]). Compare the report with an unassisted check after a gap; the observation table on the [principle](../principles/spaced-learning.md) gives the item-rating version of this row. | Keep the scheduled reviews; let the delayed check, not the feeling, decide whether an item leaves the schedule. |
| Words are recalled but not used appropriately | Item retrieval and contextual language use are different capabilities; ask for meaning and use in a new conversational context. | Add contextual practice if recall succeeds but use fails. Do not claim that a word-pair recall study validates conversation transfer. |

If six of ten words are recalled today, that is a sample response, not a calibrated probability of recall next week. For a learner valuing conversation, record the designer's recall criterion (for example eight of ten is a local choice), the sampled prompts and the one-week horizon; also agree how appropriate use will be judged in a representative conversation. Do not silently substitute recall for that valued goal. If these fields are missing, elicit them before choosing a calendar schedule.

## Evidence-bounded expectation

A word-pair experiment separated total retrieval spacing from its relative arrangement: after first correct recall, repeated retrievals with intervening trials improved one-week recall relative to massed retrieval. At matched total nominal spacing, expanding, equal and contracting arrangements did not differ detectably: [absolute versus relative spacing observation](../claims/total-retrieval-spacing-beats-massed-but-relative-schedule-not-distinguished.md) [~W]. The full source was checked. This does not establish equivalence or an advantage for expanding gaps, a universal calendar gap, or conversational use. Retain total practice, corrective feedback, initial learning criterion, actual gaps and final-test horizon when comparing schedules. Relearning to a criterion in later sessions breaks that rule: a longer gap means more forgetting and so more relearning trials, which confounds spacing with the amount of practice, so a local comparison should give a fixed number of review trials instead ([Relearning-to-criterion designs confound spacing with practice amount](../claims/relearning-confound-distributed-practice-designs.md) [~W]; a methodological argument in a synthesis, not a tested result).

An equal schedule may be selected for practical reasons while this exact comparison remains unresolved; do not predict a gain from changing only equal to expanding gaps on this evidence. Calendar timing and the dose needed for a particular learner remain local questions. Lower practice fluency can coexist with better delayed performance, but difficulty alone does not identify the cause or predict the outcome.

**Default schedule, labelled as a design proposal.** No study in the wiki tested this combination as a whole; each part names its basis, and the [principle](../principles/spaced-learning.md) carries the evidence for the relationship itself. Use it where the brief gives no reason to do otherwise, and replace any part the brief settles.

1. **Session 1.** Introduce a capped set of new items (see the flashcard row below for why a cap), with one study pass and one recall attempt with corrective feedback per item. Separate an item's repetitions with other items rather than repeating it at once.
2. **Later sessions.** Hold the total practice time a massed plan would have used, and spread it over at least two later sessions on different days ([Spaced practice improves retention](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]). Each review is a recall attempt with feedback; where feedback cannot be immediate, releasing scores and worked solutions later the same day is enough to keep the review (the classroom-course row below). Use equal gaps unless items will be retrieved many times (the expanding-intervals row below).
3. **Gap length from the horizon.** In one large experiment with adults learning facts in two sessions, the best gap was about 20–40% of a one-week test delay and about 5–10% of a one-year delay, so roughly one to two days for a test a week away and three to five weeks for one a year away ([Spaced practice improves retention](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S]). Treat these as a starting point to check, not a rule.
4. **Progression.** Early sessions carry more new items and more feedback; later sessions are mostly reviews, with missed items getting more of the time than items already recalled after long gaps (the first-spaced-test row in the table above). Add a review close to the date of use.
5. **When to move on.** An item is retired from the schedule only when it is recalled unassisted after the longest gap planned for it; the gap and the criterion are local choices recorded with the design.

## Fitting the design to a situation

Ask for three facts when the brief omits them: **when the learners will need the material** (the delay to the test or use), **how many sessions and minutes the schedule can hold**, and **whether reviews can be recall attempts with feedback**.

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| A course of several weeks with a final exam | Plan each topic's first study and at least one later review in a different week; keep the total practice time the same rather than adding sessions | [Spaced practice improves retention](../claims/spaced-practice-improves-retention.md) [+S] |
| The exam or use is months away | Set longer gaps between first study and review for material needed later; review again closer to the date. Do not fix one gap for every course. In one experiment the best gap grew with the delay but shrank as a share of it (about 20–40% of a week, 5–10% of a year), and past the best gap recall declined slowly.
