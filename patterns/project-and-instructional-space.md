# Project Space and Instructional Space

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 18 claims (12 for, 5 mixed, 1 against) · 24 studies (9 quant-synthesis, 4 causal, 4 qualitative, 4 theoretical, 3 review), `q1`–`q4` · 9 of 24 report an effect size · 12 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable policy for a unit built around an authentic project in which each learner's component skills must be certified individually: **learners work on the project → when a learner meets a gap in a component skill, the project is paused ("frozen") → the learner moves to a separate instructional space that tells, shows and gives practice with feedback on that one skill across varied situations → the learner meets an explicit criterion (with a speed criterion where the skill must become automatic) → the project is resumed and the new skill is used in it → repeat at the next gap**. The intended changes are individual mastery of the component skills (a `procedure` or `verbal-association` knowledge, and the `principle` behind them, checked on each learner, not on the team product), their use in the project and in new tasks of the same kind (`near-transfer`), and, where stated, fluent performance of routines.

The design is Reigeluth's (2011) answer to four weaknesses he names in problem-based instruction: a team product hides whether every member learned; one project gives too few uses of each skill for transfer; projects build no automaticity; and unguided searching for information wastes time. It sits where three converted pages meet, and does not repeat their models. The [Problem-based Learning pattern](problem-based-learning.md) owns how much guidance a problem-centred unit gives and when; the [Mastery Learning pattern](mastery-learning.md) owns the gate-correct-recheck cycle; and the [4C/ID pattern](4cid-four-component-instructional-design.md) owns whole learning tasks with just-in-time procedural information and part-task practice for routines. This page owns the narrower configuration: **a project that can be paused, a separate instructional space entered on a diagnosed gap, and a criterion that gates the return**.

**No claim in this wiki tests the project-space and instructional-space design as such**, against an unmodified project unit or anything else. The claims that bear on it test its parts: project-based units against conventional teaching; unguided against guided discovery; mastery gating against conventional pacing; computer tutoring against other instruction; individual accountability in group work; and problem solving before instruction against instruction first. Each is carried to this pattern by extrapolation, and the page says how far. The sequence below is the earlier page's, with doses added; each step states its evidence status, and the switching rules are **untested design proposals**.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Before the project starts, have each learner try the component skills the project will need (a short task for each, done alone) and one small whole-task attempt. Record which skills each learner can already do, how accurately and how fast, and where the whole-task attempt stalls. Preserve unknowns rather than labelling learners `novice` or `advanced` from a course title. |
| The project and its component skills | The project's product or performance, who it is for, and how long it runs. List the component skills it needs and sort each by kind: facts to recall, routines to apply (and whether they must become automatic), concepts to understand, causal or theoretical understanding. Mark which gaps are predictable (most learners will hit them) and which are not. |
| Context and activities | Setting (`classroom`, `online-self-paced`, `online-instructor-led`, `workplace-clinical`, or a mix), `single-session` or `days-weeks`, team or individual projects, group size, whether a simulation lets the project be paused with its state saved, and who delivers the instructional space (a tutoring system, a teacher, a peer, a job aid). How much facilitator time there is per learner per session. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask what the learner wants from the project (a working product, a qualification, a job skill, a real client served). Record where it diverges from the designer objective, for instance a learner who values finishing the product over practising a component to criterion. |
| Designer objective | Which of these the unit is judged on: individual mastery of named component skills, quality of the team product, transfer to a new project, or automaticity of named routines. Name the one the assessment will weigh. |
| Outcome | An individual check of each component skill on new items, separate from the team product; a measure of use in a new project or task of the same kind (`near-transfer`); a timed check where automaticity is the goal; immediate or `delayed`. Whether the check is written locally or is a standardized test, since mastery and tutoring effects were much smaller on standardized tests. |

A request to "add an instructional space to our project unit" does not specify these. Ask what the component skills are, who will diagnose a gap and deliver the instruction, and what criterion gates the return before building the instructional space or setting the freeze rules.

## Sequence and conditional policy

The default sequence is the earlier page's, with doses and a progression across sessions added. Steps 3, 5 and 7 have a neighbouring claim, carried by extrapolation; steps 1, 2, 4 and 6 are untested here, and every quantity is a labelled proposal.

1. **Launch the project and map its skills** (first session, about 20–30 minutes; proposal). Give the authentic task, its audience, the product, and a sequence of three to five milestones that each build toward it. Run the starting check on component skills (inputs, above) and plan a freeze point for each predictable gap. Evidence status: untested; the milestone advice rests on [student focus groups after project-based computing courses](../claims/student-engagement-authenticity-clear-sequence.md) [+W], one qualitative evaluation coded `r1`, which reported preferences, not learning.
2. **Work in the project space** (segments of about 30–45 minutes per session; proposal). Learners or small teams work on the current milestone, ideally in a simulation that keeps the project's state when paused. In a real-world project, keep the instructional space on a phone or laptop beside the work. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
3. **Freeze at a diagnosed gap, not at every difficulty.** Pause when the learner cannot proceed because a component skill is missing and a short check confirms it (step 4), or at a planned freeze point. For a `principle` (an idea or causal model) the project needs and learners can make a tractable attempt at, let them try first for a bounded time (about 10–15 minutes; proposal) and then teach from their attempts, rather than freezing at the first stall. For a routine or fact, freeze promptly. Evidence status: [Unguided search learns less than explicit instruction, while guided discovery outperforms other instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [+S] supports replacing unguided search with targeted instruction; [problem solving before instruction](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [~S] qualifies when to freeze for conceptual targets (benefit on conceptual knowledge and transfer, none on procedures, reversed for second to fifth graders).
4. **Diagnose the gap** (2–5 minutes; proposal). Give the learner one or two items of the suspected component skill outside the project. If they can do it in isolation, the gap is elsewhere (recognising when to use the skill, planning the next step, or the project being too hard) and the learner returns to the project with a prompt rather than entering the instructional space. Evidence status: untested proposal.
5. **Instruct and practise in the instructional space** (about 10–25 minutes per gap; proposal). Tell, show and give practice with immediate feedback on that one skill, across at least three different situations rather than only the project's. Match the method to the kind of skill: drill with chunking and spaced repetition for facts; a rule with worked examples and practice items for a routine; analogies and contrasted cases for a concept; tracing causes and effects for causal understanding. For a routine that is too complex to practise whole, practise its parts first. Evidence status: [tutoring systems that adjust to each learner raised achievement over large-group teaching, computer instruction and textbooks, though not over human tutors or small groups](../claims/adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+S]; [isolating parts of a complex task reduced load for novices](../claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices.md) [+M] (one experiment); [practice across varied tasks of a type for transfer](../claims/task-variety-enables-transfer-same-task-type.md) [+W] (a conceptual paper).
6. **Gate the return on a criterion** (proposal: at least 4 of 5 new items correct, drawn from at least two situations not used in practice; where the routine must become automatic, also a time limit set from what a competent performer takes). A learner who misses the criterion twice gets different instruction (a human explanation, a different example) before a third attempt, not the same practice again. Evidence status: [mastery learning programmes raised examination scores over conventional pacing](../claims/mastery-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]; [effects were larger with a higher mastery standard and smaller on standardized tests](../claims/mastery-learning-effects-vary-with-standard-pacing-and-test-type.md) [~M]. The criterion's numbers are a proposal; no claim here sets them.
7. **Unfreeze and use the skill in the project in the same session**, then continue to the next gap. At each milestone, check every team member individually on the component skills used so far, and base part of the team's grade on members' individual results. Evidence status: [in school cooperative learning, rewards based on each member's individual learning raised achievement more than a single group product](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S], carried from structured school group work to project teams.

**Progression across sessions** (proposal). In the first one or two sessions, the teacher or system triggers freezes at the planned points and the instructional space carries most of the teaching. In middle sessions, learners request a freeze themselves and the diagnostic check decides whether they enter. In late sessions, learners use a reference or job aid inside the project for skills already mastered, and the instructional space shrinks to short timed reviews of earlier skills. A learner moves on to the next milestone when its product meets the milestone description and every component used in it has passed its gate; a team moves to the next project when every member has. Keep milestone deadlines common to the class: in college mastery programmes, self-paced versions [lowered course completion](../claims/self-paced-mastery-programs-reduce-college-course-completion.md) [-M] and [produced smaller effects than group-paced ones](../claims/mastery-learning-effects-vary-with-standard-pacing-and-test-type.md) [~M].

| Observation | Discriminating probe | Proposed action, with its uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| A learner stalls in the project | Give one or two items of the suspected component skill on their own. | If they fail, freeze and enter the instructional space. If they pass, the stall may be in recognising when the skill applies or in planning; give a prompt in the project ("which of the skills you have would get you past this?"). Neither branch is tested. |
| Passes the gate but does not use the skill in the project | Ask the learner to say where in the project the skill applies, then watch them try. | If they cannot say where, add practice items set in project-like situations. If they can say where but still do not use it, the gate items may have been too narrow; widen them. Proposed, untested. |
| Team product is good, one member fails the individual milestone check | Ask that member to carry out one part of the product alone while explaining it. | If they cannot, the team divided the labour so that member never practised; route them through the instructional space for those skills and assign them that part next time. If they can, the check may not match the project; review it. |
| A learner freezes again and again on the same project | Count the distinct skills behind the freezes. | Many different skills: the project is too hard for this learner now; give a simpler project of the same kind or more planned freeze points. The same skill again: the instruction did not hold; change the method, not the dose. |
| Accurate but slow on a routine that must become automatic | Time the routine alone and inside the project. | Slow alone: more spaced, timed practice in the instructional space. Fast alone, slow in the project: the load is in combining it with other steps; practise it inside short whole tasks. Proposed, untested. |
| A learner treats the instructional space as the whole course and avoids the project | Ask what they want from the unit. | If they value certification over the product, show how milestones count; if the project feels pointless, revisit its audience and purpose with them. |

## Fitting the design to a situation

**The three facts that most change the decision:**

- **What kind of skill each gap is.** Routines and facts benefit from prompt instruction and practice to criterion; for concepts, an attempt before instruction helped conceptual knowledge and transfer in mathematics and science studies, though not for younger children. If unstated, ask: is this gap something to recall or carry out, or an idea to understand, and how old are the learners?
- **Who can diagnose a gap and deliver the instruction.** The design needs a tutoring system, a teacher with time, or prepared self-study materials with a check. Tutoring systems did better than large-group teaching but not better than human tutors or small groups. If unstated, ask: when a learner is stuck, who will notice, check it and teach the skill, and how many minutes do they have per learner?
- **Whether individual mastery or the team product is the target.** If only the product is graded, nothing certifies each member. If unstated, ask: what will each learner have to show alone, and what counts for the team?

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **The project space alone.** [Project-based learning improves achievement](../claims/project-based-learning-improves-achievement.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 30 journal articles (46 effect sizes, 12,585 students) found project-based learning ahead of traditional teaching (d+ = 0.71), moderated by subject area, school location, instructional hours and IT support. The table on that claim page counts **3 distinct studies** behind its question. Against it, [the overall PBL effect in a re-analysis of tutor-background data was modest (g = 0.27) with large heterogeneity (I² = 80%)](../claims/pbl-overall-effect-modest-large-heterogeneity.md) [~M], and [PBL may have a null or negative effect on basic knowledge but a positive effect on conceptual understanding and transfer, relative to direct instruction](../claims/pbl-null-basic-knowledge-positive-transfer.md) [~M] (Kapur's 2016 reading of earlier meta-analyses; **3 distinct studies** in that claim's table). That pattern of outcomes is the case for adding an instructional space for basic knowledge while keeping the project for application; it is not evidence that the addition works.
- **Guidance in place of unguided search.** [Minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction](../claims/minimal-guidance-less-effective-for-novices.md) [+S]: across 580 comparisons explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (d = 0.38), and discovery enhanced with feedback, worked examples or scaffolding beat other instruction (360 comparisons, d = 0.30); its table counts **5 distinct studies**. [Computer-based scaffolding had its largest effect in project-based contexts (ḡ = 1.33)](../claims/project-based-context-highest-scaffolding-effect.md) [+M], one meta-analysis's moderator analysis in STEM.
- **Gating the return on a criterion.** [Mastery learning improves outcomes](../claims/mastery-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+M]: across 103 studies with examination outcomes, mastery programmes raised scores by 0.52 SD; its table counts **8 distinct studies**. [Effects were larger with a higher unit standard and with group-based pacing, smaller where the comparison group also got quiz feedback, and near zero on the few standardized tests](../claims/mastery-learning-effects-vary-with-standard-pacing-and-test-type.md) [~M]. These were whole-course mastery programmes, not gates inside a project.
- **Delivering the instructional space by computer.** [Intelligent tutoring systems improve achievement over non-individualised instruction](../claims/adaptive-learning-improves-outcomes.md) [+S]: tutoring systems beat teacher-led large-group instruction (g = 0.42), other computer instruction (g = 0.57) and textbooks (g = 0.35), with no advantage over individual human tutoring (g = −0.11) or small groups (g = 0.05); a second meta-analysis found a median 0.66 SD that depended on whether tests were local or standardized. A [review's secondhand report of the same finding](../claims/its-meta-analytic-positive-outcomes.md) [+W] adds nothing independent.
- **Individual accountability in teams.** [Group rewards based on every member's individual learning](../claims/cooperative-learning-group-rewards-and-individual-accountability.md) [+S]: in 99 school studies of at least four weeks, a median effect of +.32 against +.07 for methods with a single group product or no group reward; its table counts **9 distinct studies**. School cooperative learning, not project teams.
- **When not to freeze straight away.** [Problem solving followed by instruction beat instruction first on conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36), with no difference on procedures (g = −0.03), reversing for second to fifth graders and domain-general skills](../claims/productive-failure-improves-conceptual-learning.md) [~S]; its table counts **8 distinct studies**. [Guidance during the attempt produced better solutions but no better posttest](../claims/initial-guidance-does-not-improve-learning-from-instruction.md) [~M]. This is the main qualification of the pattern: a freeze at every stall would remove the attempt that helped conceptual learning in these studies.
- **Practice of parts and of whole tasks.** [Part-task practice reduces load for novices](../claims/part-task-practice-reduces-load-for-novices.md) [+M] (one experiment, no effect size on the page); [whole-task practice improves transfer](../claims/whole-task-performance-improves-transfer.md) [+W] and [varied tasks of a type enable transfer](../claims/task-variety-enables-transfer-same-task-type.md) [+W] are design arguments, not tests.
- **The cost of self-pacing.** [In 32 college studies of the self-paced Personalized System of Instruction, 23 found higher completion in the conventional class (h = −0.14)](../claims/self-paced-mastery-programs-reduce-college-course-completion.md) [-M]; the examination advantage was not explained by dropout. A learner-paced instructional space needs common deadlines.

Do not rank this design against an unmodified project unit or against direct instruction from these claims: none compares them, none measures the time pausing and resuming costs, and several outcomes in the mastery and tutoring studies were tests written for the course. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (the same project with and without a gated instructional space, on individual component checks and on a new project, at a delay) and the local observation that would stand in for it: each learner's gate results and their use of the skills in the next project.

## Further evidence, not yet read against this model

Claims this page cited before the 2026-10-15 rewrite, both now also used above for one point each. Neither tests the pattern.

- [Students responded most positively to projects that felt authentic and were organized in a clear sequence with manageable milestones](../claims/student-engagement-authenticity-clear-sequence.md) [+W] — post-project focus groups in one evaluation of project-based computing courses (qualitative, `r1`); bears on how the project space is staged (milestones), not on the instructional space or on learning.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested course.* Trainee pharmacy technicians, adults in a `workplace-clinical` programme taken partly from home, work in pairs on a six-week project: running a simulated community dispensary through a week of prescriptions, with a real pharmacist reviewing their final log. The starting check shows most can read a prescription but few can do dose calculations accurately or quickly, and the pharmacist needs calculation and label checking to be automatic. The table changed the default: calculation and label checking get planned freeze points and a timed gate (4 of 5 new calculations correct within a time a qualified technician takes), because they are routines that must be fast; drug-interaction reasoning, a concept, gets a 15-minute attempt in the simulation before instruction, since these are adults; each pair's grade includes each member's individual gate results, because the pair could otherwise divide the calculations to one person; the simulation saves the dispensary's state when a trainee freezes, and the instructional space runs on the trainees' phones so those joining from home freeze the same way. Facilitator time is one tutor for twenty trainees, so the tutor sees only trainees who miss a gate twice. One trainee says she wants to be trusted on the counter by her supervisor, which matches the automaticity gate; her partner wants to finish the log early, so the tutor shows him that the final review checks each member's calculations.

Record: **project and milestone → component skill and its kind → starting check result → stall observed in the project → diagnostic item result → freeze or prompt → method used in the instructional space, minutes, and situations practised → gate attempts, accuracy and time → use of the skill after unfreezing → individual milestone check against the team product → candidate interpretations (missing skill, not recognising when to use it, project too hard, divided labour) and their basis → reobservation in the next project or at a delay**. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of mastery without a calibrated model.

## Elements and limits

[Simulation](../elements/simulation.md), [just-in-time information](../elements/just-in-time-information.md), [just-in-time learning](../elements/just-in-time-learning.md), [demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md), [worked examples](../elements/worked-examples.md), [practice](../elements/practice.md), [part-task practice](../elements/part-task-practice.md), [feedback](../elements/feedback.md), [mastery criterion with remediation](../elements/mastery-criterion-remediation.md), [automaticity](../elements/automaticity.md), [individual accountability](../elements/individual-accountability.md), [scaffolding](../elements/scaffolding.md), [assessment](../elements/assessment.md) and [application](../elements/application.md). Principles the pattern draws on: [Problem-based Learning](../principles/problem-based-learning.md), [Mastery Learning](../principles/mastery-learning.md), [Adaptive Learning](../principles/adaptive-learning.md), [Deliberate Practice](../principles/deliberate-practice.md) and [Transfer of Learning](../principles/transfer-of-learning.md).

This pattern is scoped to projects that can be paused and whose component skills can be named, diagnosed and checked one by one. Projects whose value lies in an unbroken performance, or whose skills cannot be separated from the whole, need the whole-task configurations in the [4C/ID pattern](4cid-four-component-instructional-design.md) or [cognitive apprenticeship](cognitive-apprenticeship.md). The policy supports observation and design reasoning; whether a project with a gated instructional space produces better individual mastery and transfer than the same project without it, for which learners, at what time cost and at what horizon, remains to be tested.

## Related Patterns
- [Epistemic Games](epistemic-games.md) — both embed learning within an authentic simulated task, though epistemic games focus on inhabiting a professional identity rather than alternating with a separate diagnostic instructional space
- [Problem-based Learning](problem-based-learning.md) — the project space on its own, with the converted policy on how much guidance a problem-centred unit gives and when
- [Mastery Learning](mastery-learning.md) — the gate-correct-recheck cycle this pattern uses to decide when a learner returns to the project
- [4C/ID](4cid-four-component-instructional-design.md) — whole learning tasks with just-in-time procedural information and part-task practice; the closest relative, which keeps instruction inside the task rather than in a separate space

## Description
Reigeluth (2011) identifies four persistent weaknesses of problem-based instruction (PBI): team-based assessment makes it hard to verify that *every* individual learned the intended content (a "loafer" can ride the team's work); learners typically use a given skill only once or twice within a project, insufficient for the extensive practice complex skills need to transfer to new situations; PBI does not build automaticity for skills that need to be executed with reduced conscious effort; and unguided search for information within a project wastes significant learner time. His response is not to abandon PBI for direct instruction, but to architect two distinct, alternating spaces within the same learning experience.

Learners work in a **project space**: an authentic, ill-structured task, ideally in a computer-based simulation, using established project-based/problem-based design guidance (problem selection, group formation, tutor facilitation, authentic assessment, debriefing). Whenever a learner or team hits a learning gap, they **"freeze" the project** and enter an **instructional space** — individualized tutoring (conceivably delivered by a virtual mentor/avatar) that tells, shows, and gives practice with immediate feedback on the specific skill needed, across diverse situations, until the learner reaches an explicit mastery criterion (including a speed criterion when automaticity matters). Only then does the learner return to the unfrozen project to apply the new skill and continue until the next gap. Instructional strategy within the instructional space is matched to the type of learning involved: drill-and-practice (chunking, repetition, mnemonics) for memorization; tutorials with generality, examples, and feedback for skill application; analogies and advance organizers for conceptual understanding; and causal exploration (examining causes, effects, and solutions) for theoretical understanding.

This design directly answers PBI's four weaknesses: individual — not just team — mastery becomes assessable because the instructional space tracks each learner's own practice to criterion; transfer is supported because the instructional space provides repeated practice across diverse situations rather than the one or two uses a project alone would offer; automaticity becomes achievable via an explicit speed criterion; and efficiency improves because targeted instruction replaces unguided search, with assessment folded directly into the practice-to-criterion process rather than run as a separate activity.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A means of "freezing" the authentic project without losing learner engagement or project state — most naturally a computer-based simulation, though Reigeluth notes the project space could also be entirely real-world (with the instructional space then delivered on a mobile device) or a hybrid
- Individualized instructional content or tutoring capable of diagnosing exactly which skill gap a learner has hit and delivering targeted tell-show-practice-feedback for it
- An explicit mastery criterion (and, where relevant, a speed/automaticity criterion) that gates return to the project space
#### Constraints
- Requires substantially more instructional design investment than either a pure project-based unit or pure direct instruction alone, since both a project space and a parallel, diagnosable instructional space must be built
- The individualized diagnosis-and-tutoring step depends on either a sophisticated computer-based system or significant human tutoring capacity — without one of these, the "freeze and get individualized help" mechanic is difficult to deliver at any scale
#### Grain Size
- Unit
- Course

### Target Goals
- Preserving PBI's intrinsic motivation and authenticity while directly fixing its documented weaknesses in individual mastery assessment, transfer, automaticity, and efficiency
- Attainment-based (not time-based) progress, consistent with the broader [Learner-Centered Paradigm of Education](../theories/learner-centered-paradigm.md)

### Target Learners
- Learners working on complex, authentic projects who need certifiable individual mastery of component skills, not just a good team product

### Theory
#### Supporting

- [First Principles of Instruction](../theories/first-principles-of-instruction.md) [+S] — the instructional space's tell-show-practice-with-feedback structure, generalized across diverse situations, is a direct application of Merrill's demonstration and application principles
- [Cognitive Load Theory](../theories/cognitive-load-theory.md) [+M] — targeted instruction at the moment of an identified gap avoids the wasted search time and load PBI's unguided struggle imposes

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Explicitly framed as a response to documented weaknesses of unmodified problem-based instruction (team-based assessment hiding individual non-mastery, insufficient repetition for transfer, no automatization, and unguided-search inefficiency)

## Design

### Sequence
1. Place learners (individually or in small teams) into an authentic project, ideally within a computer-based simulation.
2. When a learner encounters a learning gap, "freeze" the project.
3. Diagnose the specific skill/knowledge gap and enter the instructional space: tell, show (for diverse situations), and provide practice with immediate feedback.
4. Continue practice until the learner reaches the mastery criterion for the skill (and a speed criterion, when automaticity is required).
5. Unfreeze the project and have the learner apply the newly mastered skill to continue the project.
6. Repeat the freeze/instruct/unfreeze cycle at each new learning gap.

### Affordances
- [Problem-based Learning](../principles/problem-based-learning.md)
- [Mastery Learning](../principles/mastery-learning.md)
- [First Principles of Instruction](../theories/first-principles-of-instruction.md)

### Personalization
- Each learner progresses through the instructional space at their own pace and only for the specific gaps they individually encounter, rather than a fixed group sequence
- The instructional strategy used within the instructional space adapts to the type of learning required (memorization, skill, conceptual, or theoretical understanding) rather than applying one method uniformly
