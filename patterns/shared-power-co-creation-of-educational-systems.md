# Shared power: previously excluded students, families, and communities co-create and shape educational systems, policies, and practices

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 10 claims (8 for, 1 against, 1 unmarked) · 9 studies (7 qualitative, 1 review, 1 design), `q1`–`q2` · 1 of 9 report an effect size · 10 claims rest on one study

## Description
The brief characterizes rightful presence by "a fundamental shift in power" in which students, families, and communities who most frequently experience systemic inequities become integral in the shaping and design of educational systems, policies, and practices. It promotes co-creation of school experiences benefiting students, educators, staff, families, and communities, requiring collective action across the system.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Systemic power dynamics need to be attended to, and often shifted
- All students have the inherent potential for meaningful learning when provided appropriate resources and support in an environment of true belonging
- A genuine, named double-bind: a group present in a process but structurally unable to exercise the process's actual decision-making mechanisms
- Institutional willingness to create a full, equal-standing deliberative body for the underrepresented group, not merely a listening session or advisory subcommittee
#### Constraints
- Creating these conditions requires collective action from all parts of an educational system
- Attending to and often shifting systemic power dynamics is required to ensure equitable policies and practices

# Differentiated Activity Structures for Inclusive Participation

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 1 claim (1 unmarked) · 1 study (1 qualitative), `q2` · 1 of 1 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
Deliberative and participatory learning processes (civic, community, or organizational) that aim to diversify participation often face a **double-bind**: naive inclusion — outreach plus sequential interpretation into an otherwise unchanged process — gets underrepresented participants into the room but not into the decision-making, because the tools, pacing, and norms of the existing activity structure were built around the majority group's language and interactional style. Attendees experience this as tokenism and stop attending. The alternative this pattern documents is a **redesigned activity structure**: a dedicated, full-standing deliberative body for the underrepresented group (not a subcommittee reporting to the "main" one), simultaneous deliberation in participants' own language rather than sequentially-interpreted turns in the majority language, flexible norms that let participants surface priorities outside the official agenda, and an explicit shift in participants' role from input-providers to decision-makers.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A genuine, named double-bind: a group present in a process but structurally unable to exercise the process's actual decision-making mechanisms
- Institutional willingness to create a full, equal-standing deliberative body for the underrepresented group, not merely a listening session or advisory subcommittee
#### Constraints
- Evidence is from a single embedded case study (one ward's participatory budgeting process, one research-practitioner as both facilitator and observer); mechanism is plausible but not tested against alternative designs (e.g., simultaneous interpretation without a dedicated body)
- Requires ongoing facilitation investment to sustain the dedicated space, not just a one-time structural change
#### Grain Size
Program (an ongoing civic or organizational participatory process, spanning multiple deliberation cycles)

## Design

### Sequence
1. Document the double-bind concretely: observe where the underrepresented group is present but drops out of the process's actual decision-making phase, and identify the specific interactional mismatch (e.g., sequential interpretation of long turns, agenda items that exclude community priorities).
2. Propose a dedicated deliberative body for the group, with equal decision-making standing to existing committees — not a sub-committee that reports upward.
3. Redesign core mediating tools: replace sequential interpretation with native-language deliberation; replace fixed agendas with flexible norms allowing community-specific concerns to surface.
4. Support role re-mediation explicitly: name and reinforce the shift from input-provider ("just sitting there") to decision-maker ("we get to decide"), including through facilitator framing and collective reflection time.
5. Track both collective-level indicators (sustained attendance, claims-making language) and system-level indicators (adoption of the new structure by other groups or units) as separate signals of success, since one can occur without the other.

### Affordances
- [Cultural-Historical Activity Theory](../theories/cultural-historical-activity-theory.md)

### Personalization
- Where a single dedicated body isn't feasible, the pattern's individual components (native-language deliberation, flexible agenda norms, explicit role re-mediation) may transfer partially, though this has not been tested in isolation from the full redesign.
