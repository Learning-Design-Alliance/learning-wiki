    Bioart Boundary-Crossing Making¶

    Pattern · All patterns

    Description¶

    Learners make art using or about living organisms (specimens, plants, biological processes), moving progressively through increasingly specialized institutional spaces — school grounds, a natural-history museum, a research laboratory — each introducing new tools, experts, and conventions. The project deliberately crosses institutional (school/museum/lab), interpersonal (student/researcher/artist), and intrapersonal (teacher-as-manager and teacher-as-learner) boundaries at once, brokered by a facilitator experienced in art-science collaboration. Low-threshold, student-buildable technology (a smartphone-based macro-photography rig) lets every learner participate regardless of prior expertise, while leaving room for learners who want more complexity (professional lab equipment) to pursue it.

    Implications¶

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • A broker with prior art-science collaboration experience who pre-negotiates constraints with each institution (e.g., lab safety rules, sterile-room access) before students arrive
    • Low-threshold, self-buildable tools that separate "can I use this?" from "how far do I want to take this?" so learners of any prior skill level can start
    • Explicit teacher role flexibility — teachers act as logistical organizers and openly adopt a learner role alongside domain experts
    • Reflective discussion embedded immediately around, not scheduled apart from, hands-on material encounters

    Constraints¶

    • This is a small, intensive case (a single one-week project, 6 students); findings describe what happened in this project, not a generalizable causal mechanism
    • Bioart's use of living organisms raises genuine ethical questions (which organisms may be used, how) that the design should expect learners to grapple with, not treat as a side issue
    • Depends on access to a willing institutional partner (a research lab, a museum) and a broker who can negotiate that access — not available in every context

    Grain Size¶

    • Unit (the source study's project ran one intensive week)

    Target Goals¶

    • Building holistic engagement with nature across material, experiential, cognitive, emotional, and philosophical dimensions simultaneously, rather than sequentially
    • Multivoiced boundary crossing supports holistic nature connection and ethical reflection

    Target Learners¶

    • Studied with upper-secondary students; the progressive-scaffolding and low-threshold-technology structure is not level-specific in principle

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Boundary Crossing Learning — the project is explicitly structured around institutional, interpersonal, and intrapersonal boundary crossing

    Contradicting / Qualifying¶

    • None identified in the source study

    Claims¶

    • Multivoiced boundary crossing supports holistic nature connection and ethical reflection [+W]

    Design¶

    Sequence¶

    1. Orientation: learners assemble a DIY macro-photography rig from simple materials (acrylic sheet, magnifying lens, smartphone) and freely explore familiar school grounds with it.
    2. First expansion: visit a natural-history museum; bioart history introduced; guided exploration; meet a practicing bioartist (the broker).
    3. Second expansion: visit an authentic research lab; meet a researcher; observe professional conventions; try small-scale lab techniques (e.g., a "plant listening" activity using contact microphones on live plants).
    4. Sharing: guided tour of the research institution; students present in-progress work to other visiting groups; interact with additional invited experts.
    5. Synthesis: final studio day for editing, printing, and curating artifacts for a public gallery/blog.
    6. Throughout: teachers and researchers prompt reflective discussion immediately following hands-on encounters, rather than deferring reflection to a separate session.

    Affordances¶

    • Hands-on Learning
    • Broker-Facilitated Cross-Domain Integration

    Personalization¶

    • The specific institutional partners (museum, lab) and art medium can vary; what matters structurally is progressive institutional expansion, a broker who pre-negotiates access, and low-threshold tools that scale to learner interest
    • Learners can be given latitude over which organisms they engage with, since ethical reasoning about this choice was itself a productive part of the source study's learning

    Related Patterns¶

    • Problem-Based Learning (PBL) — shares an inquiry-driven structure but is organized around progressive institutional access and material engagement rather than a single driving problem

    Examples¶

    • Six upper-secondary students, one intensive week, moving from a DIY smartphone macro rig on school grounds to professional lab micropropagation techniques and a public gallery exhibition; several students spontaneously restricted their own material choices (e.g., "plants only") based on ethical concern about using animal specimens.

    Key Sources¶

    • Liukkonen, P., Vartiainen, H., Pöllänen, S., & Kokko, S. (2023). Interacting with nature in and through boundary crossing learning: A case of bioart-making. Journal of the Learning Sciences, 32(4-5), 534-572. https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2023.2263845
    • Ives, C. D., Freeth, R., & Fischer, J. (2020). Inside-out sustainability: The neglect of inner worlds. Ambio, 49, 208-217.
