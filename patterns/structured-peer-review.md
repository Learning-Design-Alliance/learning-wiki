# Structured Peer Review

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 50 claims (31 for, 19 mixed) · 58 studies (18 causal, 11 quant-synthesis, 9 associational, 8 review, 4 qualitative, 4 design, 4 theoretical), `q1`–`q4` · 13 of 58 report an effect size · 36 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable policy for improving a draft through peers' criterion-referenced comments, and for building reviewers' sense of quality by having them write those comments: **a reviewable draft (an essay, a proposal, a prototype, a recorded performance) and a short set of criteria → reviewers practise on an anchor draft of known quality → each reviewer reads one to three peers' drafts and writes comments that name a criterion, point to a place in the draft and suggest a change → the author reads the comments, decides which to act on and writes a revision plan → the author revises before the work is judged → both roles reflect on what they will do differently in their own next draft**. The intended change is in the quality of the revised work (`skill-performance`, scored on the task's own rubric), in each reviewer's own later work (`near-transfer` to their next draft), and, as a by-product, in how accurately and helpfully learners comment on drafts (`assessment-accuracy`).

It sits beside two converted pages and does not repeat them. The [collaborative evaluation pattern](collaborative-evaluation.md) owns learning to judge quality: judging alone, comparing judgments in a group and checking them against a reference. This page starts where that one ends, with a single author's draft and comments meant to change it; where reviewers cannot yet tell strong from weak work, run collaborative evaluation on anchor drafts first. The [Assessment for Learning principle](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) owns the general relationship (evidence read against shared criteria, low stakes, a revision step before the outcome is judged); this pattern is one configuration of it in which the evidence is read by peers. The [Peer Feedback/Peer Review principle](../principles/peer-learning.md) and [Process-Based Writing](../principles/process-based-writing.md) are the principles it applies; the [writing workshop](workshop-model.md) is a larger routine that can contain it.

**No claim in this wiki tests structured peer review as a whole**: none compares the full cycle here (calibration, criterion-referenced comments, required revision, reflection) with a version lacking one of its parts, or with teacher comments alone, on the revised work. The claims that bear on it are of five kinds: peer feedback on writing against no feedback, self-assessment or teacher feedback (one meta-analysis, `postsecondary`); peer assessment against no assessment or teacher assessment on academic performance (one meta-analysis); whether giving feedback goes with gains in one's own work (one meta-analysis pooling both roles and one correlational study); what rubric guidance changes in reviewers' comments and ratings (two quasi-experiments); and how accurate and helpful secondary-school reviewers' comments are (two correlational studies). Smaller, weaker sets shape single steps and rows: which comments given and received go with later project scores (one correlational study of 181 secondary students online), what reviewers attend to while reviewing (one think-aloud study of twelve), and how peer marks compare with instructors' and what biases them (tool evaluations read second-hand in one systematic review, and observational analyses of MOOC peer grading). One claim limits the model: in the peer-assessment meta-analysis, a script or rubric did not raise the effect over freeform review. Further claims bear on single steps rather than the cycle: rubric-referenced self-assessment of one's own draft, contingent fading of support in tutoring, the specificity of AI-generated feedback and the revisions that follow it, a theoretical case for training reviewers, and students' doubts about novice peers in a technical subject. Each is carried to this pattern by extrapolation, and the page says how far. The sequence below is the earlier page's five steps, with a calibration step and a revision plan added, each labelled with its evidence status; the response-dependent policy is an **untested design proposal**.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Before the first review, each learner's written comments on one anchor draft whose quality the teacher has already judged: how many comments name a criterion, point to a place and suggest a change, and whether the problems they flag are real ones. Record prior knowledge of the domain and genre, age band, language of instruction, confidence in writing or in critiquing, and status or friendship differences in the class. Preserve unknowns rather than labelling learners `novice`. |
| The draft and the task | What is reviewed (essay, report, design, code, presentation), how long it takes to read, whether its quality turns on correctness (a calculation, a factual or technical claim) or on qualities informed readers weigh (argument, structure, audience fit), and how far it is from finished. A draft that cannot still change is not worth reviewing for the author. |
| Criteria and review prompts | Who writes them (given, co-constructed from contrasting exemplars, or chosen by the author), how many, whether each has a described level and an anchor example, whether they are simple and objective (format, presence of a citation) or subjective and complex (quality of reasoning), and what the comment prompt asks for. |
| Context and activities | Setting (`classroom`, `online-instructor-led`, `online-self-paced`, `workplace-clinical`), `single-session` or `days-weeks`, how many reviewers per draft, named or anonymous, synchronous or asynchronous, where comments are written, how much time authors have to revise, and whether a teacher sees the comments. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask each author what they want from the review (a better grade, a clearer argument for a real reader, to know whether the draft works at all, reassurance) and each reviewer what they expect to get from reviewing. Record disagreement with the designer objective. |
| Designer objective | Which capability is the target: a better final version of this piece, better own writing on the next piece, accurate and useful commenting, or the habit of using comments to revise. Name the one the assessment will weigh. |
| Outcome | The revised draft scored on the task rubric against the first draft (`skill-performance`), at the final submission; the reviewer's own next draft (`near-transfer`); or comment quality scored against a teacher's reading of the same draft (`assessment-accuracy`). The amount of revision is not this outcome: authors often revise in response to comments of mixed quality. |

A request to "add peer review to the course" does not specify these. Ask what draft, against which criteria, how many reviewers, how much time to revise and what will be scored before choosing anonymity, prompt format or the number of cycles.

## Sequence and conditional policy

**One review cycle (about 60–75 minutes of contact time over two meetings, or the same steps spread over four to seven days online; proposed):**

1. **Collect a draft that can still change (before the session).** Set the draft deadline far enough before the final deadline that authors have two to five days to revise. Evidence status: untested; the earlier page's step 1. The writing meta-analysis and the peer-assessment meta-analysis both compared feedback on work that was then revised or retested, not on finished work.
2. **Calibrate on an anchor draft (15 minutes; early cycles only).** Give three or four criteria, each with a described level. The teacher thinks aloud through one anchor draft: criterion, place in the draft, level, and one change that would raise it. Reviewers then write comments on a second anchor and compare them with the teacher's. Evidence status: [rater training in peer assessment](../claims/peer-assessment-benefits-assessor.md) [+M] was the strongest moderator of peer assessment's effect in a 58-study meta-analysis; [guided-rubric training](../claims/rubrics-improve-peer-feedback-quality.md) [+M] helped MOOC writing raters place samples at the right level, mostly on subjective, complex criteria. That short, task-aligned training units make peer comments more specific, criterion-referenced and evidence-based than a rubric with generic guidance is [an expectation stated for EFL speaking classes](../claims/trainer-expected-improve-comment-quality.md) [+W], derived from earlier writing studies and not tested in that article. Why anchors may help: in a think-aloud study of twelve students giving feedback on argumentative texts, [eight compared the draft with their own picture of what such a text should look like](../claims/internal-quality-criteria-guide-error-identification.md) [+W], and that comparison was what let them identify and evaluate errors; calibration aims to make that picture shared and accurate. Qualitative, no comparison. The earlier page's step 2.
3. **Review two or three peers' drafts in writing (20–30 minutes per draft), so that each draft receives two or three reviews.** Each comment names a criterion, quotes or points to a place, and suggests a change; each review opens with at least one thing that works and should stay, because reviewers left to themselves attend to errors. No marks. Evidence status: [peer feedback on writing](../claims/peer-feedback-improves-writing.md) [+M]: across 24 higher-education studies, writers who gave and/or received structured peer feedback improved their writing more than no-feedback controls (g = 0.91) and than self-assessment only (g = 0.33); the nature of the feedback moderated the effect and the number of peers did not. [Peer assessment](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M] improved academic performance over no assessment across 54 studies (g = 0.31). Several reviews per draft rest on [one small SWoRD experiment](../claims/multiple-peer-reviews-beat-single-expert-reviewer-sword.md) [+W] (28 students, reported in a systematic review with no effect size): drafts improved most when the author received several peer reviews and least with a single expert reviewer. Asking for problems and suggestions follows [Lu & Law's online peer assessment study](../claims/giving-suggestions-predicts-assessor-performance.md) [+W] (181 secondary students), where the number of suggestions and of [problem-identifying comments](../claims/giving-problem-identification-feedback-predicts-assessor-performance.md) [+W] a student gave predicted their own project score; the "works and should stay" opening follows the same study's finding that [positive affective comments received](../claims/receiving-positive-affective-feedback-predicts-assessee-performance.md) [+W] predicted the author's project score, and a think-aloud study in which twelve students [focused more on errors than on what worked](../claims/students-focus-more-on-errors-than-pluses.md) [~W], both when giving and when processing feedback. All three are correlational or descriptive. The two-to-three-draft load is a proposal. The earlier page's step 3.
4. **Author reads, chooses and plans (10 minutes).** The author sorts each comment into "will act on", "will not, because…", and "need to ask", writes a two- or three-line revision plan, and rates each review's usefulness (helpful, partly, not) with one reason the reviewer will see; where comments conflict, the author decides by the criteria and may ask the teacher. Evidence status: untested proposal. The usefulness rating is carried from [peerScholar](../claims/peerscholar-accountability-improves-expert-peer-correlation.md) [+W], where letting students grade the reviews they received raised a low correlation between expert and peer marks (one tool evaluation, reported second-hand, no coefficient printed); it is an accountability move for reviewers, not shown to change the revision. It is added because comments are often not acted on: in [one classroom study of feedback in a digital game](../claims/critical-constructive-feedback-is-neglected-at-multiple-stages.md) [~M], only 4.5% of feedback instances to fifth-graders went from being noticed to measurable progress, with the largest loss between reading and acting. That was an on-screen agent's feedback, not a peer's. The rationale for writing a plan is [self-monitoring](../claims/self-monitoring-improves-self-regulation.md) [+M]: a review and a theoretical synthesis argue that feedback becomes useful when learners compare their current performance with a target and revise; neither studies peer review.
5. **Revise before the work is judged (two to five days).** Before submitting, the author scores the revision against the same criteria. The final version is submitted with the revision plan, and the teacher scores the final version, not the comments. Evidence status: carried from the comparisons in steps 3 and 7; no claim isolates the revision requirement. The self-check is carried from [rubric-referenced self-assessment](../claims/rubrics-improve-student-work.md) [+M]: in two non-randomised studies (162 middle-school writers; 218 pre-service teachers), self-scoring one's own draft against a rubric came with higher-quality work, with no standardised effect size; the second also found more stress and a performance-avoidance orientation. The earlier page's step 4.
6. **Reflect in both roles (5–10 minutes).** Each learner writes one thing they changed because of a comment and one thing they noticed in a peer's draft that they will use or avoid in their own next draft. Evidence status: untested; the earlier page's step 5.
7. **Check the reviewer's own next draft.** Score each learner's next piece on the same criteria they reviewed against. Evidence status: [the assessor benefit](../claims/peer-assessment-benefits-assessor.md) [+M]: in 43 undergraduate teacher-education students, the quality of feedback a student gave predicted their own final project, controlling for its initial quality, while the feedback received did not; correlational, so it supports including the reviewer role, not a claim that reviewing causes the gain. [In 181 secondary students' online peer assessment](../claims/cognitive-feedback-benefits-assessors-not-assessees.md) [~W], the same pattern held: giving suggestions and problem-identifying comments predicted the giver's project score, while the cognitive feedback a student received did not predict theirs, and the authors conclude weak students did not benefit from receiving more of it. That is the reason this step checks the reviewer's own work and not only the author's revision.

**Across cycles (four to six cycles over a term, proposed; untested):**

- **Cycles 1–2:** teacher-written criteria with anchors; calibration (step 2) every cycle; anonymous drafts; comment prompts with sentence frames ("Criterion…; at…; consider…"); the teacher reads a sample of comments and returns two or three to each reviewer with a note.
- **Cycles 3–4:** learners co-construct or refine one or two criteria from a strong and a weak exemplar; calibration shrinks to one anchor; frames optional; authors attach one question they want reviewers to answer.
- **Cycles 5–6:** named review where trust allows; authors choose the criteria they want feedback on; reviewers comment without frames; the teacher reads comments only where an author flags a disagreement.
- **Move on** to the next stage when, on two consecutive cycles, most of a reviewer's comments (for example, four of five) name a criterion, point to a place and suggest a change, and the teacher agrees the flagged problems are real. Keep a reviewer on anchors and frames while fewer than half do. Both thresholds are proposals to set locally. Withdrawing support as each reviewer's comments show they can do without it is carried from [contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [~M]: mostly small one-to-one tutoring studies (long division, the circulatory system), where support adjusted to each response beat fixed or absent support; in one meta-analysis explicit strategy training did better than scaffolding, and no study here fades support in peer review.

|---|---|---|
| Comments are praise or generic ("good flow", "fix grammar") | Ask the reviewer to point to one place in the draft for each comment, and to say which criterion it concerns. | If they can, the prompt is the problem: require criterion, place and suggestion in each comment. If they cannot find a place, the criterion may be beyond their domain knowledge: narrow the criteria or calibrate again on an anchor pair that differs only on it. Neither branch is tested for peers; [revising an AI-feedback prompt toward essay-grounded guidance](../claims/prompt-revision-increased-feedback-specificity.md) [+W] lowered the odds of low-specificity feedback in one semester's writing course, which is a prompt to a model, not to reviewers. |
| Comments flag problems that are not problems, or miss serious ones | Compare the reviewer's comments on an anchor with the teacher's. | If the reviewer misjudges the anchor too, criterion knowledge is missing: more calibration, and a teacher pass on drafts where correctness matters. If they judge anchors well but not peers', friendship or status may be colouring the review: make review anonymous. In two secondary-school writing studies, reviewers' own writing did not predict whether they found real problems, so do not assume stronger writers catch more. |
| Authors ignore the comments, or revise only surface features | Read the revision plan against the comments; ask the author why a substantive comment was not used. | If the author did not understand it, have reviewer and author talk for five minutes. If the author disagreed with reasons, that is a legitimate decision. If the author had no time, move the draft deadline earlier. If the comments themselves were all surface-level, ask each reviewer for at least one comment on meaning, organisation or argument. Proposed; in [one two-class quasi-experiment](../claims/revision-architecture-meaning-change-versus-surface.md) [+W], revisions after AI-supported diagnostic review held larger shares of meaning-change, organisation and argument work than control revisions, which stayed more surface-level; AI feedback, not peers', and no effect size. |
| Authors change everything a reviewer said, including wrong advice | Ask the author which criterion each change served. | If they cannot say, they may be deferring to any reader: keep the "will not, because…" column and ask for at least one reasoned rejection per cycle. Proposed. |
| Reviewers write useful comments but their own next draft does not improve | Ask what they saw in a peer's draft that they could use, and score their next draft on the same criteria. | If they cannot name anything, add the reflection step's second prompt and have them review their own draft against the criteria after reviewing a peer's. If they can, the gap may be in execution, not judgment: give practice on that criterion. The assessor benefit is correlational, so a null here is not surprising. |
| Peer scores (where reviewers also score) run high or low against the teacher's for some drafts | Score a sample of drafts yourself and compare; check whether the gap follows the reviewer's own attainment (strong reviewers marking weak drafts down, weak reviewers marking strong drafts up) or a shared group between reviewer and author. | If it follows attainment, assign each draft reviewers of mixed attainment and average their scores; if it follows a shared group, hide authors' names and origins and assign reviewers across groups; either way, spot-check a sample (reactive control) and tell reviewers their comments' quality is what counts (proactive control). Basis: in MOOC peer grading, [bias was least between similar-scoring peers](../claims/grader-grade-relates-to-grading-reliability.md) [~W], the best students marked the worst work down and the weakest marked the best work up, and [graders rated work from their own country 3.6% higher](../claims/in-group-favoritism-peer-grading-country.md) [~W]; both observational. The two kinds of [quality control](../claims/peer-assessment-quality-control-proactive-reactive.md) [+W] come from an inventory of peer-assessment practice, untested as a comparison. |
| Learners hold back criticism, or one learner's review hurts another | Compare named and anonymous comments where both are available; ask privately. | Make review anonymous to authors (the teacher still sees who wrote what), add a "what works and should stay" field, and keep marks out of review. [Anonymous sharing](../claims/anonymity-mitigates-evaluation-apprehension.md) [~W] is one course's authors' account, untested. |

## Fitting the design to a situation

**The three facts that most change the decision:**

- **Whether reviewers can see quality in this kind of draft.** Rater training was the strongest moderator of peer assessment's effect, and rubric guidance helped most on subjective, complex criteria; but stronger secondary-school writers found real problems no more often than weaker ones, only commented more helpfully. If unstated, ask: given one strong and one weak draft, can learners say which is stronger and point to why, without help?
- **Whether the author can still revise before the work is judged.** The tested comparisons are of feedback on work that was then improved or retested; comments on finished work have nowhere to go. If unstated, ask: how many days lie between the draft deadline and the final deadline, and will the final version be scored?
- **Whether quality turns on correctness or on judgment.** Where it turns on technical or factual correctness, peers can be confidently wrong; where it turns on structure, clarity and audience fit, peer comments are where the writing evidence lies. If unstated, ask: what does a serious error in this draft look like, and could a classmate spot it?

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Peer feedback on writing against none, self-assessment or the teacher.** [Peer Feedback Improves Writing](../claims/peer-feedback-improves-writing.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 24 quantitative studies of higher-education students' academic writing found that giving and/or receiving structured peer feedback improved writing more than no-feedback controls (g = 0.91, 95% CI [0.41, 1.42]) and than self-assessment only (g = 0.33, 95% CI [0.01, 0.64]); peer and teacher feedback did not differ reliably (g = 0.46, CI crossing zero), so peer feedback cannot be said to match the teacher's. The nature of the feedback moderated the effect; the number of peers did not. One meta-analysis (`q4`, `quant-synthesis r?`), `adult`, `postsecondary`; the authors note well-controlled primary studies are scarce. It does not separate giving from receiving, or isolate the revision step.
- **Peer assessment against none or teacher assessment.** [Peer assessment improves academic performance by a small average amount relative to no assessment or to teacher assessment](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-performance.md) [+M]: 54 control-group studies (k = 141) in primary, secondary and tertiary students; peer assessment (grading, peer dialogue, written feedback) improved academic performance over no assessment (g = 0.31) and over teacher assessment (g = 0.28), and did not differ significantly from self-assessment (g = 0.23); delivery mode, frequency and education level did not change it. The outcome is academic performance (tests, writing, practical skills), not the revised draft, and the roles are not separated. The [Peer Learning principle](../principles/peer-learning.md) also reads the wiki's one randomised test of peer assessment against instructor grading, in a large college statistics course, where the gain was negligible ([d = .115 on unit quizzes](../claims/peer-assessment-improves-unit-quiz-achievement.md) [+M], about the same on the final exam); expect a small effect from the review step on tested achievement, and look for its value in the revised draft and the reviewer's next one.
- **The reviewer may gain.** [The quality of feedback students give peers predicts gains in their own work, but no recorded study isolates a causal benefit of the assessor role](../claims/peer-assessment-benefits-assessor.md) [+M]: a meta-analysis of 58 studies found participation in either role associated with a 0.291 SD gain, larger with rater training and with computer-mediated delivery; in 43 undergraduate teacher-education students who each reviewed two peers' projects and revised their own, the quality of feedback a student gave predicted their own final project, and the feedback received did not. Carry it to "keep the reviewer role and train it", not to "reviewing causes better own work".
- **Rubric guidance changes what reviewers write.** [In two online comparisons, a rubric changed what peer reviewers commented on (one study) and helped peer raters place writing in quality tiers, most for complex criteria (the other); the first study says the rubric restrained reflective thinking](../claims/rubrics-improve-peer-feedback-quality.md) [+M]: two `q2` quasi-experiments with no effect sizes. In a MOOC writing course, guided-rubric training helped raters place samples in novice, intermediate and advanced tiers, mostly on subjective, complex criteria; in a graduate course of 47, a rubric changed comment content and restrained reflective and divergent commentary. It supports calibration on graded samples, and warns that a rubric can narrow comments.
- **Structure is not shown to raise learning (the limit).** [Structured criteria in peer assessment are not shown to improve its effect on academic performance over freeform review](../claims/peer-assessment-structured-criteria-improve-learning.md) [~M]: in the 54-study meta-analysis, studies giving reviewers a specific script or rubric (g = 0.29) did not differ significantly from freeform peer assessment (g = 0.42); the freeform subgroup was small (13 studies). This limits the "structured" in the pattern's name: criteria and prompts may make comments more specific and consistent without making the activity more effective for learning, and the evidence here does not separate the two. Keep the structure light where reviewers are already competent, and test whether it is earning its time.
- **Neighbouring arrangements in writing classes.** [Collaborative writing](../claims/collaborative-writing-improves-text-quality.md) [+S]: across seven comparisons in grades 4–12, students who planned, drafted or revised together wrote better texts than students writing alone (weighted mean ES = 0.75), and in one small randomised study of 28 children, writing in structured pairs raised the quality of their later individual writing. That is co-writing, not review of an individual's draft. [Process writing instruction](../claims/process-writing-improves-writing-quality.md) [~M], which includes peer and teacher feedback on drafts, gave general-education students in grades 1–12 a modest gain (ES = 0.34 across 29 studies) and struggling writers none. Both are the larger frame this cycle sits in; neither isolates peer comments.
- **Who comments well.** [In two secondary-school writing studies, peer reviewers' own performance predicted how helpful their feedback was but not how accurately it identified problems](../claims/peer-feedback-accuracy-depends-on-expertise.md) [~M]: in 234 high-school assessors, own writing performance was unrelated to whether they flagged real problems but predicted whether their comments were rated helpful; with a well-specified rubric, 1,215 AP English students' averaged ratings correlated with expert scores at roughly .4–.7, no differently in lower- and higher-performing schools. Both correlational. In MOOC peer grading, [higher-scoring students graded with less bias, except the very top scorers, and bias was least between peers of similar attainment](../claims/grader-grade-relates-to-grading-reliability.md) [~W] (observational, no effect size); that is about the scores reviewers give, not the problems they find, so it does not contradict the writing studies, and it points the same way: average several reviewers of mixed attainment rather than trusting any one. It is the reason for calibration on helpfulness (suggesting a change), for averaging several reviews, and for not reserving review for the strongest writers.
- **Several peers against one expert.** [In a SWoRD evaluation, drafts improved most between draft and final paper when the author received several peer reviews, and least with a single expert reviewer](../claims/multiple-peer-reviews-beat-single-expert-reviewer-sword.md) [+W]: one controlled experiment with 28 students in a research methods course, reported in a systematic review of peer-assessment tools with no effect size. It leans the other way from the writing meta-analysis's inconclusive peer-against-teacher comparison, on far less evidence; take it as a reason for two or three reviews per draft, not as evidence that peers beat teachers.
- **What reviewers do while reviewing.** In [Lu & Law's study of 181 secondary students](../claims/giving-suggestions-predicts-assessor-performance.md) [+W] assessing peers' projects online, giving suggestions and [identifying problems](../claims/giving-problem-identification-feedback-predicts-assessor-performance.md) [+W] predicted the giver's own project score, controlling for prior attainment; [receiving positive affective comments](../claims/receiving-positive-affective-feedback-predicts-assessee-performance.md) [+W] predicted the author's, while [receiving cognitive feedback did not](../claims/cognitive-feedback-benefits-assessors-not-assessees.md) [-W]. In a think-aloud study of twelve students giving and processing peer feedback on argumentative writing, [students attended more to errors than to what worked](../claims/students-focus-more-on-errors-than-pluses.md) [~W], and [most compared the draft with their own sense of the quality criteria](../claims/internal-quality-criteria-guide-error-identification.md) [+W] to find and judge errors. All correlational or qualitative. They support the comment format (problem plus suggestion), a required "works and should stay" opening, and calibration on criteria; they qualify the expectation that the author gains from receiving comments, which the model already treated as less certain than the reviewer's gain.
- **Reviewer accountability and peer marks.** From one systematic review of peer-assessment tools: [grading students on the quality of their reviews, not on the peer marks](../claims/grading-review-quality-reduces-tensions-improves-correlations.md) [+W], reduced tensions and raised student–instructor agreement; [letting students grade the reviews they received](../claims/peerscholar-accountability-improves-expert-peer-correlation.md) [+W] raised a low peer–expert correlation; [programming peers' ratings](../claims/sitthiworachart-peer-ratings-objective-judgements.md) [+W] tracked instructors', more accurately on objective judgements; and [76 high-school students' Web-SPA marks](../claims/web-spa-peer-review-consistency-and-improvement.md) [+W] were consistent with the instructor's, with work improving after review (no comparison group). An inventory of practice separates [proactive and reactive quality control](../claims/peer-assessment-quality-control-proactive-reactive.md) [+W]. Each is a single tool report read second-hand, with no effect size; they shape the high-stakes, large-class and online rows, not the cycle itself.
- **What a comment should say.** [Feedback Most Effective At Task And Process Levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]: two meta-analyses of feedback in general (not peer feedback) found high-information feedback far more effective than simple reinforcement or punishment, and effectiveness falling as feedback drew attention to the self. [Feedback improves learning by a medium amount on average, but its effect varies widely with the information it carries, and a substantial share of feedback effects are negative](../claims/feedback-improves-learning.md) [+M]: across 435 studies (d = 0.48), specific written comments outperformed generic ones. [Praise for intelligence, rather than for effort, after success undermines children's motivation and performance after a later failure](../claims/feedback-praise-reduces-learning.md) [~M]: children praised for intelligence, rather than for effort, persisted less after a later failure. Carried from teacher and system feedback to peers' comments: prompt for criterion, place and suggested change, and for praise of what in the draft works, not of the writer.
- **Comments may not be used.** [Critical constructive feedback is neglected at multiple, independently measurable processing stages](../claims/critical-constructive-feedback-is-neglected-at-multiple-stages.md) [~M]: of 424 feedback instances to Swedish fifth-graders in a digital game, 19 (4.5%) went from noticing to measurable progress, and the largest loss (77%) came between reading and acting. One `q3` study of an agent's feedback, not peers' comments; it is the reason for the author's revision plan, which is itself untested.

Do not rank structured peer review against teacher comments from these claims: the meta-analytic comparison with teacher feedback is inconclusive, the one small experiment favouring several peers over one expert is read second-hand, none tests the full cycle, and none measures the time it costs. Do not read a long list of revisions as learning, or a reviewer's fluent comments as their own improved writing. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (the full cycle against peer comments without calibration, or without a required revision plan, on the revised draft and on the reviewer's next draft, at the end of a term) and the local observation that would stand in for it.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested course.* A part-time master's module in public health (`adult`, `postsecondary`, most students working) runs `online-instructor-led` with one tutor for 60 students, some joining weekly seminars by phone; the assessed piece is a 2,500-word policy brief written for a named local decision-maker. Students have written essays before but not policy briefs (`novice` for the genre). The table changed the default in four ways: review runs asynchronously in a form, so phone users read a one-page section rather than the whole brief; the first cycle opens with collaborative evaluation of two anchor briefs the tutor has scored; one criterion is "what would the decision-maker do or misunderstand after reading this"; and peer ratings do not count towards the mark. In cycles 1–2 each student reviews two anonymous briefs with sentence frames; the tutor returns three comments per reviewer with a note; authors submit a revision plan within three days and revise over the following week. By cycle 4 authors name the two criteria they want feedback on, and the frames are optional. One student says she wants "something my director would actually read"; the designer's objective is a brief that meets the criteria. They agree to score the final brief on the rubric and to ask the student whether she would send it.

Record: **draft and the criteria as given → each reviewer's comments on the anchor, against the tutor's → reviewers assigned, named or anonymous → each comment: criterion named, place, suggested change, real problem or not → author's revision plan (acted on, rejected with reason, asked) → final version scored on the rubric against the draft → reviewer's next draft scored on the same criteria → candidate interpretations (criterion not understood, domain knowledge missing, comments ignored for lack of time, deference to any reader, status or friendship) and their basis → reobservation at the next cycle**. Preserve each author's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or treat the number of revisions as improvement.

## Description
Structured Peer Review is a pattern in which learners review one another's work using explicit criteria, provide feedback, and then use that feedback to revise. The pattern supports learning in two directions: reviewers deepen their understanding of quality by judging another's work, and authors improve by receiving feedback before final submission.

The pattern works best when the review process is designed carefully. Unstructured peer review often produces vague praise or unhelpful criticism. The "structured" part is what turns the activity into instruction rather than mere exchange.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A reviewable artifact**: Learners need drafts, prototypes, proposals, or performances that can be evaluated meaningfully.
- **Explicit criteria**: Rubrics, checklists, or prompting questions should guide what reviewers attend to.
- **Training or modeling**: Learners often need examples of useful feedback before reviewing well.
- **Revision after review**: The feedback should feed a later improvement cycle.
#### Constraints
- **Feedback quality varies**: Without calibration, some reviews will be inaccurate or superficial.
- **Social risk**: Learners may hesitate to critique peers honestly without norms and support.
- **Rubric overdependence**: Overly narrow criteria can reduce feedback to box-checking.
- **Weak revision follow-through**: If authors do not use the feedback, much of the learning value is lost.
#### Grain Size
- Lesson
- Unit

- **Revision and improvement**: Strengthening work before final evaluation.
- **Evaluative judgment**: Helping learners internalize standards of quality.
- **Metacognitive reflection**: Noticing patterns in both one's own work and others' work.

- **Learners in writing, design, and project-based settings**: Strong fit where drafts and revision cycles are normal.
- **Learners developing quality standards**: Reviewing others helps clarify what good work looks like.
- **Adult and professional learners**: Useful where review and iteration are part of real practice.
