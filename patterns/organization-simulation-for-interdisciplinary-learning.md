# Organization Simulation for Interdisciplinary Learning

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)

## Description
Students form simulated consulting organizations (e.g., HR-service companies) that compete to win a real external client's request for tenders. Teams of 11-15 students, deliberately mixed across degree programs, take on internal roles (CEO, HR head, finance head, communications head) and spend a multi-week course iteratively producing a shared, evolving offer — the course's central [knowledge object](../theories/knowledge-objects-and-knowledge-practices.md). An authentic external evaluator (a real HR professional) reviews and selects the winning offer, creating real stakes and a genuine success criterion beyond an instructor's grade.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A real external client/evaluator who issues an authentic request for tenders and gives feedback on both draft and final offers
- Deliberately interdisciplinary team composition (the source study mixed educational psychology, engineering, economics, humanities, and education students in each team)
- Weekly session structure combining brief disciplinary input (a lecture), extended self-organized team work time, and reflective closure
- Material tools for externalizing and negotiating the shared object (whiteboards, shared documents) — teams that used these to shift from unfocused talk to structured problem-framing showed better integration
#### Constraints
- Time pressure and unresolved role ambiguity are the most commonly reported barriers: teams without a clear, accepted leader reported disorganization, while teams where one member's leadership was contested reported conflict
- Disciplinary differences in vocabulary and underlying assumptions (e.g., "leadership" meaning task-management to engineering students vs. people-management to education students) can cause friction and lost time before being explicitly surfaced
- Additional mid-course disruptions (e.g., an unplanned "cooperation negotiations" task) are a deliberate design feature that forces re-organization — useful for building adaptive capacity, but adds real cognitive and coordination load
#### Grain Size
- Course (the source study used a 6-week, 3.5-hour/week Master's course; 5 ECTS)

### Target Goals
- Producing an authentic collective advancement (a competitive offer) that would be unlikely through single-disciplinary work alone
- [Organization simulation knowledge practices support interdisciplinary learning](../claims/organization-simulation-knowledge-practices-support-interdisciplinary-learning.md)

### Target Learners
- Higher-education students from mixed academic backgrounds working in unfamiliar teams; studied at the Master's level but not level-specific in principle

### Theory
#### Supporting
- [Knowledge Objects and Knowledge Practices](../theories/knowledge-objects-and-knowledge-practices.md) — this pattern is the source study's central example of how a knowledge object (the offer) evolves through six collective knowledge practices
- [Ecological Paradigm of Interdisciplinary Learning](../theories/ecological-paradigm-of-interdisciplinary-learning.md) — a pedagogy-dilemma response built around simulation, role-play, and a negotiable shared artifact
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- The source study could not independently verify that all team activity met a strict definition of interdisciplinary integration (as opposed to parallel, divided-labor contribution), since the final offers themselves were not systematically analyzed for integration quality

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Week 1**: Teams form; map individual expertise; establish an organization identity (name, logo, values) — an early knowledge object that builds group cohesion before the harder integrative work begins.
2. **Weeks 1-4**: Teams iteratively draft, negotiate, and restructure a competing offer for the external client, informed by weekly disciplinary-content lectures (e.g., organization dynamics, mobile work, trust).
3. **Weeks 3-4**: A game-based HR-competency activity and an annual HR resources plan introduce domain content in a low-stakes way before it is applied to the team's own offer.
4. **Week 5**: An unplanned "cooperation negotiations" disruption forces teams to reorganize roles and priorities mid-stream, simulating real organizational complexity.
5. **Weeks 5-6**: Teams finalize pricing, budget, and presentation; present the completed offer to the external client, who selects a winner.
6. **Throughout**: Individual reflective learning diaries (graded 1-5) capture each student's personal sensemaking and application of concepts — the only individually assessed component; team artifacts themselves are not graded.

### Personalization
- The specific simulated industry/client can vary; what matters structurally is a real external evaluator, a competitive framing, and a single evolving shared artifact
- Diary-based individual reflection can be swapped for other individual-accountability mechanisms if diaries are not culturally or logistically suitable

## Examples
- Four course iterations at a Finnish university, 175 students total (84 in the analyzed sample), 12 teams; diary analysis (1,083 coded units) identified six recurring collective knowledge practices, and video analysis of two teams showed material tools (flip charts) mediating shifts from unfocused talk to structured, client-centered problem framing.
