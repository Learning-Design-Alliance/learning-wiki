# Body-Scale Collaborative Geometric Construction

## Description
Groups are given materials (e.g., rods and connectors) and a goal structure — an image of a complex geometric solid plus a short list of its defining properties (e.g., "triangular faces," "five edges meet at each vertex") — but no step-by-step assembly instructions. Working at body scale, the group must physically discover the structural constraints through trial and error, and develop shared perceptual vocabulary (e.g., naming a repeating sub-shape as a "star" or "base") to coordinate their construction. The named structures function as reusable coordination resources: once one member articulates a perceptual insight through speech, gesture, and material action together, peers take it up, refine it, and redeploy it to guide the next step of construction.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A target structure complex enough that its construction path is not obvious from the properties list alone
- Materials that allow physical, body-scale (not tabletop-miniature) construction
- No assembly instructions — the constraint-discovery process is the point
#### Constraints
- Evidence for this pattern comes from a single 4-person case study of graduate students with embodied-cognition training, not typical classroom learners — [Embodied collaborative construction can build shared geometric reasoning](../claims/embodied-collaborative-construction-can-build-shared-geometric-reasoning.md) [~W]

### Target Goals
- Building shared, embodied perceptual structures that can later support formal geometric reasoning (e.g., using an embodied "base" concept to calculate edge count)

### Target Learners
- Studied with graduate students; the underlying mechanism (multimodal coordination around a shared physical construction) is not intrinsically limited to that population, but has not been tested with younger or more typical learners

## Design

### Sequence
1. Present the target structure as an image plus a short list of its defining properties, with construction materials but no instructions.
2. Groups begin exploratory construction, negotiating initial subtasks.
3. Groups detect structural problems from material affordances and goal-checking (e.g., counting edges at a vertex that doesn't look right).
4. A group member names an emergent perceptual sub-structure through combined speech, gesture, and material positioning (e.g., "this is a star — five triangles around a point").
5. The named structure is taken up by peers, stabilizes as a shared resource in group talk, and is reused to guide and monitor further construction.
6. After completion, learners use the embodied structures they named (e.g., "base," "star") to answer formal questions about the solid (e.g., total edge count).

## Examples
- Four graduate students collaboratively built a body-scale icosahedron from dowels and connectors with only a diagram and properties list, then used their own coined term "base" (a pentagonal-pyramid sub-structure) to calculate the solid's 30 edges.
