# Traditional Lecture + Reading + Midterm + Final Assessment

## Description
A common higher education course structure where learning is primarily lecture-based, supplemented by assigned readings, and assessed through high-stakes exams (midterm and final assessments). The model emphasizes passive content absorption, requiring students to take notes, engage in self-study, and prepare for exams through independent review. Grading is typically weighted heavily toward the exams, with limited formative assessment or active learning. Variations may include occasional discussions, quizzes, or writing assignments, but the core structure remains lecture-driven with summative assessments.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- - Requires large lecture halls or digital lecture delivery platforms.
- Requires graded assessments (midterms/finals) that align with the lecture and reading material.
- Typically lacks structured opportunities for feedback before high-stakes assessments.
#### Constraints
- - Low interactivity: Minimal active learning, discussion, or collaborative engagement.
- Heavy reliance on memorization rather than deeper learning strategies.
- One-size-fits-all approach: Can disadvantage learners with different learning preferences or needs.
- High-stakes assessments can increase anxiety and may not reflect deeper conceptual understanding.

## Design

### Sequence
<!-- Steps with links to elements -->
- Linear sequence: Content is presented in lectures, supplemented by reading assignments, followed by major exams.
- Minimal formative assessment between the midterm and final.
- Success is dependent on student self-regulation and exam preparation.

## Examples
- - Introductory university courses: Many large lecture-based general education courses.
- Professional certification programs: Lecture-heavy formats with a final qualifying exam.
- Online courses using recorded lectures with exams as primary assessment methods.

## Impact
- - Works well for content-heavy disciplines where direct instruction is effective.
- Often criticized for limited student engagement and lack of feedback mechanisms.
- Research suggests active learning approaches may lead to better retention than passive lecture-based instruction.
