    Debate¶

    Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 11 claims (1 for, 9 mixed, 1 against) · 20 studies (6 causal, 5 quant-synthesis, 4 review, 3 theoretical, 1 associational, 1 qualitative), q1–q4 · 3 of 20 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

    Description and scope¶

    A reusable policy for structured argument on a contested question: a debatable resolution → assigned or chosen positions → preparation with evidence → timed turns with opening, rebuttal and closing → judging against stated criteria → debrief in which learners say what they now think and why. It instantiates the Debate principle, and it is one configuration of the broader activity of argumentation. Structured Academic Controversy sits beside it as a cooperative variant: positions are argued, then reversed, and the group ends by building a joint conclusion rather than a winner. Choose between them by the target (an adversarial case defended under challenge, or an integrated position), not by habit.

    No claim in this wiki tests classroom debate as such, against another format or against no debate. The claims that bear on it are of three kinds: syntheses and comparison-group studies of argumentation and discussion in general, which the page carries over to debate by extrapolation; one review naming "consider the opposite" as a debiasing strategy; and one 1996 author's critique and informal tournament survey of competitive parliamentary debate, which are argument and perception, not tests of learning. The response-dependent policy below is an untested design proposal. Use it to gather better observations of how learners reason about a contested question, not to certify reasoning because a round was won.

    Inputs: establish the design brief¶

    Field Record or elicit
    Learner and starting observation A short written position on the resolution, with the best reason for it and the best reason against it, collected before preparation. Prior knowledge of the topic, language of debate, experience of public speaking and anyone who rarely speaks. Preserve unknowns rather than labelling learners novice or "confident".
    The question Whether the resolution supports competing claims that can be reasoned about with evidence, or would force a false binary; whether it is personally charged for anyone in the room.
    Context and activities Setting (classroom, online-instructor-led), single-session or days-weeks, team size, how positions are assigned, preparation time and sources, whether published evidence may be used in the round, whether cross-examination or questions are allowed, turn lengths, who judges and how.
    Learner-valued goal Ask what learners want from it (to win, to be able to defend a view at work, to understand the other side, to pass the assessment). Record disagreement with the designer objective.
    Designer objective Which capability is the target: building and defending claims with evidence, responding to the opposing case, perspective-taking, oral communication under structure, or knowledge of the topic (principle, concept, complex-skill). Name the one the assessment will weigh.
    Outcome Instrument and horizon: a rubric for the round, an individual written argument on the same or a new question afterwards (near-transfer, far-transfer), a position-revision note, conceptual-understanding of the topic. Say which is immediate and which delayed.

    A request for "a debate to get them thinking" does not specify these. Ask for the target and the instrument before choosing a format, team size or judging scheme.

    Sequence and conditional policy¶

    The default sequence is the earlier page's, kept because no claim here tests a debate sequence; each step says what bears on it.

    1. Choose and check the resolution, and state the criteria. The question must support competing, evidence-bearing claims. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page (merged Debate Format). The critique of unconstrained topic writing [~W] is one author's argument from tournament examples that frivolous resolutions sustain non-argumentative practice; it is not a test.
    2. Collect each learner's starting position in writing, with one reason for and one against. Evidence status: untested; proposed here so a debrief has something to compare with.
    3. Assign positions or let teams prepare opposing interpretations, and give preparation time with sources. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. Whether to allow evidence in the round is a design choice: the parliamentary-rules critique [-W] argues that banning published evidence and omitting cross-examination removes research, evidence use and questioning from the training. It is argument, not a measured comparison.
    4. Run the debate with openings, rebuttals and closings, under moderation of turn-taking. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. Discussion quality [~M] suggests that the amount of talk is not what matters, so the moderator's job is to keep turns responsive to the other case rather than to keep them full.
    5. Debrief the reasoning, evidence use and what learners now think, and collect a revised individual position. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. Considering the opposite [~W] gives a reason to ask each learner, specifically, what makes the opposing case strongest.
    6. Reobserve alone: an individual written argument on a new question at the stated horizon. Evidence status: the closest test is dialogic peer argumentation transferring to individual essays on new topics [~M], against a comparison group with essay practice and whole-class discussion; not a debate.
    Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
    A team repeats its own case and never answers the other side Ask one speaker, out of the round, to state the opposing team's strongest point; compare the answer with what they said in rebuttal. If they can state it but did not answer it, add a required response-to-rebuttal turn or cross-examination. If they cannot state it, give a structured listening task (note and rank the other side's claims) before rebuttal. Neither branch is tested.
    Fluent, confident speech with little evidence Ask for the source of one claim; score the round with the evidence criterion separated from delivery. If evidence is missing, lengthen preparation or require cited evidence; if the rubric rewards delivery, reweight it. A won round is not evidence of reasoning.
    A few learners dominate; others say nothing Compare each learner's written pre- and post-position with their speaking time. If silent learners' written reasoning is sound, change the response mode (written rebuttal, roles rotated, smaller teams) rather than read silence as weak reasoning. If it is weak, add preparation support.
    Learners leave holding the side they were assigned, or their first view, unchanged Ask for the strongest point against their final position and whether anything in the round moved them. If they can name strong counter-points, unchanged views may be reasoned; record it. If they cannot, run a consider-the-opposite prompt or a role reversal, as in Structured Academic Controversy.
    Distress, personal attack, or a resolution that one group experiences as about them Ask privately; check whether the question is a genuine academic controversy or a false equivalence. Reframe the question, change to a synthesis format, or drop debate for this topic. The earlier page's constraints name this risk; no claim here measures it.

    Choosing configurations from evidence¶

    • Argumentation in general. Argumentation improves reasoning [~M]: a meta-analysis of 46 online science argumentation studies (5,415 students) reports a medium effect on argumentation skill (g = 0.603) and on content learning through argument (g = 0.596), larger in K-12 than in higher education, read from its abstract only, so the comparison conditions are not established; and a multiyear dialogic-argumentation intervention with young adolescents, not described as randomised, transferred to better individual essays on new topics than a comparison group given essay practice and whole-class discussion, with no effect size in the abstract. Neither is a debate, and neither tests adversarial turns, judging or a public audience. Carry it as far as "structured argument with peers can improve individual argument", no further.
    • Talk is not the active ingredient. Discussion quality drives comprehension [~M]: a meta-analysis of classroom discussion approaches found that only a minority of approaches improved comprehension or critical thinking, with effects moderated by design, outcome measure and student ability (abstract only, no pooled effect read), and a year-long single-group study of 35 fourth-graders had no control group. It qualifies any debate design that counts participation or speaking time as the outcome; it does not test debate.
    • Consider the opposite. Considering the opposite reduces bias [~W]: a systematic review names the strategy as previously validated in its background, but its own data do not isolate it, and no effect size is reported. It motivates the debrief prompt, not the format.
    • Format rules in competitive debate. The parliamentary-rules critique [-W] and unconstrained topic writing [~W] are one author's analysis of competitive parliamentary debate (1996), coded q1, theoretical. They warn that rules can remove evidence use and questioning and that topics can stop being arguable; they measure nothing, and they concern tournaments, not classes.

    Do not rank debate against Socratic seminar, Structured Academic Controversy or discussion from these claims: none compares them. Do not read a round's result, a judge's ballot or a learner's enthusiasm as a learning gain, and do not predict delayed or far-transfer outcomes from immediate round scores. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (debate against another argument format, on an individual outcome) and says what local observation would stand in for it.

    Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

    Claims this page cited before it was rewritten as a conditional model. They are evidence about the relationship, but none has yet been re-read against the model above; each says how far its own sources have been checked.

    • In one informal tournament survey, coaches and students listed partly different strengths and weaknesses of parliamentary debate [~W] — not yet checked against its sources
    • Prompting learners to self-explain improves understanding and problem solving on immediate tests by a moderate average amount, with little evidence yet on delayed or classroom gains. [~S] — not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract)
    • Contingent scaffolding improves learning more than fixed or absent support. [~M] — not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract)
    • Self-monitoring improves self-regulation and supports better learning decisions. [~M] — not settled: the text available could not confirm the entries (abstract)
    • Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague "do your best" goals. [~M] — not yet checked against its sources
    • Process goals lead to better skill acquisition for novices than outcome goals. [+M] — novice debaters benefit from explicit routines for claim-evidence-reasoning rather than only being told to "argue well"

    Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

    Invented illustration, not a tested lesson. In a first-year public-policy seminar, the resolution is whether a city should replace a bus route with on-demand shuttles. Each learner writes a position with one reason for and one against. Teams of three are assigned sides and given a week and a source pack; published evidence may be cited in the round, and each side gets one two-minute question period. Rounds are scored on a rubric that separates evidence, response to the opposing case and delivery. One learner says she wants to be able to argue a budget case to a council; the instructor's objective is responding to counter-evidence. After the round every learner names the opposing side's strongest point and writes a revised position; a week later each writes an individual argument on a different transport question. These are local design choices; the evidence above does not establish their optimality.

    Record: resolution and intended target → starting written position → assignment of sides and preparation given → rules in force (evidence, questions, turn lengths) → who spoke and for how long → rubric scores by criterion → debrief prompt and revised position → candidate interpretations (reasoned change, adopted team line, unchanged and reasoned, unchanged and unexamined) and their basis → individual follow-up argument at the stated horizon. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical probabilities of reasoning skill without a calibrated model.

    Elements and limits¶

    Debate, structured debate, argumentation, assigned positions, justification, role reversal, peer discussion, class discussion, rubric, feedback, assessment and debrief. Principles the pattern draws on: Debate, Perspective-Seeking/Multiple Perspectives and Peer Discussion.

    This pattern is scoped to structured adversarial argument on a question that admits more than one defensible answer. Questions with one correct answer, topics where a pro/con frame would create a false equivalence, and goals that call for collaborative synthesis need other configurations (Structured Academic Controversy, seminar or discussion formats). The policy supports observation and design reasoning; whether debate improves individual reasoning, topic understanding or speaking more than another argument format, for which learners and at what horizon, remains to be tested.
