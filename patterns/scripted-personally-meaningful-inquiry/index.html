    Scripted Personally Meaningful Inquiry¶

    Pattern · All patterns

    Description¶

    This pattern operationalizes Personal Inquiry's scripted-orchestration approach as a six-step activity sequence, implemented in the source study using the nQuire software toolkit. A persistent octagon diagram of the theory's 8-phase inquiry cycle is shown on every screen, functioning as both orientation ("where am I in this investigation?") and a navigable to-do list, and the software allows non-linear movement — students can return to revise a question or plan after collecting evidence. Teachers configure which phases and activities happen individually, in groups, at home, or in the field, and whether students see each other's data in real time or after the fact.

    Implications¶

    Context¶

    Requirements¶

    • Mobile or take-home devices capable of collecting and later uploading data (numeric, pictorial, or textual measures)
    • A topic with a plausible individual-difference dimension, so that pooling different students' individually-collected data produces a genuinely analyzable shared dataset (e.g., different locations' noise levels, different households' food-storage conditions)

    Constraints¶

    • Minor technical friction (slow device response, temporary connectivity loss) is common but did not substantially block use in the source study's classroom deployments
    • Evidence on inquiry-knowledge gains is confounded by pre-existing group differences in the one controlled comparison available (see linked claim); evidence on attitude/behavior change is anecdotal (parent/student interview reports), not measured

    Grain Size¶

    Unit (a multi-lesson sequence spanning classroom time, field trips, and/or take-home data collection, e.g., a 10-lesson ecology unit)

    Target Goals¶

    • Scripted personally meaningful inquiry is associated with gains in inquiry-decision accuracy, with mixed evidence on attitude and behavior change

    Target Learners¶

    • Secondary students (ages 11-16 in the source study) in science classes with access to portable data-collection devices

    Theory¶

    Supporting¶

    • Personal Inquiry

    Claims¶

    Supporting¶

    • Scripted personally meaningful inquiry is associated with gains in inquiry-decision accuracy, with mixed evidence on attitude and behavior change [~M]

    Design¶

    Sequence¶

    1. Find My Topic / Form My Question: Whole class agrees on an inquiry topic and wording of the guiding question in the classroom, with the phase-cycle diagram displayed throughout.
    2. Plan My Investigation: Student groups propose methods and select the measures (variables) they will track, with teacher guidance on what's feasible to measure.
    3. Collect My Evidence: Students individually collect data outside the classroom (at home, in the playground, at a field site), viewing peers' data in real time if connectivity allows, or upon return to class otherwise.
    4. Analyze My Evidence: Back in the classroom, groups pool and jointly analyze the shared dataset, explicitly relating findings back to the original question — including cases where the result is unexpected or inconclusive, which the teacher frames as an occasion for explanation rather than a failed experiment.
    5. Respond to Evidence / Share My Results: Students prepare and present findings for whole-class discussion.
    6. Reflect on My Experience: Students and teacher reflect on the inquiry process itself, closing the loop back to the visual inquiry-cycle diagram.

    Affordances¶

    • Personal Inquiry

    Personalization¶

    • Where take-home or field data collection isn't feasible, the sequence can be compressed into classroom-only data collection (e.g., varying conditions across groups within one room), though this forfeits the theory's out-of-classroom personal-relevance mechanism.

    Examples¶

    • A Year 8 (ages 12-13) noise-pollution inquiry in which students measured bird-feeder visits in noisy versus quiet schoolyard locations, and — when results contradicted their hypothesis — were prompted to re-observe and explain the discrepancy (e.g., noting that pigeons, which eat more per visit, dominated the noisy-yard feeder).
    • An after-school "Sustainability Squad" (ages 12-14) food-decay and packaging investigation in which some students' data collection prompted them to revise their hypotheses multiple times over several weeks before settling on a testable question.
