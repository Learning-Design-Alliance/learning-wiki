# Professional Development

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 51 claims (33 for, 14 mixed, 4 against) · 45 studies (11 review, 10 qualitative, 8 causal, 7 quant-synthesis, 4 design, 3 theoretical, 2 associational), `q1`–`q4` · 7 of 45 report an effect size · 45 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable policy for changing what practising educators do in their own classrooms: **choose one observable classroom practice → find out how each teacher currently enacts it and what they know of its content → show the practice and the reasoning behind it → rehearse it with colleagues → teachers enact it with their own students while a coach or peer observes (live or on video) → feedback on that enactment, tied to the practice and to what students did → repeat the enact-and-feedback cycle with support withdrawn as the practice holds → check later that it is still in use**. The learners are teachers (`adult`, `adult-workplace`): the target is a `complex-skill` enacted in a `classroom`, with the knowledge and beliefs that go with it. The intended change is in observed teaching practice (`skill-performance`), and through it in students' behavior or achievement, at a horizon of a term or more (`term-plus`). Teacher knowledge, satisfaction ratings and stated intentions are not the target, though they are worth recording.

It applies the [cognitive apprenticeship pattern](cognitive-apprenticeship.md) to adult learners whose task is teaching, and it sits inside the [scaffolding and fading principle](../principles/scaffolding-and-fading.md), which owns the general relationship between contingent support, its withdrawal and independent performance; this page does not restate either model. Where the PD is carried mainly by a standing group of colleagues (a professional learning community, lesson study), the [Communities of Practice principle](../principles/communities-of-practice.md) holds the model for sustained participation in a shared practice, and the [Fostering Communities of Learning pattern](fostering-communities-of-learning-fcl.md) is the nearest community pattern. This page owns the narrower configuration: a cycle of modeling, enactment with one's own students, and feedback on that enactment, run for teachers over a term or more.

**No claim in this wiki tests this pattern's distinguishing relationship**: that sustained, practice-embedded PD with coached enactment changes practice or student outcomes more than a one-off workshop. The two syntheses the earlier page leaned on are now recorded as claims, and they bear on the coached-enactment step rather than on the whole pattern: Kraft, Blazar & Hogan (2018) pool causal studies of teacher coaching ([Teacher coaching programs improve teachers' instructional practice by a pooled 0.49 SD across 43 causal studies](../claims/coaching-pooled-instruction-effect-0-49-sd.md) [+M]; [Teacher coaching raises student achievement on standardized tests by a pooled 0.18 SD across 31 causal studies](../claims/coaching-pooled-achievement-effect-0-18-sd.md) [+M]), and coaching paired with group training and materials went with larger effects ([Pairing coaching with group trainings is associated with larger effects on instruction and achievement; instructional materials with larger, and video libraries with smaller, effects on instruction](../claims/coaching-complementary-elements-moderators.md) [~M], an association across studies, not a test); Darling-Hammond, Hyler & Gardner (2017) report coaching or expert support in 30 of 35 effective programmes ([Coaching and expert support appeared in 30 of 35 studies, with coached classrooms showing larger gains in a randomized trial](../claims/pd-coaching-expert-support-gains.md) [+M]). Neither compares sustained, practice-embedded PD with the same content as a one-off workshop, which is this pattern's own relationship. Beyond them the wiki holds: two reviews reported second-hand in one 1994 monograph (staff-development experiments mostly changed instruction and raised achievement against control teachers; the techniques that trained instructional skills); one review of 16 supervisory-coaching studies; one small quasi-experiment in which coaching changed four mathematics teachers' small-group moves; case studies of a three-year science program and of two teachers adapting a practice; programme reports, implementation studies and surveys from coaching and PD initiatives, which report sustained, followed-up and job-embedded PD, coaches' own support and district backing as conditions of success, and one difference-in-differences comparison of schools by how much standards-related professional learning they took part in, none comparing formats with the same content; and learner-level claims on feedback, contingent support and fading, carried to teachers by extrapolation. The sequence below is the earlier page's, kept as the default, with each step's evidence status stated; the doses are **labelled proposals**, and the response-dependent policy is an **untested design proposal**.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | Before any session, observe or video one lesson per teacher (or collect a lesson plan and a sample of student work if observation is impossible) and record how the target practice is currently enacted, using the same look-for list that will be used later. Record content familiarity in the subject the practice serves, role (new teacher, experienced teacher, faculty member, teaching assistant, volunteer), and whether participation is voluntary. Do not infer readiness from years of experience; keep unknowns as unknowns. |
| The practice | Name one practice specific enough to observe in a lesson (for example "pose a question, give think time, take three responses before evaluating", or "run the curriculum's launch–explore–discuss lesson structure"), not a broad aim ("student-centered teaching"). Whether it is a teacher behavior (praise, questioning) or the faithful running of a specific academic task or program. Whether it needs content knowledge teachers may lack. |
| Context and activities | Setting (`classroom`, `online-instructor-led`, mixed in-room and remote), span (`single-session`, `days-weeks`, `term-plus`), number of teachers, coach or facilitator hours available per teacher per month, whether release time or cover exists, whether lessons can be filmed, whether teams from one school or department can take part together, and whether the PD is linked to performance evaluation. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask each teacher what they want from it (a calmer class, a curriculum that works, students who talk more, getting through an inspection, career progression). Record where it differs from the designer's objective. |
| Designer objective | Which change is the target: enactment of the named practice, faithful running of a specific program, teachers' own design of lessons using a framework, a team's shared routine, or a student outcome. Name the one the evaluation will weigh. |
| Outcome | An observation instrument for the practice (a look-for checklist or coded video), used at baseline, during, at the end, and again at least six weeks after support ends; a student measure aligned with the practice, if student change is claimed; and teacher reports kept apart from observed practice. |

A request to "design PD on formative assessment" or "run a coaching program" does not specify these. Ask what practice, observed how, over what span and with how much coach time, before choosing a format.

## Sequence and conditional policy

The default sequence is the earlier page's seven steps, with doses added as labelled proposals. Steps 1, 3 and 7 are untested here; steps 2, 4, 5 and 6 have neighbouring claims, each carried by extrapolation.

1. **Diagnose starting points** (weeks 0–2). Observe or film one lesson per teacher with the look-for list; look at a sample of student work; ask each teacher what they want from the PD. Use the result to set each teacher's first target and the coaching intensity. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. [Content familiarity, not teaching experience, predicted reform-based practice](../claims/content-familiarity-predicts-reform-practice-experience-does-not.md) [~M] (334 science teachers, one district partnership, an association) is a reason to diagnose content familiarity rather than sort teachers by years of service.
2. **Model the practice and its reasoning** (launch: proposed one or two days, about 6–12 hours, before or at the start of the term). Show the practice live, on video or by think-aloud, with the reasoning for each decision, and present the rationale for it. Evidence status: [the techniques that trained teachers' instructional skills](../claims/skill-training-effective-techniques.md) [+M] (a review by Showers, Joyce and Bennett as a 1994 monograph reports it, no effect sizes): presentation of the rationale, demonstration and modeling, practice with feedback, and coaching for transfer to the classroom.
3. **Analyze together** (within the launch). Teachers pick apart the modeled practice against student work, agreeing on what the look-for list will count. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page.
4. **Rehearse with colleagues** (within the launch, and 15–20 minutes at the start of each later team meeting). Teachers try the practice with peers acting as students and get feedback before using it with their classes. Evidence status: part of the same reported technique list [+M]; no claim tests rehearsal separately.
5. **Enact with support** (proposed coaching cycles of two to three weeks each, four to six cycles across a term). Each cycle: a short planning conversation (20–30 minutes); the teacher teaches with the practice while the coach observes live or the teacher films the lesson; a debrief within two school days (30–45 minutes) that starts from the teacher's own viewing of the video, names one thing to keep and one change, and states what the students did. Feedback is about the enacted practice and students' responses, not about the teacher as a person. Evidence status: [coaching aimed at the fidelity of a specific academic task](../claims/coaching-academic-task-fidelity-higher-quality.md) [+M] and [coaching to increase teacher praise](../claims/coaching-increases-teacher-praise.md) [+M] (one review of 16 K–12 studies): coached behaviors rose, and studies of task fidelity met more quality indicators and showed more sustained outcomes. [Group-level scaffolding training with on-the-job coaching](../claims/group-level-scaffolding-training-increases-teacher-process-support-and-student-participation.md) [+M] changed two mathematics teachers' small-group moves against two matched teachers (five lessons each; more students took part in each interaction) but did not improve how well their content support was calibrated. [Feedback at the task and process levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S] is from two meta-analyzes of learners and of performance generally, not of teachers. [Video self-observation](../claims/video-self-observation-powerful-coaching-component.md) [+W] is teachers' report in one focus-group study. In one survey, [coached teachers more often reported PD with enough time to try and evaluate new ideas, and most ranked one-to-one meetings and classroom visits as the most valuable contact](../claims/job-embedded-coaching-active-learning.md) [+W] (self-report, coached against non-coached teachers in one technology programme, no effect size).
6. **Reflect, iterate and fade** (later cycles). Early cycles: the coach models in the teacher's class or co-teaches, and chooses the focus. Middle cycles: the teacher teaches, the coach observes and gives one focus. Late cycles: the teacher chooses the video segment and the focus, a colleague observes instead of the coach, and the coach meets monthly. Move a teacher on to the next practice, or to peer-only support, when the practice is seen at the agreed level in two consecutive observed lessons and the teacher can say why they made the choices they did (a proposal). Evidence status: [contingent support](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] and [fading support](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M], both from studies of students in tutoring and example-based learning, carried to coaching by analogy; [guidance that helps novices can become redundant as expertise grows](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M], from studies of trainees and students, is the reason to fade rather than hold support constant. Across four sites of one teacher-led PD network, [lasting change was observed to be more likely where teachers tried strategies, got feedback and improved them over several workshops with coaches at hand](../claims/iterative-follow-up-supports-lasting-change.md) [+W] (a cross-case observation, follow-up "variable yet indispensable", no comparison group).
7. **Sustain and check maintenance** (after support ends). Fold the practice into a standing team routine: lesson study, peer observation or a professional learning community meeting every two to four weeks for about an hour. Observe again at least six weeks after coaching stops, with the same look-for list. Evidence status: untested; from the earlier page. [Most coaching studies did not measure maintenance, and where it was measured it was not always kept](../claims/coaching-maintenance-rarely-measured.md) [~M], which is why the later observation is in the design.

Total contact under this default is about 20–30 hours over a term, close to the earlier page's "20+ hours spread across a semester or year"; neither figure rests on a test here. Three reports bracket it. One randomised trial described in the Darling-Hammond review used 16 hours of workshops and seven biweekly coaching sessions over a semester, and coached classrooms gained more on a classroom-quality measure ([coaching and expert support](../claims/pd-coaching-expert-support-gains.md) [+M], reported second-hand). In one technology programme, [coached teachers averaged more than 16 hours of coaching over a school year, most at least 30 minutes a week](../claims/sustained-coaching-contact-hours.md) [+W] (survey; dose described, not tested). And [roughly 50 hours in one specific area](../claims/fifty-hours-content-specific-pd.md) [+W] is a recommendation reported second-hand from Darling-Hammond et al. (2009), whose printed evidence is a survey of how useful teachers found content-related PD, not a tested threshold. So the default keeps one term at about 20–30 hours for one narrow practice, and **plans a second term (towards 40–50 hours) when the practice is broad or teachers also need content study** (a proposal). The one program with a recorded dose, [three years of PD for Leader Teachers](../claims/leader-teacher-diffusion-uneven.md) [~M], gave nearly 500 hours and reports dramatic change by the third year, in one case study.

## Fitting the design to a situation

**The three facts that most change the decision:**

- **How specific and observable the target practice is.** Coaching studies that targeted fidelity of a specific academic task met more quality indicators and showed more sustained outcomes than those targeting general teacher behaviors, and knowing a framework did not make teachers able to design lessons with it. If unstated, ask: what will an observer see in a lesson when the practice is in place?
- **How much coach or facilitator time each teacher gets, and over what span.** Short, fragmented training is reported as hindering change, and the one recorded long program gave hundreds of hours; most real PD is a few hours. If unstated, ask: how many hours of a coach's or facilitator's time per teacher per month, for how many months, and is there release time?
- **Each teacher's content familiarity and current enactment of the practice, not years of experience.** If unstated, ask: what does one of their lessons look like now, and how confident are they in the subject the practice serves?

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **PD against no PD.** [Experimental staff-development programs mostly raised student achievement against control teachers](../claims/experimental-staff-development-raises-student-achievement.md) [+M]: a review attributed to Gage and Needels, as a 1994 monograph reports it, found that in all but one of nine experiments with reading and mathematics achievement outcomes, staff development changed teachers' instruction and their students achieved more than control teachers' students. No effect sizes are printed, and the programs are not described on the claim page, so it says that PD aimed at student achievement can work, not which configuration did. It is the nearest this wiki has to a test of the pattern's outcome.
- **What the training contains.** [Effective skill training combines theory presentation, demonstration, practice with feedback and coaching for transfer](../claims/skill-training-effective-techniques.md) [+M]: second-hand again, from a review attributed to Showers, Joyce and Bennett (1987). This is the source of steps 2, 4 and 5; it is a list of effective techniques, not a comparison of them.
- **Coaching.** One review of 16 supervisory-coaching studies in K–12 classrooms, coded by quality indicators, is the base for three claims: [coaching that targeted praise raised it in every such study](../claims/coaching-increases-teacher-praise.md) [+M]; [studies coaching the fidelity of a specific academic task met more quality indicators and showed more immediate and sustained outcomes than those coaching teacher behaviors](../claims/coaching-academic-task-fidelity-higher-quality.md) [+M]; and the limiting claim, [effects were mixed, with 6 of 16 reporting improved teacher results and 4 improved student behaviors, and only 6 meeting all quality indicators](../claims/supervisory-coaching-mixed-teacher-student-outcomes.md) [~M]. In a quasi-experiment with four seventh-grade mathematics teachers, [training and on-the-job coaching on a small-group scaffolding tool](../claims/group-level-scaffolding-training-increases-teacher-process-support-and-student-participation.md) [+M] changed which scaffolding moves teachers used and roughly doubled the students taking part in each interaction (3.48 against 1.96 on average), but did not change how well-calibrated their content support was (61% against 49%, not significant); no student learning was measured. Read together: coaching reliably moves the specific behavior it targets; whether that reaches student outcomes is mixed and seldom measured.
- **Sustained, followed-up, job-embedded PD: programme reports and surveys.** A later Learning Policy Institute synthesis restates the feature list: [effective PD is content focused, active, collaborative, modeled, coached, feedback-rich and sustained](../claims/effective-pd-seven-features.md) [+W] (a review, no effect sizes). The nearest to a comparison is one difference-in-differences study of Chicago middle-grade schools, in which [student reports of standards-aligned practice improved more in schools with more extensive standards-related professional learning](../claims/standards-aligned-practice-reports-improved-with-pl.md) [+W]: schools chose how much to take part, the outcome is students' reports of practice, and no effect size is printed. In one formative-assessment project, [videotaped lessons showed more discussion and student–student interaction after a year of PD](../claims/tefa-pd-year-one-observable-practice-changes.md) [+W] (no comparison group). Survey contrasts in one technology-coaching programme found coached teachers more often reported [PD sustained with coherent follow-up](../claims/sustained-coaching-contact-hours.md) [+W] and [time to try and evaluate new ideas](../claims/job-embedded-coaching-active-learning.md) [+W]. Across charter schools, [frequent feedback and coaching for teachers went with larger achievement impacts](../claims/teacher-feedback-coaching-associated-charter-success.md) [+W] (an association among school practices). Read together, these agree with the default's emphasis on duration, follow-up and enactment, but each is self-report, an association, or an uncontrolled before-and-after, so none settles how much or what kind.
- **Feedback, contingency and fading**, from learners rather than teachers. [Feedback at the task and process levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S] (two meta-analyzes; one coded information content, not levels; more than a third of feedback interventions lowered performance); [contingent scaffolding](../claims/contingent-scaffolding-improves-learning.md) [+M] (mostly one-to-one tutoring of children); [fading support](../claims/fading-support-promotes-transfer-of-responsibility.md) [+M] (worked-example studies, near transfer); [expertise reversal](../claims/expertise-reversal-effect.md) [~M] (trainees reading circuit diagrams, and a narrative review). Carry them to coaching as reasons for feedback on the enacted practice, support matched to the teacher's current enactment, and its withdrawal, no further.
- **Limits.** [Most coaching studies did not measure maintenance](../claims/coaching-maintenance-rarely-measured.md) [~M]; [teachers struggled to design Constructive and Interactive activities after ICAP PD](../claims/teachers-struggle-designing-interactive-activities.md) [-M], though their students still learned more in Constructive than Active activities; [Hunter's ITIP, widely implemented, lacked evidence of improved achievement](../claims/itip-lacks-empirical-evidence-achievement.md) [-M]; [peer diffusion from lead teachers was uneven](../claims/leader-teacher-diffusion-uneven.md) [~M].

Do not rank coaching against workshops, or one PD model against another, from these claims: the coaching meta-analysis (Kraft et al. 2018, recorded above) compares coaching with no coaching, not with a workshop on the same content, and its moderator results are associations across studies. Do not read teachers' satisfaction or stated intentions as changed practice. A precise abstention names the missing comparison (coached, practice-embedded PD against the same content as a workshop, on observed practice and a student measure at a stated delay) and the local observation that would stand in for it: the same look-for list at baseline, at the end, and six weeks after support ends.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested program.* A district adopts a new middle-school mathematics curriculum. One coach serves 18 teachers across three schools for a year, with about three hours a month per teacher; four teachers at one school join meetings remotely. The table changed the default: the target is one observable practice from the curriculum (running the lesson's closing discussion so that students compare two solutions), not "the new curriculum"; the launch is one day in which teachers do two lessons as students, watch a filmed closing discussion and rehearse it in trios; coaching runs in three-week cycles, with teachers filming a 10-minute closing discussion and the coach debriefing for 20 minutes online within two days; the coach spends extra cycles on the five teachers whose baseline lessons had no closing discussion; remote teachers watch the same clips and rehearse with an in-room partner. A teacher says she wants her quieter students to speak; the designer's objective is faithful use of the closing discussion. They agree to record both how many students speak and whether two solutions are compared, and to observe again in the eighth week after coaching ends. The coaching claims come from other practices, settings and populations; these are local choices to observe, not settled ones.

Record: **target practice and its look-for list → teacher's content familiarity, role and stated aim → baseline enactment → modeling and rehearsal given → each cycle's observed enactment, the feedback focus and who chose it → students' observed responses → support withdrawn and when → candidate explanations for any gap (does not know how, is blocked by a constraint, has adapted it, does not value it) and their basis → enactment at the end and at the later check**. Preserve the teacher's stated aim and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record but must not fabricate missing observations or assign numerical probabilities of practice change without a calibrated model.

## Description
Professional development applies the science of teacher learning to the design of experiences that change what educators know, do, and believe about instruction. Effective PD treats teachers as learners of a complex cognitive skill — much as [Cognitive Apprenticeship](cognitive-apprenticeship.md) treats students — combining modeling of expert practice, coached enactment in real classrooms, and iterative feedback. The problem it solves is well documented: one-shot workshops and one-off "sit and get" trainings rarely change classroom practice or student outcomes, while sustained, practice-embedded programs do Darling-Hammond et al.'s review of effective PD features.

## Implications

PD design is grounded in the same learning sciences as student instruction: teachers, like all learners, acquire complex skills through modeling, guided practice, feedback, and progressive independence. Reviews converge on a set of features that distinguish effective programs — sustained duration, active learning, collective participation, coherence with curriculum and policy, and a focus on concrete classroom practice Effective PD features review. Coaching with feedback is among the most powerful single components: instructional coaching produces larger effects on both teaching and student achievement than workshop-only formats Kraft, Blazar, & Hogan meta-analysis of coaching, consistent with evidence that [feedback is most effective at the task and process levels](../claims/feedback-most-effective-at-task-and-process-levels.md) [+S]. Because teaching expertise is largely tacit, PD must make expert reasoning visible rather than merely transmit principles.

### Context
#### Requirements
- Sustained duration — typically 20+ hours of contact time spread across a semester or year, not a single session Effective PD features review
- Opportunities for active practice: rehearsal, [coaching](../elements/coaching.md), video analysis, or peer observation
- Alignment with the curriculum, materials, and assessments teachers actually use
- Collective participation — teams from the same school or department learning together
- A feedback mechanism tied to enacted practice, not just stated intentions

#### Constraints
- One-shot workshops and lecture-format trainings show near-zero effects on practice or achievement Effective PD features review
- Coaching effects shrink as dosage decreases and when coaches lack domain expertise Coaching meta-analysis
- PD that ignores teachers' existing beliefs and prior knowledge produces compliance, not change; overly prescriptive programs can trigger reactance in experienced teachers [~M]
- Effects attenuate without organizational support — scheduling, materials, and leadership follow-through [~M]
- High-intensity coaching is expensive and difficult to scale; lower-digital-fidelity versions often lose effectiveness Coaching meta-analysis

#### Grain Size
Program — effective PD unfolds over months to years, embedded in teachers' ongoing work rather than delivered as isolated events.

### Target Goals
- Change in classroom instructional practice, not just teacher knowledge or satisfaction
- Improved student achievement as the downstream outcome Coaching meta-analysis
- Development of adaptive expertise — teachers who can reason through novel situations rather than enact scripts
- Collective capacity: shared norms, common language, and collaborative analysis of student work

### Target Learners
- Practicing K–12 teachers, especially those adopting new curricula, technologies, or pedagogies
- Novice teachers in induction and mentoring programs, who benefit most from [coaching](../elements/coaching.md) and observation
- Instructional coaches, department heads, and teacher-leaders who mediate PD for others
- Higher-education faculty transitioning to active or [flipped classroom](../principles/flipped-learning.md) formats

### Theory
#### Supporting

- [Situated Learning](../theories/situated-learning.md) (Lave & Wenger) — teacher learning is most effective when embedded in authentic classroom practice and professional [communities of practice](../theories/situated-learning.md), not decontextualized workshops
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](cognitive-apprenticeship.md) (Collins, Brown, & Newman) — expert teaching is tacit; PD works by modeling, coaching, and fading support as teachers gain independence
- [Social Learning Theory](../theories/social-learning-theory.md) (Bandura) — observation of modeled practice, especially via video, builds self-efficacy and provides vicarious rehearsal
- [Self-Regulated Learning](../theories/self-regulated-learning.md) (Zimmerman) — goal-setting, self-observation, and reflective cycles turn PD into sustained professional self-improvement

#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- [Expertise Reversal Effect](../theories/expertise-reversal-effect.md) — highly scaffolded, scripted PD can impede experienced teachers who already have well-developed routines; support must be calibrated to prior expertise [~M]

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Diagnose starting points** — assess teachers' current practice, beliefs, and student data to calibrate support ([check-in](../elements/check-in.md), [assessment](../elements/assessment.md))
2. **Model expert practice** — demonstrate target strategies via live demonstration, video exemplars, or [think-aloud](../elements/think-aloud.md) of instructional decision-making ([demonstration](../elements/demonstration.md))
3. **Analyze together** — deconstruct the modeled practice against student work and evidence, building shared language ([class discussion](../elements/class-discussion.md))
4. **Rehearse in low-stakes settings** — teachers practice routines with peers before enacting them with students ([coaching](../elements/coaching.md))
5. **Enact with support** — teachers implement in their own classrooms while a coach observes and provides [feedback](../elements/feedback.md) at the task and process levels
6. **Reflect and iterate** — cycles of enactment, [reflection](../elements/reflection.md), and revision, with support progressively [faded](../elements/fading.md) as independence grows
7. **Sustain through community** — ongoing collaborative inquiry, lesson study, and peer observation embed the practice in school culture

### Affordances
- [Cognitive Apprenticeship](cognitive-apprenticeship.md) — PD enacts this principle for adult learners: modeling makes expert teaching visible, coaching supports enactment, and fading builds independent professional judgment
- [Active Learning](../principles/active-learning.md) — effective PD replaces passive transmission with rehearsal, video analysis, and collaborative problem-solving, mirroring what works for students
- [Assessment for Learning](../principles/assessment-for-learning.md) — student work and classroom evidence serve as formative data that drive PD content, rather than generic curricula delivered regardless of need

### Personalization

**Novice teachers:** Provide high-frequency coaching, co-planning, and full modeling before independent enactment. Delay fading; novices need extended guided practice.

**Experienced teachers adopting a new practice:** Compress modeling; focus on the points of friction between existing routines and the new practice. Avoid scripted materials that trigger the expertise reversal effect — offer principles plus exemplars instead.

**Teachers with low confidence or resistance:** Use video of similar-status peers (not expert demonstrators) and build early wins with small, well-defined practices before tackling ambitious instruction.

**Heterogeneous departments:** Differentiate coaching intensity — some teachers need in-class support while others benefit from peer observation and collaborative analysis. Use team structures so stronger practitioners extend reach.

**Remote or resource-constrained settings:** Virtual coaching with video capture (e.g., [Edthena](https://edthena.com)) preserves the enactment–feedback cycle at lower cost, though dosage and coach quality remain critical [~M].
