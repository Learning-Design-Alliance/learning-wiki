    Blended Learning¶

    Pattern · All patterns Evidence · 10 claims (5 for, 5 mixed) · 9 studies (4 quant-synthesis, 3 associational, 1 causal, 1 qualitative), q2–q4 · 4 of 9 report an effect size · 9 claims rest on one study

    Description and scope¶

    A reusable policy for dividing a course between online and in-person work so that each mode does some work the other does not, and for watching how learners respond to the division. The earlier page's working definition is kept as a convention: a course delivering roughly 30–80% of its content online, with some face-to-face interaction, is blended; one delivering 80% or more online is fully online. That threshold is a definition from the earlier page, not a tested boundary.

    It instantiates no single principle. The in-person part usually applies active learning (class time spent generating, explaining or applying with feedback); the online part depends on self-regulated learning, because learners pace, attend and persist on their own; and where the online part runs as discussion, community of inquiry describes the presences it aims at. Flipped learning is a narrower configuration inside this one: exposition moved online so that class time is free for application.

    The wiki has no claim that tests a blend as such against the same content, time and materials delivered in one mode. The one synthesis recorded here found blended courses ahead of face-to-face ones, and its authors say the blended arms usually also had extra learning time and resources, so the advantage cannot be credited to the blend. The study configurations below are evidence; the response-dependent policy is an untested design proposal. Use it to decide what each mode is for and to collect better observations, not to promise a gain from the label.

    Inputs: establish the design brief¶

    Field Record or elicit
    Learner and starting observation Prior knowledge of the content (novice, intermediate, advanced, mixed), age and population_band; a short task-specific response on the course's first topic; prior experience of studying online without a teacher; access to a device and connection, and where and when they can study. Record unknowns as unknown.
    Context and activities Setting of each part (classroom or workplace-clinical for in-person, online-self-paced or online-instructor-led for online), duration (days-weeks, term-plus), how often the group meets in person and for how long, the platform, who teaches each part, what support exists between sessions, and the share of content online.
    Learner-valued goal Ask why the learner is taking the course and what they want from the in-person time and from the flexibility: fewer trips, fitting study around work, contact with a teacher, a credential. Record divergence from the designer's objective as an observation.
    Designer objective The capability and its knowledge type (verbal-association, concept, procedure, principle, complex-skill, attitude-motivation); the representative task; which parts need equipment, a live partner, a teacher's observation or a safe practice space (candidates for in-person) and which need repetition, self-paced exposure or flexible timing (candidates for online).
    Outcome Course assessment or examination (general-achievement), performance on the representative task (skill-performance), delayed-retention if targeted, completion and withdrawal (persistence), time spent (learning-time), and participation in each mode, each with its instrument and horizon.
    Comparison being made What the blend replaces: a face-to-face course, a fully online one, or nothing. Record what else changes with it (time, materials, assessment, teacher), because that is what the evidence cannot separate from the blend.

    A request to "make the course blended" or "move some of it online" does not specify these. Ask for them before prescribing a split, a weekly rhythm or an expected gain. Check first that the online and in-person parts are linked: what learners do online should be needed in the next in-person session, and the session should draw on it.

    Sequence and conditional policy¶

    1. Assign each activity to a mode by what it needs. Put exposition, worked material, repeated low-stakes practice and anything learners need to revisit at their own pace online; put practice that needs observation, feedback on performance, equipment, a live partner or discussion in person. Write down for each activity why it sits where it does. A part that does the same work in both modes is redundant (the earlier page's constraint). Basis: untested; from the earlier page.
    2. Set a regular rhythm that links the modes. For each in-person session, name the online work that prepares for it and the online work that follows from it, with a deadline before the session. Tell learners the rhythm. The earlier page gives no quantity; a weekly cycle (online preparation, in-person session, online follow-up) is an untested proposal.
    3. Use the in-person time actively. Spend most of it on learners generating, explaining or applying with feedback rather than on repeating the online exposition. Evidence for this comes from active learning against lecture, not from blends (see below).
    4. Support the online part's self-regulation. Add planning prompts, a visible schedule, self-monitoring checks with answers and the deadlines in step 2. Evidence: SRL interventions in online and blended settings (see below).
    5. Watch participation and withdrawal in the online part from week 1. Record who completes the preparation before each session, and contact non-starters early. Online-only courses in one programme lost more learners than comparable onground ones.
    6. Shift the teacher's role and record it. The earlier page names the requirement that the instructor move from delivering material to facilitating and coordinating the two modes; record what the teacher actually does in each session. Basis: untested; from the earlier page, with one teacher's qualitative account below.
    7. Reobserve at the course horizon. Measure the representative task and completion, and compare with the course's earlier version only after recording what besides the mode changed (time, materials, assessment).
    Observation Discriminating probe Proposed action, with its uncertainty
    Many learners arrive at the in-person session without having done the online preparation Check completion data by learner; ask about time, access and whether the session used the preparation last time. If access or time is the barrier, shorten the preparation or give in-session time for it; if the session did not need it, make the next session depend on it visibly. Low completion alone does not separate lack of time, lack of access and lack of purpose.
    Learners do the online part but in-person performance is weak Give a short in-session check on the online content before applying it; compare learners who did and did not complete it. If the check fails too, the online part is not teaching enough (add worked material, embedded checks, or move that segment in person); if the check passes, the gap is in application, so give more guided practice in person.
    Some learners fall behind steadily in the online part Look at when they log in and what they skip; ask how they plan their study. Add planning and monitoring support, intermediate deadlines or a scheduled check-in; offer a shorter route. Falling behind does not establish low self-regulation; competing work, access and a missing prerequisite each fit.
    Learners say they want more teacher contact, or rate the online part as less valuable Ask what they want the teacher for (explanation, feedback, reassurance, company); compare with what the in-person time is currently used for. Move the activities they name into the in-person time or add a live online slot; do not read stated preference as evidence of what they learn more from, since the recorded preference data measure priorities, not learning.
    The blended version scores no better than the face-to-face version it replaced Record whether time, materials, practice or feedback actually changed; compare sections that changed more of them. Read a null result as possibly a blend that added nothing before reading it as a failure of the pattern; equally, a gain where more was added is not evidence for the blend itself.

    Fitting the design to a situation¶

    The three facts that most change the decision:

    • How well the learners study without a teacher. The online part shifts pacing, attendance and persistence onto learners, and what it needs in support depends on that. If unstated, ask: have they completed a self-paced or online course before, and what happened to their study when no one was checking?
    • Which parts of the goal need someone present. Practice that needs a teacher's observation, a partner, equipment or a safe space decides what must stay in person; the rest can move. If unstated, ask: in the final task, what does the learner do that a teacher must watch or respond to live?
    • What the blend replaces and what else changes with it. The only recorded gain for blends comes with extra time and resources, so the design should say what it adds. If unstated, ask: compared with the current course, will learners get more practice, more feedback or more time, or only a different delivery?
    If the brief says… Then change… Basis
    Learners are novice in the content Keep each online exposition segment short, with an embedded check and worked material, and spend the in-person time on guided practice with feedback before independent work. untested proposal
    Learners are child (pre-k-12) Run the online part in school time under supervision (rotation between stations or groups) rather than at home, and keep online segments short; give the teacher a view of who completed what. untested proposal; the recorded synthesis is mostly higher education and professional training
    Learners are adult working professionals (adult-workplace) Put content and repeated practice online where they can fit it around work; use in-person time for practice with equipment, cases or colleagues and for feedback on performance. Blended learning improves outcomes [~M], pooled mostly across higher education and professional training, with extra time and resources confounded
    The goal is a complex-skill or procedure that needs practice with feedback Reserve most in-person time for learners carrying out the task with feedback, not for exposition; move the exposition online. Active learning improves exam performance [+M], tested against lecture in undergraduate STEM courses, not within a blend
    The goal is mostly facts or vocabulary (verbal-association) Put spaced, self-checked practice online with immediate answers; keep in-person time for using the material and for checks of what was missed. untested proposal
    The online part is online-self-paced and learners have little experience of it Add planning prompts, a visible schedule with intermediate deadlines and self-monitoring checks; review them at the next in-person session.     No teacher present between sessions, or a large group Monitor completion each week, contact non-starters within the first week or two, and keep a named person learners can ask.     Only one session, or a few days Use a single online preparation task before the session and a short online follow-up; the course-level split does not apply. untested proposal
    High-stakes assessment Hold the summative assessment in person or proctored; keep the online checks low-stakes and formative so learners use them to monitor. untested proposal

    Choosing configurations from evidence¶

    • What to expect from blending itself. Blended learning improves outcomes [~M]: a meta-analysis of studies published 1996–2008, mostly in higher education and professional training, found 23 independent contrasts of a blended condition against face-to-face only, with a mean g+ = +0.35, larger than the g+ = +0.14 for purely online against face-to-face. The authors caution that the blended conditions typically also had more learning time and instructional resources than the controls, so the advantage cannot be attributed to the blended format. The outcome is "learning outcomes"; no horizon is reported on the claim page. Not settled: the abstract available could not confirm the entry. Design the blend to add practice, feedback or time on purpose and record what it adds; do not expect a gain from moving delivery online alone.
    • Support for the online part. Self-regulated learning interventions in online and blended environments [+M]: a meta-analysis of 15 studies (5,519 participants) found a pooled effect of 0.65 (random effects) of SRL interventions on learning outcomes in online and blended environments. The comparator and the populations are not reported on the claim page; not yet checked against its source. No moderator differences were detected [~M] across course type, SRL strategy, school level or learning context in the same 15 studies; with that few studies this is not evidence the effect is the same everywhere, and equivalence was not tested. It supports step 4 as a candidate; it does not say which support to choose.
    • Persistence when learners study without a teacher. Online continuing-education courses showed lower persistence [~W]: over two years of enrolment records at one university extension programme (1,847 online of 27,815 enrolments), attrition was 21% online against 15% in similar onground courses, a significant difference. This is an observational comparison of fully online courses, not blends, and it does not say why. It is a reason to monitor completion in the online part (step 5), not a forecast for a blend.
    • What the in-person time is for. Active learning improves exam performance [+M]: across 158 studies in undergraduate STEM courses, exam and concept-inventory scores were 0.47 SD higher under active learning than under traditional lecturing; a second meta-analysis found that only high-intensity active learning narrowed gaps for underrepresented and low-income students. Neither tests a blend; carry it to the in-person part of a blend as an untested transfer. One quasi-experiment with two intact first-year calculus classes found a peer-teaching flipped classroom [+W] scored higher on conceptual understanding than a conventional flipped classroom after controlling for the pretest, which suggests the in-person activity matters within a flipped design; one study, not yet checked against its source.
    • What learners say each mode is for. Students ranked teacher interaction first for face-to-face and content interaction first for online learning [~W] in a survey of 236 students at four universities; no inferential statistic is printed, and it measures stated priorities, not learning. It is a reason to ask learners what they want from each mode (the fourth row of the observation table), not a rule for assigning activities.

    Do not combine these into one strength or rank them: their comparators (face-to-face, lecture, no intervention, onground courses) and outcomes (pooled learning outcomes, exams, persistence, stated priorities) differ. A precise abstention names the missing comparison, outcome or observation and how to obtain it.

    Further evidence, not yet read against this model¶

    Claims this page cited before it was rewritten as a conditional model. They are evidence about the relationship, but none has yet been re-read against the model above; each says how far its own sources have been checked.

    • Blended teaching shifted the teacher's self-identity from facilitator to co-facilitator and co-designer with students [+W] — not yet checked against its sources
    • In a survey of 236 blended-learning students, learners readily ranked teacher, student and content interaction, which the authors read as supporting both theses of the Interaction Equivalency Theorem [+W] — checked by the judge: all 1 entries pass (full text)
    • In Pennsylvania districts using hybrid instruction with precautions in fall 2020, increases in student and staff infections were no greater than community increases outside school [~M] — attached 2026-10-08 from Brian Gill (2021), which proposed "Pair hybrid instruction with precautions such as mask-wearing and physical distancing to mitigate in-school virus transmission".

    Illustrative design instance and observation record¶

    Illustration, invented for this page; not a tested course. A term-long secondary-school physics unit (adolescent, pre-k-12, term-plus) in a rural school where most students have only a shared family phone and patchy connection, unlike the higher-education and professional settings most of the recorded evidence comes from. The table changed three things. Because students are adolescents with little experience of self-paced study and limited devices, the online part runs in school, in a weekly station rotation (one group on the school tablets with short downloaded explanations and self-checked questions, one with the teacher on practical work, one on paper problems), rather than as homework. Because the goal includes carrying out experiments safely (a procedure with feedback), the practical work stays in person with the teacher. Because no one supervises study at home, the teacher checks the tablet completion log weekly and talks to any student who missed two stations. In week 3 one student completes every tablet station but cannot set up the circuit in the practical; an in-session check on the tablet content shows the content was understood, so the gap is treated as one of application and the student gets a second guided set-up before the independent one, with the result recorded.

    Record: activity and the mode it was assigned to, with the reason → online work set, its deadline and completion → in-person activity and what it drew on → response on the representative task → candidate interpretations and the probe chosen → change made (to the split, the support or the rhythm) → completion and performance at the next session and at the unit's end, alongside the learner's stated goal and what they said they wanted from each mode. An agent may summarise this record but must not invent missing inputs or credit an outcome to the blend without recording what else changed.

    Elements and limits¶

    Pre-class video lecture, self-paced learning, in-process comprehension checks, quizzes, self-monitoring, check-in, discussion, practice, feedback and lecture.

    This pattern is scoped to dividing a course between online and in-person work. It does not establish that a blend outperforms a single-mode course with the same time and materials, an optimal online share, a weekly rhythm, or a forecast for an individual learner. Fully online courses, MOOCs and the narrower flipped configuration have their own pages and evidence. The requirement that instructors shift towards facilitation, and the constraint that a poorly integrated blend feels redundant or disconnected, come from the earlier page and remain untested here.
