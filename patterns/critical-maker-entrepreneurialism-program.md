# Critical Maker-Entrepreneurialism Program

## Description
This pattern operationalizes [Critical Maker-Entrepreneurialism](../theories/critical-maker-entrepreneurialism.md) as a four-phase, multi-month program sequence: (1) community ethnography and problem identification, where youth use surveys, interviews, and observation to surface a problem rooted in their own community's historicized injustices, not an externally-assigned prompt; (2) prototype development with iterative community input, where youth design and test solutions against ongoing feedback from the people the solution serves; (3) discursive reframing, where youth are supported in co-opting required entrepreneurial forms (e.g., a business plan) to argue for nonprofit, community-benefit framing rather than market framing; and (4) public presentation at a high-visibility, often market-oriented platform (e.g., a citywide entrepreneurial fair), where youth use the platform for visibility while collectively resisting and critiquing its competitive, profit-centered norms. Adult mentors play a specific, bounded role throughout: technical and organizational scaffolding, transparency about the tensions of engaging mainstream platforms, and active advocacy with organizers — while deferring to youth on problem identification and success criteria.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A sustained (ideally multi-year) community-based making program with existing trust and relationships between youth and mentors, since identity work of this kind was observed to develop over years, not weeks
- Access to a public entrepreneurial platform (a fair, a pitch event) that creates a genuine, consequential tension between justice-oriented and market-oriented framing for youth to navigate
#### Constraints
- Evidence is a single-program, 12-youth critical ethnography (no comparison group, no quantified outcome measures); findings describe what happened in this program, not a validated causal design
- Requires mentors willing to advocate with external organizers on youths' behalf and to openly discuss the risks of engaging market-oriented platforms, which may require organizational standing mentors do not always have

Program (spans a full program year or more; the source study's intensive phase was one year within a 10+ year program)

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Community ethnography and problem identification**: Youth conduct surveys, interviews, and observations (e.g., mapping bus routes and library access) to surface a community problem rooted in their own historicized experience, with mentors providing technical support on method but not on problem selection.
2. **Prototype development with iterative community input**: Youth design and build a solution, soliciting feedback from the community members the solution serves at each stage, and treat the design as a tool for new social relations rather than a market product.
3. **Discursive reframing**: When required to produce mainstream entrepreneurial artifacts (e.g., a business plan), youth are supported in using the form's own categories (problem, solution, financial plan) to argue for nonprofit, community-benefit framing.
4. **Public presentation and strategic platform use**: Youth present at a public, often market-oriented platform, using it for visibility and social capital while collectively maintaining solidarity (e.g., sitting together, offering each other public praise) and responding to misrecognition (e.g., judges dismissing high-tech nonprofit work as lacking market "polish") as a group rather than individually.

### Personalization
- Where no public entrepreneurial platform is available or appropriate, phases 1-2 (ethnography and iterative prototyping) can likely stand alone as a community-making program, though phase 4's identity-negotiation and resistance dynamics — a central part of the source study's findings — would not occur.

## Examples
- A community center's "Green Club" afterschool STEM program (10+ years running) in which five youth teams designed a heated boot for people experiencing homelessness, a solar-powered "Little Free STEM Library," a heated bus shelter, an emergency phone-alert button, and a "for us, by us" STEM YouTube channel, then presented at a citywide entrepreneurial fair.
