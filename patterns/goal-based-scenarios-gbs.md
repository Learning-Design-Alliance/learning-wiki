# Goal-Based Scenarios (GBS)

## Description
Goal-Based Scenarios organize learning around a mission or role-based objective that learners pursue inside a realistic scenario. Instead of learning content first and applying it later, learners confront a goal, make decisions, access information as needed, and experience the consequences of their choices. The scenario gives purpose and coherence to the learning sequence.

GBS is especially useful when the instructional target involves judgment, procedure, and context-sensitive decision making. It combines authenticity, problem solving, and feedback in a form that can be more motivating than decontextualized instruction.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- **A meaningful mission**: Learners need a clear goal that makes the scenario worth engaging.
- **A realistic scenario with consequences**: The context should preserve enough of the real task structure to support transfer.
- **Just-in-time supports**: Learners should be able to access information, hints, or resources when needed.
- **Feedback and debriefing**: The scenario needs consequences, reflection, or coaching to turn action into learning.
#### Constraints
- **High design burden**: Strong scenarios require careful writing, sequencing, and support design.
- **Weak authenticity undermines engagement**: A thin or artificial mission makes the pattern feel contrived.
- **Novice overload**: Learners may need more scaffolding if the scenario assumes too much prior knowledge.
- **Not ideal for purely factual coverage**: The pattern is strongest when learners must do something with knowledge.
#### Grain Size
- Unit
- Course

### Target Goals
- **Contextualized decision making**: Using knowledge in realistic situations.
- **Procedural and strategic application**: Coordinating choices, information gathering, and action.
- **Transfer to authentic performance**: Learning in conditions closer to eventual use.

### Target Learners
- **Professional and adult learners**: Strong fit for workforce, corporate, clinical, and technical training.
- **Learners motivated by purposeful roles or missions**: Useful when context helps sustain engagement.
- **Learners preparing for scenario-based performance**: Best where judgment under conditions matters.

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Situated learning perspectives — knowledge is more usable when learned in context.
- Experiential learning perspectives — action, consequence, and reflection support deeper learning.
- Goal-oriented motivation perspectives — clear missions can organize attention and persistence.
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- Scenarios need instructional discipline; immersion alone does not guarantee learning.
- Some learners need more direct explanation or guided practice before complex scenarios become productive.

## Design

### Sequence
1. Present a mission, role, or goal inside a realistic scenario.
2. Have learners identify what they need to know or do to succeed.
3. Let learners act, gather information, and make decisions within the scenario.
4. Provide feedback, consequences, or coaching tied to those decisions.
5. Debrief the reasoning and connect it to target concepts or future situations.

### Personalization
- Different learners can pursue different paths within the same scenario.
- Supports can be adjusted from heavily guided to more open mission execution.
- Roles, stakes, and domain framing can be adapted for different audiences.

## Examples
- Clinical training scenarios where learners manage a patient case through evolving decisions.
- Business simulations built around a crisis or client objective.
- Public-sector or military training built around mission execution with constrained information.

## Impact
- Supports engagement and transfer when learning needs to feel purposeful and situated.
- Strongest when scenarios are paired with explicit debrief rather than left as pure experience.
