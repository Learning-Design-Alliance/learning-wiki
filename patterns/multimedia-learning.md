# Multimedia Learning

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 9 claims (5 for, 4 mixed) · 12 studies (7 causal, 3 quant-synthesis, 2 review), `q2`–`q4` · 4 of 12 report an effect size · 5 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable policy for deciding whether an explanation needs a picture, what the picture should show, where its words go (on it, beside it, spoken or written), and how the learner controls the pace. It instantiates the [conditional principle](../principles/multimedia-learning.md). The study configurations below are evidence; the response-dependent policy is an **untested design proposal**. Use it to gather better evidence about a learner's understanding, not to certify a learning style or a latent state.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | What the learner can already say or do about this structure or process (an explanation, a labelled sketch, a prediction), with prior knowledge of this content, reading and listening access, and any uncertainty. Preserve unknowns. |
| Context and activities | Medium and device, whether learners can pause, replay or re-read (system-paced or learner-paced), captioning or accessibility requirements, available time, and what the learner does after viewing. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask what the learner wants to be able to do with this and why. Record disagreement with the designer objective; negotiate rather than assume agreement. |
| Designer objective | The capability intended (recall, explanation of a mechanism, troubleshooting, a procedure) and the knowledge type (`concept`, `procedure`, `principle`, `complex-skill`); whether the picture will be available when the capability is used. |
| Outcome | Instrument, locally chosen criterion, immediate or delayed horizon (`immediate-performance`, `delayed-retention`, `near-transfer`, `far-transfer`), and any separate use outcome. |

A request to "make it more engaging" or "add visuals" does not specify these inputs. Ask for them before choosing a format, a segment length or a predicted gain. Check first that the picture is correct and depicts what the words describe; a misleading diagram studied carefully is not progress.

## Sequence and conditional policy

1. **Elicit briefly.** Ask the learner to explain or sketch how the thing works, or to predict what happens when one part changes, before the lesson. Record the response and any help given; this probe is itself an exposure.
2. **Decide whether a picture has a job.** If the explanation concerns a structure, process or spatial relation the learner cannot yet describe, consider adding a picture that shows that relation. If the content is a list of verbal facts, or the learner already explains it well, a picture may add little; the evidence below does not require one for every learner.
3. **Configure the candidate.** Place words on or next to the part of the picture they describe; do not present the same words as narration and as full on-screen text over a visualization; leave out decorative or interesting-but-irrelevant images; where the presentation runs continuously and the learner is new to it, consider pause points or learner control. Record exactly what was delivered.
4. **Observe and choose the next activity provisionally.** Use the table below, varying one feature at a time where feasible; placement, pacing, channel and prior knowledge can confound each other.
5. **Reobserve.** Use a new representative task under the agreed aids, criterion and horizon. Retain unresolved explanations. If the response disagrees with the expectation, revise the interpretation or configuration rather than adding more media.

| Observation | Discriminating probe | Proposed action, with its uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Can repeat the words but not explain how parts relate | Ask for a "what if this part changed" prediction with the picture present, then withdrawn. | If the prediction fails with the picture present, revise the picture to show the relation, or prompt the learner to explain the link between words and picture. If it fails only when withdrawn, the objective decides whether independence from the picture is required. Neither branch is a validated diagnosis. |
| Reports the screen as hard to follow; looks back and forth | Re-present one segment with labels placed on the picture and no duplicated on-screen text; compare effort and the explanation produced. | If integration helps, apply it across the lesson. If not, check pacing and language access before changing anything else. One comparison does not establish the cause. |
| Misses spoken content in a narrated animation | Offer pause control or the same words as re-readable text with the same picture; check comprehension of the words alone. | Keep narration where learners can follow it; where they cannot (language, hearing, noisy setting, dense terminology), keep written text or short key terms, which the evidence does not show to be harmful without a competing visualization. Captions required for access stay. |
| Likes the illustrated version but explains no better | Remove decorative images, keep the informative picture, compare explanation quality. | Drop images that do not inform. Liking, mood and interest are not learning outcomes, although they may matter to the learner. |
| Explains and predicts well from the start | Give a changed-condition question without supports. | Reduce segmentation and extra support provisionally and move to application; the reversal evidence is from one support in one study, so check again rather than assume. |

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Add an informative picture to words.** In two randomized lab experiments with German university students (n=48, n=47), [text with a picture beat text alone](../claims/multimedia-principle-improves-learning.md) [+M] on a learning-outcomes test (η²=.14, .09); horizon not reported. A second study on the same page found no reliable gain from adding animation once words were present, with adults and 12–17-year-olds; that entry is not settled against its abstract, so treat the direction as unsettled.
- **Place mutually referring words and pictures together.** A meta-analysis of 58 comparisons (n=2426) found [integrated placement beat separated placement](../claims/split-attention-effect-degrades-learning.md) [+M], g=0.63; learners and horizon are not broken down on the page, and it is not yet checked against its source.
- **Speak the words that accompany a fast-moving visual.** A meta-analysis of 43 effects found [narration rather than on-screen text alongside graphics](../claims/modality-effect-narration-over-text.md) [+M] improved learning, with the benefit moderated by element interactivity, system- versus learner-pacing and field of study, strongest when system-paced; an experiment with 146 college students on an animation of lightning formation found narration easier to integrate. No pooled effect size is recorded; not settled against its sources. It does not predict a benefit for learner-paced static material, or for learners who cannot follow the spoken language.
- **Do not duplicate words over a visualization; do not delete text by rule.** A review of 63 studies reports that [identical written text added to a narrated visualization](../claims/redundancy-principle.md) [~M] most often impaired retention and transfer, with reversals for older learners, learner-paced delivery and abridged text, and *positive* effects of written text added to narration with no visualization. A meta-analysis of 57 studies on [verbal redundancy](../claims/redundancy-hurts-learning.md) [~S] found spoken–written did not differ from written-only and beat spoken-only, for low prior knowledge, system-paced and picture-free material, and key-term text beat verbatim text. Choose the configuration by whether a visualization competes with the text.
- **Segment continuous presentations for learners new to the content, and check whether it is still needed.** In one randomized experiment with 75 Dutch secondary students, [segmentation of narrated animated worked examples](../claims/segmentation-benefits-shrink-with-expertise.md) [~M] was more efficient for lower prior knowledge, with no advantage at higher prior knowledge, on an efficiency composite rather than on transfer scores alone. Not yet checked against its source.

Do not convert η² or a regression β into a probability for a learner, rank these unlike comparisons by effect label, or predict delayed use from an immediate test. A precise abstention names the missing comparison, target or observation and the next way to resolve it.

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested lesson.* Adult apprentices in a workplace programme study a short narrated animation of a pump cycle. One apprentice says they want to work out why a pump on their own site keeps failing; the designer's objective is a stage-naming quiz. Elicit the difference and agree whether explaining a fault matters, then choose a representative task (predict what a stuck valve does to the cycle) and decide whether the diagram is available at test. Before the lesson, the apprentice names parts but cannot say what a valve does to the flow. The candidate configuration places labels on the diagram, speaks the explanation, adds pause points, and leaves out a stock photo of a plant room. If the apprentice still cannot predict the fault with the diagram present, revise the diagram to show flow direction before adding anything else; assess delayed application separately if it is a target.

## Elements and limits

[Multimedia learning](../elements/multimedia-learning.md), [multimedia instruction](../elements/multimedia-instruction.md), [digital learning](../elements/digital-learning.md), [personalized pacing](../elements/personalized-pacing.md), [self-explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md), [pedagogical agent signaling](../elements/pedagogical-agent-signaling.md) and [accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md).

This pattern is scoped to explanations of structures, processes and relations presented through words and pictures. Learner-made representations, interactive simulations, video modelling of skills and language-learning glosses need their own configurations and evidence. Most of the recorded evidence comes from short lab lessons with university students; the policy supports observation and design reasoning, and its predictive accuracy and the learner-significance mechanism remain to be tested with actual learners.

# Prefer narration over on-screen text when designing multimedia science instruction for middle school students

> **Principle** · [All principles](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 2 claims (2 for) · 3 studies (2 causal, 1 quant-synthesis), `q3`–`q4` · 1 of 3 report an effect size · 1 claim rests on one study

## Description
The article's practical recommendation is that multimedia designers and teachers working with middle school students should use spoken rather than written text alongside visual materials. As the authors put it, "They should prefer using narration rather than on-screen text with visual materials in a multimedia instruction for more effective learning," because this makes it "possible to avoid extraneous cognitive load for learners." The recommendation rests on the study's modality findings in real middle school settings.

## Design Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- Spoken (narrated) text must be available as an alternative to on-screen written text in the multimedia material.
#### Constraints
- The recommendation is grounded in the modality comparisons in this study; the authors note conflicting results in some prior studies and that redundancy and signaling principles need further research with children.

### Target Learners
- Middle school students (aged 11-12) studying science in real school settings

### Target Learning Objectives
- Conceptual and procedural knowledge of an electricity unit
