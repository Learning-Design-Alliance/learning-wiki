# Multimedia Learning

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)
> **Evidence** · 75 claims (35 for, 38 mixed, 2 against) · 57 studies (31 causal, 10 quant-synthesis, 9 review, 2 associational, 2 design, 2 theoretical, 1 qualitative), `q1`–`q4` · 17 of 57 report an effect size · 66 claims rest on one study

## Description and scope

A reusable policy for deciding whether an explanation needs a picture, what the picture should show, where its words go (on it, beside it, spoken or written), and how the learner controls the pace. It instantiates the [conditional principle](../principles/multimedia-learning.md). The study configurations below are evidence; the response-dependent policy is an **untested design proposal**. Use it to gather better evidence about a learner's understanding, not to certify a learning style or a latent state.

## Inputs: establish the design brief

| Field | Record or elicit |
|---|---|
| Learner and starting observation | What the learner can already say or do about this structure or process (an explanation, a labelled sketch, a prediction), with prior knowledge of this content, reading and listening access, and any uncertainty. Preserve unknowns. |
| Context and activities | Medium and device, whether learners can pause, replay or re-read (system-paced or learner-paced), captioning or accessibility requirements, available time, and what the learner does after viewing. |
| Learner-valued goal | Ask what the learner wants to be able to do with this and why. Record disagreement with the designer objective; negotiate rather than assume agreement. |
| Designer objective | The capability intended (recall, explanation of a mechanism, troubleshooting, a procedure) and the knowledge type (`concept`, `procedure`, `principle`, `complex-skill`); whether the picture will be available when the capability is used. |
| Outcome | Instrument, locally chosen criterion, immediate or delayed horizon (`immediate-performance`, `delayed-retention`, `near-transfer`, `far-transfer`), and any separate use outcome. |

A request to "make it more engaging" or "add visuals" does not specify these inputs. Ask for them before choosing a format, a segment length or a predicted gain. Check first that the picture is correct and depicts what the words describe; a misleading diagram studied carefully is not progress.

## Sequence and conditional policy

1. **Elicit briefly.** Ask the learner to explain or sketch how the thing works, or to predict what happens when one part changes, before the lesson. Record the response and any help given; this probe is itself an exposure.
2. **Decide whether a picture has a job.** If the explanation concerns a structure, process or spatial relation the learner cannot yet describe, consider adding a picture that shows that relation. If the content is a list of verbal facts, or the learner already explains it well, a picture may add little; the evidence below does not require one for every learner.
3. **Configure the candidate.** Where the material has many named parts and the learner is new to it, name the parts briefly first (pretraining). Choose a single static picture to show structure and an animation to show change over time. Write labels and captions into the picture at the part they describe; do not present the same words as narration and as full on-screen text over a visualization; leave out decorative or interesting-but-irrelevant images; where the presentation runs continuously and the learner is new to it, consider pause points or learner control. Record exactly what was delivered.
4. **Observe and choose the next activity provisionally.** Use the table below, varying one feature at a time where feasible; placement, pacing, channel and prior knowledge can confound each other.
5. **Reobserve.** Use a new representative task under the agreed aids, criterion and horizon. Retain unresolved explanations. If the response disagrees with the expectation, revise the interpretation or configuration rather than adding more media.
6. **Across sessions, withdraw support as the learner explains unaided** (*proposal, untested as a schedule*). Early sessions: pretraining, short segments of a few minutes with a pause or "next" between them, labels in the picture, narration for system-paced material, and explicit guidance on when to pause and replay. Once the learner answers a new "what if" question correctly without supports on two occasions, lengthen the segments, drop the pretraining and offer the picture with narration on request, then move to application tasks. Basis for the direction: [segmentation helps less as prior knowledge grows](../claims/segmentation-benefits-shrink-with-expertise.md) [~M] and [integration helps less for more experienced learners](../claims/expertise-reversal-split-attention-redundancy-review.md) [~M]; the segment length and the switching criterion are proposals, and no claim in the wiki says how many sessions the withdrawal takes.

| Observation | Discriminating probe | Proposed action, with its uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Can repeat the words but not explain how parts relate | Ask for a "what if this part changed" prediction with the picture present, then withdrawn. | If the prediction fails with the picture present, revise the picture to show the relation, or prompt the learner to explain the link between words and picture. If it fails only when withdrawn, the objective decides whether independence from the picture is required. Neither branch is a validated diagnosis. |
| Reports the screen as hard to follow; looks back and forth | Re-present one segment with labels placed on the picture and no duplicated on-screen text; compare effort and the explanation produced. | If integration helps, apply it across the lesson. If not, check pacing and language access before changing anything else. One comparison does not establish the cause. |
| Misses spoken content in a narrated animation | Offer pause control or the same words as re-readable text with the same picture; check comprehension of the words alone. | Keep narration where learners can follow it; where they cannot (language, hearing, noisy setting, dense terminology), keep written text or short key terms, which the evidence does not show to be harmful without a competing visualization. Captions required for access stay. For children with cochlear implants, [seeing the speaker as well as hearing them raised sentence recognition for most](../claims/av-sentence-recognition-exceeds-auditory-alone-ci-children.md) [+W] (23 of 27), so keep the speaker's face visible where speech carries the content. |
| Agrees with whatever the picture seems to show | Mix in statements that are false of the picture, and ask the learner to say what in the picture supports each answer. | If false statements are accepted, the picture is lending credibility rather than information: [graphs made students confirm statements regardless of truth](../claims/picture-bias-graphs-confirm-statements.md) [~W]. Prompt the learner to check claims against the picture before adding more pictures; one eye-tracking study, so treat as a hypothesis. |
| Likes the illustrated version but explains no better | Remove decorative images, keep the informative picture, compare explanation quality. | Drop images that do not inform. Liking, mood and interest are not learning outcomes, although they may matter to the learner. |
| Explains and predicts well from the start | Give a changed-condition question without supports. | Reduce segmentation and extra support provisionally and move to application; the reversal evidence is from one support in one study, so check again rather than assume. |

## Choosing configurations from evidence

- **Add an informative picture to words.** In two randomized lab experiments with German university students (n=48, n=47), [text with a picture beat text alone](../claims/multimedia-principle-improves-learning.md) [+M] on a learning-outcomes test (η²=.14, .09); horizon not reported. A second study on the same page found no reliable gain from adding animation once words were present, with adults and 12–17-year-olds; that entry is not settled against its abstract, so treat the direction as unsettled. A conference experiment with 37 students found [recall and retention differed across media combinations](../claims/media-combinations-affect-recall-and-retention.md) [+W], better where both verbal and pictorial coding were supported; no effect size is printed and the entry is not checked against its source. A practice article reports, second-hand, that [cited studies found better post-tests with words and relevant pictures than words alone](../claims/multimedia-principle-words-and-pictures-outcomes.md) [+W], especially with spoken words; it prints no effect size. Mayer's 2008 summary of his lab's experiments (brakes, pumps, generators, lightning, arithmetic) reports [words with corresponding graphics beat words alone on transfer](../claims/generative-processing-principles-mayer-2008.md) [+M] in 11 of 11 comparisons, median d = 1.39, with university students on short lessons; older secondary reports agree without new data ([congruent pictures with text aid reading comprehension](../claims/congruent-pictures-with-text-facilitate-comprehension.md) [+W], citing Levie and Lentz 1982; [complementary auditory and visual channels in video](../claims/modality-matching-complementary-channels-video.md) [+W]). Two qualifications. In Jaeger and Fiorella's online experiments with 163 and 48 undergraduates reading biology texts, [adding an instructional image changed neither test scores (d = 0.01) nor how well learners judged their own understanding](../claims/instructional-visuals-no-effect-test-performance-or-test-explain-accuracy.md) [~M]: an image does not help by being there, which is why step 2 asks what job it does. And in an eye-tracking study with 36 students, [a graph drew attention away from the problem statement](../claims/graph-attention-away-from-statement.md) [~W] and [made students more likely to confirm statements whether or not they were true](../claims/picture-bias-graphs-confirm-statements.md) [~W]: a picture can lend a statement credibility it has not earned (see the observation table).
- **Name the parts before a complex lesson.** In a randomized experiment with 93 adults, [a short video naming the parts of a micropipette before an immersive VR lesson](../claims/pretraining-improves-transfer.md) [+M] raised knowledge-test scores and cut errors when the task was then done for real, with lower reported load and no difference inside VR; no standardized effect size is printed. A practice article on the same page reports the same direction second-hand. Use pretraining where the material has many named parts and learners are new to them; one study, in VR, so judge it in step 5.
- **Place mutually referring words and pictures together.** A meta-analysis of 58 comparisons (n=2426) found [integrated placement beat separated placement](../claims/split-attention-effect-degrades-learning.md) [+M], g=0.63; learners and horizon are not broken down on the page, and it is not yet checked against its source. An earlier meta-analysis of 50 effects from randomized between-group studies (2375 students) found that [integrating spatially or temporally separated related elements](../claims/integration-of-related-elements-large-mean-effect.md) [+M] gave d = 0.85, with significant heterogeneity, and that the gain was [larger for high element-interactivity material](../claims/element-interactivity-moderates-contiguity-effect.md) [+M] (d = 0.78) than for low (d = 0.28); [static and dynamic presentations did not differ reliably](../claims/static-dynamic-presentation-no-moderation.md) [~M]. What "integrated" means matters: across Mayer et al.'s (1995) three lightning-text experiments with college students, [the benefit came from captions and labels written into the illustration, not from moving the illustration next to its paragraph](../claims/annotations-not-proximity-drive-illustration-benefit.md) [+M], and the integrated group's roughly 50% more transfer solutions were [larger for students with little meteorology experience](../claims/illustration-integration-effect-stronger-for-low-experience-learners.md) [~M]; no effect sizes are printed. For narration about a picture, an eye-tracking experiment found learners [looked sooner at the part being described when localising, describing and interpreting were interleaved for each part](../claims/ldi-integrated-faster-first-fixation.md) [+W] (ηp² = .44) rather than given as separate blocks; that is an attention measure, not a learning outcome. For timing, a practice article cites one study in which [simultaneous notes, worked example and explanation aided understanding, and another in which temporally contiguous instruction lowered perceived load](../claims/temporal-contiguity-lower-cognitive-load.md) [+W]; second-hand and with no effect sizes, so present spoken words at the same time as the part they describe as a default, not a tested rule.
- **Signal the parts the words refer to.** The same practice article reports that the signaling studies it cites found [higher outcomes with signaled multimedia (illustrations with labelled text)](../claims/signaling-principle-increased-learning-outcomes.md) [+W] than without. It is a second-hand report with no effect sizes; use labels and highlighting on the picture as part of step 3, and judge them in step 5.
- **Speak the words that accompany a fast-moving visual.** A meta-analysis of 43 effects found [narration rather than on-screen text alongside graphics](../claims/modality-effect-narration-over-text.md) [+M] improved learning, with the benefit moderated by element interactivity, system- versus learner-pacing and field of study, strongest when system-paced; an experiment with 146 college students on an animation of lightning formation found narration easier to integrate. No pooled effect size is recorded; not settled against its sources. In a middle-school quasi-experiment on an electricity unit, [spoken text beat written text with both abstract and concrete animation](../claims/modality-effect-holds-middle-school-electricity.md) [+M] (d = .35 in each comparison), the one school-age, classroom test recorded here; pacing is not stated on that page. In the same study, [spoken text did not differ reliably from *signaled* written text](../claims/spoken-versus-signaled-written-null.md) [~W] (highlighted key words), so where narration is not possible, signaling the written text is a candidate for closing some of the gap; a nonsignificant difference does not show the two are equal. It does not predict a benefit for learner-paced static material, or for learners who cannot follow the spoken language, and in immersive VR a recorded experiment found the reverse (see the VR row below).
- **Do not duplicate words over a visualization; do not delete text by rule.** A review of 63 studies reports that [identical written text added to a narrated visualization](../claims/redundancy-principle.md) [~M] most often impaired retention and transfer, with reversals for older learners, learner-paced delivery and abridged text, and *positive* effects of written text added to narration with no visualization. A meta-analysis of 57 studies on [verbal redundancy](../claims/redundancy-hurts-learning.md) [~S] found spoken–written did not differ from written-only and beat spoken-only, for low prior knowledge, system-paced and picture-free material, and key-term text beat verbatim text; there the spoken–written advantage was [larger for unsegmented presentations](../claims/segmentation-moderates-verbal-redundancy.md) [~M] (g = 0.53, k = 7) than for segmented ones (g = 0.15, k = 22). In the middle-school electricity quasi-experiment, [adding written text to narration made no significant difference with either abstract or concrete animation](../claims/redundancy-effect-not-significant-middle-school.md) [~W]; equivalence was not tested. Choose the configuration by whether a visualization competes with the text.
- **Segment continuous presentations for learners new to the content, and check whether it is still needed.** In one randomized experiment with 75 Dutch secondary students, [segmentation of narrated animated worked examples](../claims/segmentation-benefits-shrink-with-expertise.md) [~M] was more efficient for lower prior knowledge, with no advantage at higher prior knowledge, on an efficiency composite rather than on transfer scores alone. Not yet checked against its source.
- **Choose animation or a static picture by what the learner must identify.** In a randomized experiment with 88 pre-service teachers learning a six-bar linkage, [an animation beat one or four static pictures at identifying how the parts move](../claims/animation-beats-static-pictures-for-identifying-motions.md) [+M] (d = 2.33, 2.41), while [a single static picture beat both the animation and four pictures at identifying how the parts are arranged](../claims/single-picture-beats-animation-for-spatial-arrangements.md) [~M] (d = 1.30, 1.11). A meta-analysis of animated against static graphics found the animation advantage largest [when the animation was system-paced](../claims/animation-advantage-larger-system-paced.md) [~M] (g = 0.31) and [when it came with spoken commentary](../claims/animation-advantage-larger-auditory-commentary.md) [~M] (g = 0.34). Rebetez et al. found [animation helped retention overall](../claims/animation-benefits-retention-over-static-graphics.md) [+W] but helped transfer only for learners working in pairs (see the situation table); no effect sizes are printed there. A single picture, then, is the default for structure and an animation for change over time.
- **Do not count on learners to use pause and replay; teach them when to.** Learners given flow control over an animation [rarely used it](../claims/flow-control-unused-no-learning-correlation.md) [~W], and in the same study [the animation group navigated far less than the static-picture groups](../claims/animations-pacify-learners-navigation-imbalance.md) [~W] (8.5 against about 34 actions); fifth graders studying videos clicked back about 1.5 times. In a randomized experiment with 90 undergraduates watching an instructional video, [guidance on when to pause and scroll back beat unguided learner control](../claims/strategy-guidance-beats-unguided-learner-control-immediate.md) [+M] on immediate comprehension and [beat continuous presentation without controls](../claims/strategy-guidance-beats-continuous-presentation-immediate.md) [+M] on immediate recall and comprehension; no effect sizes are printed and no delayed test is recorded here.

Do not convert η² or a regression β into a probability for a learner, rank these unlike comparisons by effect label, or predict delayed use from an immediate test. A precise abstention names the missing comparison, target or observation and the next way to resolve it.

## Fitting the design to a situation

Ask for four facts when the brief omits them: **who controls the pace** (system-paced video or lecture, or learner-paced pages), **how complex the material is** (how many parts must be held in mind together), **the learners' prior knowledge and reading fluency in the language of instruction**, and **whether the outcome is retention or transfer**. Each row changes step 3 (configure the candidate) unless it says otherwise.

| If the brief says… | Then change… | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| A system-paced video, lecture or narrated animation | Apply the configuration rules strictly (words beside the picture, narration rather than printed text, no extras); the design principles matter more when learners cannot pause | [Design principle effects are moderated by pacing and complexity](../claims/design-principle-effects-moderated-by-pacing-and-complexity.md) [+M]; [Pacing moderates the modality effect](../claims/modality-effect-moderated-by-pacing.md) [+M] |
| Learner-paced pages, slides or a website | Keep the words printed next to the picture; do not convert them to narration, which showed no benefit (slightly negative) when learners set the pace | [Pacing moderates the modality effect](../claims/modality-effect-moderated-by-pacing.md) [~M] |
| Complex material: a process with many interacting parts, and learners new to it | Integrate each label or sentence into the picture at the point it describes, present spoken words with the matching part of an animation, and name the parts briefly before the lesson | [Integration benefit is larger for high element-interactivity material](../claims/element-interactivity-moderates-contiguity-effect.md) [+M]; [Integrating related elements gives a large average benefit for novices](../claims/integration-of-related-elements-large-mean-effect.md) [+M]; [Pretraining improves transfer](../claims/pretraining-improves-transfer.md) [+M] (one VR experiment) |
| Learners already know the content well | Cut explanatory text that repeats what the diagram shows, and reduce segmentation; check on a transfer item rather than assuming | [Integration benefits may reverse for more experienced learners](../claims/expertise-reversal-split-attention-redundancy-review.md) [~M]; [Segmentation benefits shrink with expertise](../claims/segmentation-benefits-shrink-with-expertise.md) [~M]; [The integrated-illustration benefit was stronger for low-experience students](../claims/illustration-integration-effect-stronger-for-low-experience-learners.md) [~M] |
| Second-language readers or less fluent readers, and the material has no picture or animation | Give the words both spoken and written | [Spoken–written advantages are larger for L2 and nonfluent readers](../claims/reader-fluency-prior-knowledge-moderate-redundancy.md) [+M]; [Verbal redundancy does not help when images or animation are included](../claims/images-animation-eliminate-redundancy-benefit.md) [~M] |
| A narrated animation carries the full narration as on-screen text | Remove the duplicated text, or keep only key terms beside the picture; keep captions where access requires them | [Redundancy principle](../claims/redundancy-principle-mayer-2008.md) [+M]; [Verbal redundancy does not help when images or animation are included](../claims/images-animation-eliminate-redundancy-benefit.md) [~M] |
| Video in a second language, for language learners | Add captions in the target language | [Captioning second-language videos produces large benefits](../claims/captioning-second-language-videos-large-benefits.md) [+M]; [captioned video improved L2 listening, generalising to new sentences and speakers](../claims/bimodal-captioned-input-improves-segmentation.md) [+W] (reported second-hand) |
| The brief asks for engaging extras: stock photos, background music, anecdotes | Leave out what the explanation does not need, and replace decorative images with ones that show the relation being explained | [Coherence principle](../claims/coherence-principle-irrelevant-material-hurts-learning.md) [+M]; [Interesting but irrelevant details can impair learning](../claims/seductive-details-effect.md) [~M] |
| The choice is between a static diagram and an animation | Choose by what the learner must identify: an animation for how parts move, a single static picture for how they are arranged (not a strip of snapshots, which helped neither); if an animation is used, prefer it system-paced with spoken commentary. The integration rules apply equally to both | [Animation beats static pictures for identifying motions](../claims/animation-beats-static-pictures-for-identifying-motions.md) [+M]; [a single picture beats animation for spatial arrangements](../claims/single-picture-beats-animation-for-spatial-arrangements.md) [~M]; [animation advantage larger when system-paced](../claims/animation-advantage-larger-system-paced.md) [~M] and [with auditory commentary](../claims/animation-advantage-larger-auditory-commentary.md) [~M]; [Contiguity effects do not differ between static and dynamic presentation](../claims/static-dynamic-presentation-no-moderation.md) [~M] |
| A long continuous presentation, and learners are new to the content | Break it into meaningful segments with a pause or "next" between them; allow for more study time | [Segmenting improves retention and transfer](../claims/segmenting-improves-multimedia-learning.md) [+M]; [Segmentation increases learning time](../claims/segmenting-increases-learning-time.md) [~M] |
| The plan relies on learners using pause and replay controls | Build the pauses into the presentation, or tell learners when to pause and replay (after each step, when a term is new), rather than counting on them to use the controls unprompted | [Learner flow control over animations was rarely used](../claims/flow-control-unused-no-learning-correlation.md) [~W]; [strategy guidance beat unguided learner control](../claims/strategy-guidance-beats-unguided-learner-control-immediate.md) [+M] (immediate tests only) |
| Learners will study the animation in pairs or small groups | Animation is more worth its cost here; avoid adding snapshot strips of the key steps for pairs | Rebetez et al., one experiment, no effect sizes printed: [transfer benefited from animation only for learners studying collaboratively](../claims/collaboration-enables-transfer-benefit-of-animation.md) [~W]; [snapshots were marginally helpful alone and harmful in dyads](../claims/snapshots-detrimental-in-dyads-beneficial-individually.md) [~W] |
| A digital storybook for young children | Keep animated illustrations, sound and music that match the story; leave out hotspots, games and other interactive features | [Multimedia-only stories helped comprehension (g+ = 0.39); stories that also had interactive features did not](../claims/multimedia-only-benefits-interactive-features-detrimental.md) [+M] (meta-analysis). The gain was larger in studies of children the authors classed as disadvantaged (g+ = 0.66): a difference reflecting circumstances, not a property of the children or a cause |
| Choosing between a video and an illustrated text | For children and slower readers, consider a narrated video, and check comprehension rather than assuming it; for university students integrating several sources, choose on access and time, since the medium made no difference to understanding | Fifth graders [studied videos faster than equivalent illustrated texts](../claims/videos-studied-faster-than-illustrated-texts-fifth-graders.md) [~W], [most of all the slowest readers](../claims/videos-especially-beneficial-slower-readers.md) [+W] (studying rate, not learning); with 255 university students, [medium had no effect on integrating complementary sources](../claims/no-medium-context-effect-integration.md) [~M] and [videos took longer to process](../claims/videos-longer-processing-time-than-texts.md) [~W] (d = 0.52) |
| Narration, animation or slides generated by an AI tool | Check before use that the picture keeps the concept's defining constraint, that the synthetic voice reads numbers and pauses correctly, and that each slide element appears when the narration reaches it | Educators' focus groups: [generated animations can drop a defining constraint](../claims/anvil-pedagogical-mismatch-risk.md) [~W]; expert reviewers of generated lecture videos [flagged voice, reveal timing and layout](../claims/bespoke-free-text-voice-reveal-layout-issues.md) [~W]. Neither measures learning |
| An immersive VR headset lesson | Do not assume narration wins: pilot spoken and short anchored printed text on the brief's own test. Name the equipment's parts before the VR session | Conflicting, as on the [modality principle](../principles/modality.md): [the modality effect was larger with head-mounted displays](../claims/modality-effect-stronger-in-virtual-reality.md) [~M], but in a VR experiment with 61 learners [printed text beat narration on recall](../claims/vr-reverse-modality-effect-recall.md) [-M]; [pretraining before VR improved real-task transfer](../claims/pretraining-improves-transfer.md) [+M] |
| The outcome is transfer, not recall | Expect smaller gains from adding written text to narration, and judge the configuration on a transfer item in step 5 | [Spoken–written advantages are larger for retention than transfer](../claims/outcome-constructs-test-formats-moderate-redundancy.md) [~M] |

## The evidence by question

- *Should words sit next to, and arrive with, the picture they describe?* [Split Attention Effect Degrades Learning](../claims/split-attention-effect-degrades-learning.md) [+M]
- *Should the words be spoken or printed?* [Presenting words as spoken narration rather than on-screen text alongside graphics improves learning](../claims/modality-effect-narration-over-text.md) [+M]
- *Does presenting the same words twice (spoken and written) hurt or help, and for whom?* [On-screen text that duplicates the narration of a diagram can hurt learning, but spoken–written redundancy is not harmful on average and helps over spoken-only presentation in some conditions](../claims/redundancy-hurts-learning.md) [~S]
- *Animation or a static picture?* [Animation beats static pictures for identifying motions](../claims/animation-beats-static-pictures-for-identifying-motions.md) [+M] against [a single picture beats animation for spatial arrangements](../claims/single-picture-beats-animation-for-spatial-arrangements.md) [~M]
- *Does adding a picture help at all?* [Words with graphics beat words alone on transfer](../claims/generative-processing-principles-mayer-2008.md) [+M], qualified by [instructional visuals did not affect test performance](../claims/instructional-visuals-no-effect-test-performance-or-test-explain-accuracy.md) [~M]
- *Will learners use pause and replay?* [Strategy guidance beats unguided learner control](../claims/strategy-guidance-beats-unguided-learner-control-immediate.md) [+M]

## Illustrative design instance and observation record

*Invented illustration, not a tested lesson.* Adult apprentices in a workplace programme study a short narrated animation of a pump cycle. One apprentice says they want to work out why a pump on their own site keeps failing; the designer's objective is a stage-naming quiz. Elicit the difference and agree whether explaining a fault matters, then choose a representative task (predict what a stuck valve does to the cycle) and decide whether the diagram is available at test. Before the lesson, the apprentice names parts but cannot say what a valve does to the flow. The candidate configuration places labels on the diagram, speaks the explanation, adds pause points, and leaves out a stock photo of a plant room. If the apprentice still cannot predict the fault with the diagram present, revise the diagram to show flow direction before adding anything else; assess delayed application separately if it is a target.

Record: **material and its configuration (picture, placement, channel, pacing, segments) → permitted and actual help → response or explanation → candidate interpretations and confidence basis → discriminating probe → chosen activity and justification → next observation at the stated horizon**. Preserve the learner's stated purpose and any change in it. An agent may summarize this record, but must not fabricate missing inputs or assign numerical state probabilities without a calibrated model.

## Elements and limits

[Multimedia learning](../elements/multimedia-learning.md), [multimedia instruction](../elements/multimedia-instruction.md), [digital learning](../elements/digital-learning.md), [personalized pacing](../elements/personalized-pacing.md), [self-explanation](../elements/self-explanation.md), [pedagogical agent signaling](../elements/pedagogical-agent-signaling.md) and [accommodations](../elements/accommodations.md).

This pattern is scoped to explanations of structures, processes and relations presented through words and pictures. Learner-made representations, interactive simulations (a field study in undergraduate electrodynamics found [sketching and simulation tasks alongside the formula](../claims/multiple-representations-improve-learning.md) [~M] beat calculation-only tasks, d = 0.40, while raising perceived cognitive load, and a review there finds a second visual representation helps only with the learner's skill at connecting representations), video modelling of skills and language-learning glosses need their own configurations and evidence. Most of the recorded evidence comes from short lab lessons with university students; the policy supports observation and design reasoning, and its predictive accuracy and the learner-significance mechanism remain to be tested with actual learners.
