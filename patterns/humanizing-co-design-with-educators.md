# Humanizing Co-Design with Educators

> **Pattern** · [All patterns](index.md)

## Description
Co-design (researchers and educators collaboratively designing curriculum) is not automatically humanizing — it requires deliberate facilitation to treat participants' emotional and relational experience as legitimate design material, not a distraction from the "real" design work. This pattern adds concrete, replicable structures on top of a standard co-design process: shared contemplative training so no one enters as "the expert," ritualized opening/closing affective check-ins at every meeting, a reflective field-note protocol that surfaces educators' lived classroom struggles as design input, and explicit responsiveness that lets facilitators set the design agenda aside when participants' personal or collective wellbeing needs it.

## Implications

### Context
#### Requirements
- A shared foundational experience before design work begins that positions all participants (researchers included) as co-learners rather than experts and novices (the source study used an 8-week Compassion Cultivation Training)
- Recurring, ritualized opening and closing practices at every meeting (a brief guided practice, then a personal check-in; a closing practice, then a one-word feeling check)
- A structured reflection protocol educators use to write low-inference field notes about their own classroom interactions, paired with co-constructed prompts (e.g., "What feelings, thoughts, emotions arose for you?" / "How did you — or could you — care for yourself while caring for others in this interaction?")
- Facilitators who visibly affirm vulnerability when it surfaces (treating a shared struggle as a legitimate contribution, not a distraction to redirect past)
#### Constraints
- This is not humanizing by default — the source study is explicit that co-design must be *intentionally* facilitated this way; without deliberate structure, affective experience tends to stay invisible or gets treated as off-task
- Requires facilitators comfortable relinquishing some agenda control (e.g., canceling a planned session for an unstructured check-in during an acute disruption)
- Documented in a single district with an existing long-term research partnership, which may have made trust easier to establish than in a cold-start relationship
#### Grain Size
- Unit (a multi-month co-design series; the source study ran 14 meetings, 58 total hours, over roughly eight months)

### Target Goals
- Building a co-design community that supports educators applying what they learn across multiple scales — personal practice, classroom teaching, and broader systems-level vision
- [Attending to affect in co-design supports community and cross-scale transfer](../claims/attending-to-affect-in-co-design-supports-community-and-cross-scale-transfer.md)

### Target Learners
- Educators (teachers, counselors, principals, librarians) participating as co-designers alongside researchers, especially in emotionally demanding domains (e.g., compassion/wellbeing curricula, trauma-informed practice)

### Theory
#### Supporting
- Expansive learning (Engeström & Sannino) — extended here to emphasize that transforming *subject-subject relations* (not just the object being designed) is itself part of the collective learning
- Affect theory (Ahmed; Tomkins) — treats naming and sharing emotion as inseparable from, not prior to, cognitive and design work
#### Contradicting / Qualifying
- None identified in the source study; the authors note their own facilitator positionality (researchers as co-participants) likely shaped what became visible or expressible

## Design

### Sequence
1. **Shared foundation**: All participants (educators and researchers) complete an extended shared contemplative/relational training together before co-design work begins, so no one enters the room positioned as expert over novice.
2. **Opening ritual** (every meeting): brief guided practice → small-group debrief of the practice experience → open personal check-in ("anything else you wish to share about your week").
3. **Design work**: small-group brainstorming and drafting, mixing educators' classroom expertise with researchers' pedagogical frameworks.
4. **Field-note cycle**: educators write low-inference observations of their own school interactions using co-constructed reflection prompts; groups share notes and jointly reinterpret them with compassion, identifying intrapersonal, interpersonal, and systems-level sources of difficulty — these become source material for course design.
5. **Closing ritual** (every meeting): brief practice → one-word or short check-out on how people are feeling.
6. **Responsive adaptation**: when an acute disruption hits (the source study's cohort was mid-program when COVID-19 began), cancel the planned agenda and hold a loosely structured community check-in instead.

### Personalization
- The specific shared foundational training can be swapped for a different relational/contemplative practice suited to the design topic; what matters structurally is a genuinely shared, non-hierarchical starting experience
- Field-note prompts should be co-constructed with participants rather than imposed, to keep the reflection genuinely responsive to their concerns

## Examples
- A Rocky Mountain–region school district partnership: 10 educators from 6 schools, 8-week shared compassion training followed by 14 co-design meetings (58 hours) producing a 4-course digital sequence on compassion and dignity for educators; educators reported 43 distinct named affective experiences (21 negative, 22 positive) across the process, and documented applying course concepts in their own teaching, parenting, and colleague relationships.
